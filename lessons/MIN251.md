# MIN251 — Effective Leadership

## Course objectives

- 1. Identify biblical examples of leadership and explain lessons that can be learned from these leaders.
- 2. Explain the characteristics of a leader’s heart and how they are cultivated.
- 3. Explain the requirements of the godly leader for guiding the church of Jesus Christ.
- 4. Identify the various tests of leadership and what they seek to develop in a leader’s life.
- 5. Explain the mission of the church as provided in God’s Word.
- 6. Explain the process of vision from its inception to its communication to the body.
- 7. Describe the recommendations of Moses’ father-in-law and how they apply to modern leadership.

## 1 - Leading Yourself Before Leading Others

### Jesus as the Model for Leadership

#### 1.1.1 — Describe how Jesus’ example of humility, service, and reliance on the Father redefines true leadership.

“The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.” (Mark 10:45)

Jesus redefined leadership. In a world that equated power with dominance, He demonstrated that true greatness comes through humility and service. Christ-centered leadership begins with following His example—leading not for recognition or control, but from love, obedience, and a heart to serve others.

Jesus stands as the ultimate model of leadership—not because He held earthly authority, but because He embodied divine authority expressed through compassion, truth, and sacrificial love. Every aspect of His leadership flowed from His relationship with the Father. Before choosing the twelve disciples, He spent the night in prayer (Luke 6:12–13). Before beginning His public ministry, He fasted and sought God’s will in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1–11). Even in moments of triumph and trial, His decisions were guided by communion with His Father.

This shows that spiritual leadership is first and foremost relational. It is not rooted in ambition or strategy, but in intimacy with God. Jesus’ leadership flowed out of who He was—a Son walking in perfect unity with His Father. Likewise, a Christian leader’s authority and influence do not come from title or position but from abiding in Christ. When our leadership springs from that connection, it bears fruit that lasts.

Jesus modeled servant leadership. In John 13, He knelt to wash His disciples’ feet—an act reserved for the lowest servant. In doing so, He shattered every worldly definition of leadership. He did not demand honor; He gave it. He did not seek followers to elevate himself; He lifted others up. He taught that leadership is not about climbing higher but about stooping lower. “Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant” (Mark 10:43).

The life of Jesus reveals that leadership is less about command and more about compassion. He led by listening, healing, feeding, and forgiving. He was both lion and lamb—bold in truth, yet gentle in spirit. His leadership carried authority, but it was authority rooted in love. Christian leaders are called to reflect this same heart: to be courageous without being controlling, influential without being intimidating, and visionary without losing humility.

Biblical Examples

Moses led with God’s presence as his guide. He declared, “If your Presence does not go with us, do not send us up from here” (Exodus 33:15). Like Jesus, he recognized that leadership without divine presence is powerless.

Paul followed Christ’s example of servanthood. Though he planted churches and led movements, he saw himself as a servant of Christ and others (1 Corinthians 9:19).

Peter learned servant leadership from Jesus directly. Once impulsive and self-promoting, he later exhorted church leaders to “be shepherds of God’s flock; . . . not lording it over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock” (1 Peter 5:2–3).

Modern-Day Application

Christlike leadership is radically countercultural. In today’s world, leaders are often measured by their ability to assert influence, build platforms, or achieve visible results. But Jesus modeled something deeper: influence through integrity, not image. A leader who follows Christ’s example will prioritize people over performance, faithfulness over fame, and calling over comfort. Leading like Jesus means praying before acting, listening before speaking, serving before leading, and loving before judging.

Practical Application

Begin with the Secret Place: Prioritize Intimacy over Activity

Jesus often withdrew from crowds to pray (Mark 1:35; Luke 5:16). His power in public came from His time in private with the Father. For the Christ-centered leader, spiritual strength, wisdom, and direction are cultivated in the secret place—through prayer, reflection, and worship.

Practical ways to apply this include the following:

Schedule daily time alone with God. Guard it as intentionally as any leadership meeting.

Listen as much as you speak in prayer. Let the Holy Spirit guide your thoughts and bring clarity to your decisions.

Lead from overflow, not emptiness. Ministry without intimacy eventually leads to burnout. When your relationship with God is strong, your leadership naturally carries peace, wisdom, and grace.

A leader who neglects the secret place may still produce results, but they will lack the spiritual authority that comes from abiding in God’s presence. Jesus led with divine insight because He first learned to abide before He acted.

Lead by Serving: Influence Through Humility

Jesus showed us that leadership is not about being served but about serving others (Mark 10:45). When He washed the disciples’ feet, He modeled a kind of greatness the world cannot understand—humility that elevates others.

To lead like Jesus means embracing the servant’s towel before the leader’s title. True greatness is measured not by how many people serve you but by how you serve.

Practical ways to apply this include the following:

Look for opportunities to meet practical needs—not just spiritual ones. This could be helping a team member, mentoring someone quietly, or encouraging the discouraged.

Adopt a posture of humility. No task should be beneath you if it helps someone grow or experience God’s love.

Serve unseen. The purest form of leadership happens when no one is watching and no recognition is expected.

When leaders serve with humility, they gain lasting influence because people follow authenticity, not authority. Servant leadership builds trust, and trust sustains teams through every challenge.

Reflect Christ’s Heart: Lead with Love and Integrity

Everything Jesus did was motivated by love (John 13:1). His leadership was relational, not transactional. He saw people not as tools to accomplish a mission but as treasures worth dying for. Christlike leaders must lead with the same heart—balancing truth and grace in every interaction. When people encounter leaders who reflect the compassion and consistency of Christ, they experience the love of God through that leadership.

Practical ways to apply this include the following:

Ask before acting: “Does this decision reflect the heart of Jesus?”

Be slow to speak and quick to listen. This demonstrates love and builds trust.

Extend grace freely. People remember how you treat them far more than what you accomplish.

Model integrity. Keep your word, confess mistakes, and act consistently whether seen or unseen.

Measure Success Differently: Faithfulness Over Fame

The world measures success by numbers, achievements, and visibility. Jesus measured it by obedience to the Father’s will. At the end of His earthly ministry, He could say, “I have brought you glory on earth by completing the work you gave me to do” (John 17:4). That’s true success.

Practical ways to apply this include the following:

Focus on obedience. Ask, “Am I doing what God asked of me?” rather than, “Is everyone pleased with me?”

Value transformation over attendance. Celebrate spiritual growth, not just metrics.

Stay faithful in the unseen seasons. Some of Jesus’ most significant work happened in obscurity—praying, preparing, and discipling a few.

When leaders redefine success by faithfulness, they lead from peace instead of pressure. They stop striving for applause and start living for God’s approval.

Summary

The practical example of Jesus’ leadership teaches us that spiritual authority is born in humility, sustained by intimacy, and expressed through service. Leading like Jesus means walking slowly enough to hear God’s voice, kneeling low enough to serve others, and staying humble enough to keep learning.

Christlike leaders live with a different posture:

From striving to serving

From performing to abiding

From leading by control to leading by compassion

**Q:** Why is Jesus considered the ultimate model for leadership?

**A:** His leadership was rooted in intimacy with the Father and expressed through humble service, love, and sacrifice.

**Q:** What distinguishes Christ-centered leadership from worldly leadership?

**A:** Worldly leadership seeks power and recognition; Christ-centered leadership seeks to serve and glorify God.

### Identity Formed in Christ

#### 1.2.1 — Explain how our identity in Christ provides a secure foundation for confident and consistent leadership.

“If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; the old has gone, the new has come!” (2 Corinthians 5:17)

A leader’s confidence, direction, and endurance come from knowing who they are in Christ. True identity is not based on performance, position, or the opinions of others—it is rooted in belonging to Jesus. When leaders are secure in their identity in Him, they can lead with humility, courage, and consistency regardless of circumstances.

Every leader wrestles with questions of identity: Who am I? What defines me? Where does my worth come from? The world answers these questions with titles, achievements, and appearances. But Christian leadership calls for a deeper foundation—one built not on what we do, but on who we are in Christ .

Our identity is not earned; it is received. The moment we surrender to Jesus, we are made new creations. This transformation gives us a secure and unshakable foundation for leadership. We no longer lead to prove ourselves but to glorify God. We are not defined by success or failure, by praise or criticism, but by our relationship with Christ.

Jesus himself modeled this truth. Before performing a single miracle or preaching a sermon, the Father declared over Him: “This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased” (Matthew 3:17). That affirmation came before any public accomplishment. Jesus began His ministry from acceptance, not striving—and so must we.

When leaders forget this, insecurity and comparison creep in. Insecurity leads to striving for validation; comparison breeds jealousy and burnout. But when our identity is anchored in Christ, we no longer compete for approval—we lead from rest. Our worth is not determined by how many people follow us but by the One we follow.

Christ-formed identity transforms the way we lead. It frees us to celebrate others’ success, admit weakness, and serve without fear of losing status. It creates a leadership culture grounded in authenticity and grace, not pressure and performance. The secure leader lifts others up rather than feeling threatened by their gifts. The insecure leader needs to be seen; the Christ-centered leader is content to reflect Jesus instead.

Biblical Examples

David found his confidence in God, not in appearances or approval. When others doubted him, he declared, “The Lord who delivered me from the paw of the lion . . . will deliver me from the hand of this Philistine” (1 Samuel 17:37). His confidence came from covenant identity, not human recognition.

Gideon struggled with self-doubt, calling himself “the least in my family” (Judges 6:15). But when God called him “mighty warrior,” Gideon learned that identity is defined by God’s declaration, not by personal weakness.

Paul understood his identity in Christ so deeply that he could say, “I no longer live, but Christ lives in me” (Galatians 2:20). This gave him the strength to endure hardship, rejection, and persecution without losing his sense of calling.

Modern-Day Application

Leaders today face constant pressure to perform and project confidence. Social media amplifies comparison, ministry results can become measurements of worth, and failure often feels final. But Christ-centered leaders resist defining themselves by outcomes. Instead, they return again and again to the truth: I am loved, chosen, forgiven, and called by God.

Leading from identity in Christ means:

resting in God’s approval rather than chasing people’s praise;

leading from grace, not guilt or performance pressure;

owning weaknesses and depending on God’s strength; and

encouraging others’ callings because secure leaders are not threatened by gifted people.

When leaders know who they are, they can handle both criticism and success with maturity. They do not crumble under pressure or boast in achievements—they stay grounded, steady, and surrendered.

Practical Application

Guard Against Comparison: Celebrate, Do Not Compete

Comparison is one of the enemy’s most effective tools for eroding identity. It leads to envy, insecurity, and distraction from your God-given assignment. Christ-centered leaders learn to celebrate others without feeling diminished.

Refrain from asking, “Why not me?” Instead, ask, “What is God doing through them that I can rejoice in?” The kingdom of God is not a competition—it is a collaboration. When one part of the body succeeds, the whole body benefits (1 Corinthians 12:26).

Practical steps can include

limiting time on platforms that trigger comparison;

regularly expressing gratitude for the unique gifts God has given you; or

verbally affirming and celebrating others’ achievements—it builds unity and strengthens your heart against jealousy.

Serve with Security: Lead from Identity, Not for Identity

When leaders know who they are, they can serve without striving for approval. They no longer lead to earn love or respect; they lead because they are loved and respected by God.

Serving with security means being content to serve in hidden places, knowing God sees what people do not (Matthew 6:4). It means being faithful in small things without needing recognition. Secure leaders are not threatened by others’ gifts; they invite them. They do not manipulate people for validation—they minister to people for transformation. True peace in leadership comes when you can answer, “I’m serving because it honors Him.”

Ask yourself:

Would I still serve this way if no one noticed?

Am I leading to prove something or to please God?

Embrace Growth: Remain Teachable

A secure leader is a growing leader. Because their identity is grounded in Christ, they can receive correction, admit mistakes, and keep learning without shame. Insecurity resists feedback; identity in Christ welcomes it as an opportunity to mature.

God develops leaders through both success and struggle. Every challenge can refine character and deepen dependence on Him. When you see growth as part of your calling, failure becomes feedback, not finality.

Practical habits can include

inviting trusted mentors or peers to speak truth into your life;

reflecting regularly on lessons God is teaching you; or

replacing self-criticism with gratitude for God’s ongoing work in you (Philippians 1:6).

Find Accountability: Surround Yourself with Truth-Tellers

Even the strongest leaders need others to remind them who they are in Christ. Isolation weakens identity; community strengthens it. God designed us to walk with others who encourage, challenge, and remind us of truth when we forget it.

Accountability partners or spiritual mentors can help you stay grounded in your true identity, especially during seasons of pressure or discouragement. They help you see blind spots, correct unhealthy motivations, and celebrate spiritual growth.

Practical ways to build accountability includes

joining or forming a small group with other leaders who value authenticity over image;

meeting regularly for prayer and honest conversation; and

giving permission to trusted friends to speak truth—even when it is uncomfortable.

Living from identity in Christ is not a one-time revelation—it is a lifelong formation. Every day, leaders must return to the foundation of who they are: loved, chosen, redeemed, and empowered by God. When that truth settles deep in the heart, leadership becomes an overflow of grace, not a pursuit of validation.

Leaders grounded in Christ’s identity will lead with peace instead of pressure, encourage others instead of competing, serve faithfully, whether in the spotlight or obscurity, and remain steadfast through both praise and criticism. Their lives declare a powerful message: “My identity is not in what I do for God—it is in who I am in Christ.”

**Q:** How did God affirm Jesus’ identity before His public ministry, and what does that teach us?

**A:** God called Jesus His beloved Son before any accomplishments, teaching us that identity precedes achievement.

**Q:** Why must a Christian leader’s identity be rooted in Christ?

**A:** Identity in Christ provides stability and confidence that are not dependent on performance or others’ opinions.

### Leading from Overflow, Not Emptiness

#### 1.3.1 — Identify practical ways to maintain spiritual intimacy with God in order to lead effectively from a place of abundance rather than exhaustion.

“Remain in me, and I will remain in you. No branch can bear fruit by itself; it must remain in the vine.” (John 15:4)

Leaders cannot give what they do not have. Fruitful leadership flows from abiding in Christ, not from striving in self-effort. A leader’s greatest asset is not their skill, strategy, or stamina—it is their spiritual intimacy with God. When a leader abides in Christ, their influence becomes an extension of His life flowing through them. But when a leader disconnects from the Source, their leadership becomes mechanical, weary, and ineffective.

Jesus modeled this rhythm of abiding throughout His ministry. Even though crowds constantly pressed around Him and demands never ceased, He withdrew often to pray (Mark 1:35, Luke 5:16). His public power was sustained by private communion. True leadership strength comes not from perpetual motion but from consistent connection.

To “remain in the vine” means to continually draw life, wisdom, and renewal from Christ. Just as a branch does not strain to produce fruit but simply stays attached, a Christian leader bears lasting fruit by staying rooted in Christ’s presence. The moment we begin leading from emptiness—relying on talent, busyness, or adrenaline—we move from grace to self-effort, and the fruit begins to wither.

Practical Application

Rest in God’s Presence, Not Just in Productivity

It is easy for leaders to confuse activity with fruitfulness. Jesus reminds us that apart from Him, we can do nothing of eternal value. Take intentional moments throughout the day to pause, breathe, and realign your heart with His presence. Ministry done in God’s rest bears more fruit than ministry done in human rush.

Practical Tip:

Practice stillness—take five quiet minutes midday to reconnect with God’s presence and remember why you are leading.

Establish Rhythms of Renewal to Prevent Burnout

Healthy leaders plan for both work and rest. Schedule regular times for sabbath, retreat, and reflection. These rhythms restore perspective and protect your heart from spiritual exhaustion. Even Jesus invited His disciples to “come. . . to a quiet place and get some rest” (Mark 6:31). Renewal is not a luxury; it is obedience to the design of God.

Practical Tip:

Build a sustainable pace—plan weekly rest, quarterly retreats, and annual sabbaticals to maintain spiritual vitality.

Lead from Overflow in Relationships and Decision Making

When you are spiritually full, your interactions are marked by grace, patience, and wisdom. When you are empty, your leadership becomes reactive, controlling, or anxious. By staying connected to the Vine, you allow the Holy Spirit to flow through you into every meeting, conversation, and challenge.

Practical Tip:

Before major decisions or difficult conversations, pause to pray: “Lord, let me lead from Your strength, not my stress.”

Model Healthy Rhythms for Your Team

Your example sets the tone for your organization. If you live exhausted, your team will think exhaustion is the price of faithfulness. If you live renewed, they will learn that rest is an act of trust. Show your team what it means to prioritize soul health and balance.

Practical Tip:

Encourage your team to take Sabbath rest, attend retreats, and nurture their own walk with God. Lead with both vision and vitality.

Reflection Questions:

1. Am I leading from intimacy with Christ or from my own strength?

2. What daily or weekly practices help keep me spiritually replenished?

3. How can I model and encourage rhythms of rest and renewal within my team?

4. When was the last time I withdrew from ministry activity to simply be with Jesus?

5. What warning signs indicate that I’m leading from emptiness instead of overflow?

Fruitful leadership is not about doing more for God—it is about being more with God. Abiding leaders bear lasting fruit because their lives are rooted in the endless supply of His presence.

**Q:** Why is abiding in Christ essential for effective leadership?

**A:** Leaders cannot give what they do not have; fruitful leadership flows from remaining in Christ, not from self-effort.

**Q:** What are some practical ways leaders can maintain spiritual renewal?

**A:** Prioritize personal devotion, rest in God’s presence, establish rhythms of renewal, and model healthy practices for the team.

### Build a Godly Foundation Early

#### 1.4.1 — State at least five steps you can take toward being a person of integrity and character. Self-Leadership: How Did Daniel Do It?

Develop and maintain your own character and integrity. Here are some early and practical steps to help you accomplish that:

• Discipline yourself to do two necessary things you do not like every week.

• Fix your eyes on a clear, specific goal.

• Learn the whys behind God’s commands.

• Interview a leader who has integrity, determining how the quality was developed.

• Write out the promises you have made and take responsibility for all your actions and emotions.

• Monitor and adjust (when necessary) the motives behind your actions.

Daniel possessed character. Combine character with his courage and you have an incredible man of God. Mix in God’s favor and you have a man who influenced influencers and led nations through good and bad times. In the late 1800s, Philip Paul Bliss wrote the words and music for a popular Sunday school song about Daniel, an ideal hero. The chorus contained these words: “Dare to be a Daniel; dare to stand alone; dare to have a purpose firm; dare to make it known” (Snider 2006, Bliss 1873).

May the Lord provide in the twenty-first century, men and women of God who will dare to be Daniels! The following is a prayer, composed with the help of my colleague, Tim Elmore. I include it here to help you on your journey toward possessing greater character and integrity:

Lord, give me insight into what makes up a healthy, effective leader. Make me sensitive to Your Spirit and show me the areas in which I need to grow and develop. Please help in this area of character. Help me to be the same person I am when people are not watching me as I am when people are watching me. Thank You for Daniel’s example. Thank You that people who love You can make a difference in a lost world. I desire to be transformed in my heart and mind so that I might reflect the qualities of a leader who, like King David, is after Your own heart . Amen!

## 2 - The Heart of a Leader

### God Uses Leaders with These Qualities: Part One

#### 2.1.1 — Identify, explain, and implement the primary steps of preparing your heart for the task of leadership.

God Uses Leaders Who Have a Purpose

“There are different kinds of gifts, but the same Spirit. There are different kinds of service, but the same Lord. There are different kinds of working, but the same God works all of them in all men. Now to each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good.” (1 Corinthians 12:4–7)

“Now you are the body of Christ, and each one of you is a part of it.” (1 Corinthians 12:27)

Every leader who has been mightily used of God has discovered their God-given purpose in life. Leadership expert John Maxwell lists three crucial steps to successful leadership in his book, Your Road Map for Success : (1) Know your purpose in life; (2) Grow to reach your maximum potential; and (3) Sow seeds that benefit others (Maxwell 2002, 11).

Establishing our purpose is foundational to the life of leadership effectiveness. While God works uniquely in each individual’s life to make His will known, we can say that for everyone, God uses both our brokenness and our spiritual gifts to clarify and validate our individual purpose or mission.

God Uses Our Brokenness to Reveal Our Purpose

When we fully surrender to the lordship of Christ, we begin to see the world through the eyes of Christ. When we begin to see others the way Christ sees them, our hearts are soon broken with the things that break the heart of God. The brokenness becomes a revealer of our growing relationship with God and the burden that begins to grow in our hearts as leaders. The heart of a surrendered child of God is gripped with a sense of urgency about real needs in the lives of people. Whether it is Mother Teresa burdened for the starving of Calcutta, Bob Pierce gripped by needy children around the world, or Billy Graham broken over lost souls in America’s major cities, a vision to make an eternal difference seems to begin when a Christian’s heart is broken over a specific need that requires personal involvement.

God Uses Our Giftedness to Reveal Our Purpose

God’s purpose for our lives is closely aligned with our strengths in ministry. Our Father assigns us where we can best use our gifts and abilities to our maximum potential for His greatest glory. In this sense, the giftedness becomes a revealer of how and where we can best serve Him. When we are using God-given strengths in Kingdom work, we experience our greatest fulfillment and we perform to our greatest effectiveness. It is then that we love what we do regardless of the work’s difficulties and demands.

Do you know your God-given purpose? As a help, ask yourself these questions:

What are my burdens?

What are my spiritual gifts?

What are my natural talents?

What are my desires and passions?

What do others affirm about or within me?

What are my dreams and visions?

What opportunities lie before me?

God Uses Leaders Who Move Past Hindrances

Therefore, since we are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, let us throw off everything that hinders and the sin that so easily entangles, and let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us. Let us fix our eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy set before him endured the cross, scorning its shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God. (Hebrews 12:1–2)

Character and integrity are indispensable. Character can be defined as self-leadership. Once you have learned to take responsibility for governing your own will, others will want to follow you. Again I emphasize this: self-leadership is the foundation on which the leader’s life is built. Leadership operates on the basis of trust. If people do not trust you, they will not follow you. Character adds these intangibles to a person’s life:

Character communicates credibility.

Character garners respect.

Character creates consistency.

Character earns trust.

To develop strong character, a person must choose to develop their personal discipline, identity, convictions, values, and ethics (Maxwell 2003a, 11–12).

A Christian’s character must be rooted in the character of Christ himself. The process begins when we are born again. The things of our old natures pass away. All things become new. There is an instant transformation of the heart resulting in a desire to be like Jesus. Our passion to be Christlike is intensified as we surrender our hearts and lives fully to the control of the Holy Spirit. The journey continues as our minds are saturated with the Word of God. With His help we are enabled to think like Jesus: His thoughts become our thoughts, His values our values, His choices our choices, and His priorities our priorities.

Our personalities are refined by life’s experiences. Often it is daily interaction with caustic , abrasive people that polishes the rough edges from our lives. Sometimes it is the difficult places, the adversities, and the battles that mold us into conformity to the beautiful image of our Savior. A great sculptor was looking at a large piece of marble when he was asked what he planned to create from the rough stone. He replied that he intended to produce an angel. He was asked how he would do this. The master craftsman replied, “Simply remove everything that is not part of an angel.” God is working in our lives to remove everything from our character that is not like Jesus. It is liberating to give Him all that hinders our spiritual growth.

God Uses Leaders Who Are Completely Available to Him

“Therefore, I urge you, brothers, in view of God’s mercy, to offer your bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God—this is your spiritual act of worship. Do not conform any longer to the pattern of this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind. Then you will be able to test and approve what God’s will is—his good, pleasing and perfect will.” (Romans 12:1–2)

Being completely and consistently available to God’s call and good will is the same as being totally at God’s disposal . He can pour you out or fill you in whenever He sees the need; He sees the big picture all the time. His plan simply requires, on our part, total surrender to God. Then, as we serve Him more, we come to realize that surrender is the route to our spiritual growth and the enhancement of our ministry. Of course this process does a major overhaul to the attitudes we possessed at the beginning of our journey as a spiritual leader. Eventually each leader who earnestly desires to guide others must allow God to modify their attitudes and desires. Then we can relax.

We will have no need to prove our self-worth because God will validate our self-worth .

We will not need to struggle to gain status because we have nothing to lose .

We will not need to put on a mask because we have nothing to hide.

The story of Jim Elliott and his missionary colleagues who died in South America is well known. The five men were brutally murdered by the people they were trying to reach with the message of God’s love and salvation. These devoted missionaries’ lives truly reflected what Jim had penned in his journal years before their deaths: “He is no fool who gives what he cannot keep to gain what he cannot lose” (Elliot 1958, 108). As faithful servants called of God, they had prayed like their Savior in Gethsemane, “Not my will, but yours be done” (Luke 22:42) and then placed themselves unreservedly at the disposal of the Master. They believed the Christian’s calling in life is to a Person, not to a place or a position. When Jesus is in full control in our lives, reigning and ruling over our hearts, bending our wills to conform to His will, we can be at peace with His selection of the place and position of our service. When He makes clear our assignment, whatever its circumstances, our reply is, “Yes, Lord.”

In the spring of 2005, a young Muslim girl was walking across the campus of the largest university in her country. Since 1979, her homeland had been dominated by radical factions. The government, controlled by an extremist religious group, persecuted Christians, repressed the people (especially the women), and supported all sorts of terrorism worldwide. This college girl had become a Christian in response to a popular film that had been smuggled into her country and shown in a secret location on her campus. After giving her heart to Christ, she desired to share her faith with family and friends. She knew the cost could be her life. One morning during her prayer time she decided she would witness the next time God gave her an opportunity. Like Queen Esther, she vowed to God, “If I perish, I perish” (Esther 4:16). On the way to her first class she became strongly impressed to share her faith with the professor, but beyond being frightened, she had no idea what to say to him. She again promised the Lord her obedience, although she knew the result could be imprisonment or death.

When the class ended and all students had left, she approached him, sharing out of her heart about how Jesus had changed her life. She was amazed when he began to weep and share the account of a dream he had experienced the night before. God had gone before her and prepared his heart. He told her that in his dream he stood with a huge mass of people before the great God of the universe. He realized he was about to receive a sentence of eternal punishment. In his dream he began to run everywhere, searching frantically for Muhammed who could save him from punishment. Muhammed was nowhere to be found. The professor told his student, “I heard a loud voice in my dream saying, ‘Muhammed is not real. Jesus is the true and only Savior!’” He asked her how he could know Jesus as his Savior. This professor became a Christian.

A few days later, a similar experience happened as the young woman was being taxied to her campus. God spoke to her heart that she was to witness to the taxi driver. At first she hesitated, knowing he had radical opinions and could drive her directly to the secret police if provoked. But she again shared about Jesus.

Immediately he began to weep and stopped his taxi on a side street. He said, “Last night I saw Jesus in a dream. In my own language I heard him say He died to give me peace in my heart. What does this mean?” Within minutes the taxi driver had discovered the peace that “transcends all understanding” (Philippians 4:7).

While still in college this young lady began a house church in the city of her grandparents, a city of approximately seven hundred thousand people without a single church or gospel witness until she arrived. One young Christian placed herself absolutely at God’s disposal, choosing to obey at any cost.

God Uses Leaders Who Prevail in Prayer

“The prayer of a righteous man is powerful and effective.” (James 5:16)

Jesus lists three kinds of prayer in Matthew 7:7. He said we must learn to ask . This is the prayer in which we lay hold of God’s promises by faith. He said we must learn to seek . This is the prayer of dedication in which we seek to know God’s will. And He encourages us to knock (Maxwell 2003a, 12). This is the prayer of intercession whereby we stand in prayer for someone who cannot or will not pray for themselves. A leader continues to labor in this area during their service and in every life circumstance.

God Uses Leaders Who Are Students of His Word

“Do your best to present yourself to God as one approved, a workman who does not need to be ashamed and who correctly handles the word of truth.” (2 Timothy 2:15)

“All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work.” (2 Timothy 3:16–17)

The leader who is a student of God’s Word does not simply read the Bible for the sake of knowing what content is found where. This servant has come to know that it is necessary to study God’s Word. The aim in searching the Scripture is to understand its meaning. You will have the help of the Holy Spirit as you study; it has been said that the Bible is the only book that is always read in its author’s presence. Bible study involves a process of asking and answering questions about what you are reading:

What did it mean to the original audience?

What is the universal and timeless principle we can learn now?

What should we do in response to it?

If you determine the answers to those questions, you come closer to forming a correct interpretation for each passage you study. You can also enhance your understanding through study Bibles and other resources, mentoring, and group study.

“As you hold out the word of life—in order that I may boast on the day of Christ that I did not run or labor for nothing.” (Philippians 2:16)

In Romans 1:14–16, the apostle Paul mentions three elements that should be true for every leader who wants to be used by God to present the gospel of Jesus Christ. They know it is the most vital message and will meet the needs of any man, woman, or child, anywhere in the world. All believers should make these three commitments:

1. I am obligated (v. 14)—I am persuaded that sharing the message is a debt I owe to the world.

2. I am eager (v. 15)—I am compelled by a fire within to share this message with the world.

3. I am not ashamed (v. 16)—I am moved by the privilege to share it because it alone can save us.

Sometimes young children have more boldness and less inhibition about telling others about Jesus. One young mother had to take her six-year-old son to the hair salon with her when the babysitter cancelled at the last minute. The little boy asked one of the stylists if she wanted to know how to become a Christian. She smiled at him but said, “No, thanks.” He did not give up easily; he simply pleaded, “Don’t you want to go to heaven?” Leaders should rekindle this kind of enthusiasm and persistence, becoming obligated, eager, and not ashamed.

#### 2.1.2 — Describe the tools for shaping a leader’s mind, and demonstrate their applications.

God Uses Leaders Who Have a Purpose

“There are different kinds of gifts, but the same Spirit. There are different kinds of service, but the same Lord. There are different kinds of working, but the same God works all of them in all men. Now to each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good.” (1 Corinthians 12:4–7)

“Now you are the body of Christ, and each one of you is a part of it.” (1 Corinthians 12:27)

Every leader who has been mightily used of God has discovered their God-given purpose in life. Leadership expert John Maxwell lists three crucial steps to successful leadership in his book, Your Road Map for Success : (1) Know your purpose in life; (2) Grow to reach your maximum potential; and (3) Sow seeds that benefit others (Maxwell 2002, 11).

Establishing our purpose is foundational to the life of leadership effectiveness. While God works uniquely in each individual’s life to make His will known, we can say that for everyone, God uses both our brokenness and our spiritual gifts to clarify and validate our individual purpose or mission.

God Uses Our Brokenness to Reveal Our Purpose

When we fully surrender to the lordship of Christ, we begin to see the world through the eyes of Christ. When we begin to see others the way Christ sees them, our hearts are soon broken with the things that break the heart of God. The brokenness becomes a revealer of our growing relationship with God and the burden that begins to grow in our hearts as leaders. The heart of a surrendered child of God is gripped with a sense of urgency about real needs in the lives of people. Whether it is Mother Teresa burdened for the starving of Calcutta, Bob Pierce gripped by needy children around the world, or Billy Graham broken over lost souls in America’s major cities, a vision to make an eternal difference seems to begin when a Christian’s heart is broken over a specific need that requires personal involvement.

God Uses Our Giftedness to Reveal Our Purpose

God’s purpose for our lives is closely aligned with our strengths in ministry. Our Father assigns us where we can best use our gifts and abilities to our maximum potential for His greatest glory. In this sense, the giftedness becomes a revealer of how and where we can best serve Him. When we are using God-given strengths in Kingdom work, we experience our greatest fulfillment and we perform to our greatest effectiveness. It is then that we love what we do regardless of the work’s difficulties and demands.

Do you know your God-given purpose? As a help, ask yourself these questions:

What are my burdens?

What are my spiritual gifts?

What are my natural talents?

What are my desires and passions?

What do others affirm about or within me?

What are my dreams and visions?

What opportunities lie before me?

God Uses Leaders Who Move Past Hindrances

Therefore, since we are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, let us throw off everything that hinders and the sin that so easily entangles, and let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us. Let us fix our eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy set before him endured the cross, scorning its shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God. (Hebrews 12:1–2)

Character and integrity are indispensable. Character can be defined as self-leadership. Once you have learned to take responsibility for governing your own will, others will want to follow you. Again I emphasize this: self-leadership is the foundation on which the leader’s life is built. Leadership operates on the basis of trust. If people do not trust you, they will not follow you. Character adds these intangibles to a person’s life:

Character communicates credibility.

Character garners respect.

Character creates consistency.

Character earns trust.

To develop strong character, a person must choose to develop their personal discipline, identity, convictions, values, and ethics (Maxwell 2003a, 11–12).

A Christian’s character must be rooted in the character of Christ himself. The process begins when we are born again. The things of our old natures pass away. All things become new. There is an instant transformation of the heart resulting in a desire to be like Jesus. Our passion to be Christlike is intensified as we surrender our hearts and lives fully to the control of the Holy Spirit. The journey continues as our minds are saturated with the Word of God. With His help we are enabled to think like Jesus: His thoughts become our thoughts, His values our values, His choices our choices, and His priorities our priorities.

Our personalities are refined by life’s experiences. Often it is daily interaction with caustic , abrasive people that polishes the rough edges from our lives. Sometimes it is the difficult places, the adversities, and the battles that mold us into conformity to the beautiful image of our Savior. A great sculptor was looking at a large piece of marble when he was asked what he planned to create from the rough stone. He replied that he intended to produce an angel. He was asked how he would do this. The master craftsman replied, “Simply remove everything that is not part of an angel.” God is working in our lives to remove everything from our character that is not like Jesus. It is liberating to give Him all that hinders our spiritual growth.

God Uses Leaders Who Are Completely Available to Him

“Therefore, I urge you, brothers, in view of God’s mercy, to offer your bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God—this is your spiritual act of worship. Do not conform any longer to the pattern of this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind. Then you will be able to test and approve what God’s will is—his good, pleasing and perfect will.” (Romans 12:1–2)

Being completely and consistently available to God’s call and good will is the same as being totally at God’s disposal . He can pour you out or fill you in whenever He sees the need; He sees the big picture all the time. His plan simply requires, on our part, total surrender to God. Then, as we serve Him more, we come to realize that surrender is the route to our spiritual growth and the enhancement of our ministry. Of course this process does a major overhaul to the attitudes we possessed at the beginning of our journey as a spiritual leader. Eventually each leader who earnestly desires to guide others must allow God to modify their attitudes and desires. Then we can relax.

We will have no need to prove our self-worth because God will validate our self-worth .

We will not need to struggle to gain status because we have nothing to lose .

We will not need to put on a mask because we have nothing to hide.

The story of Jim Elliott and his missionary colleagues who died in South America is well known. The five men were brutally murdered by the people they were trying to reach with the message of God’s love and salvation. These devoted missionaries’ lives truly reflected what Jim had penned in his journal years before their deaths: “He is no fool who gives what he cannot keep to gain what he cannot lose” (Elliot 1958, 108). As faithful servants called of God, they had prayed like their Savior in Gethsemane, “Not my will, but yours be done” (Luke 22:42) and then placed themselves unreservedly at the disposal of the Master. They believed the Christian’s calling in life is to a Person, not to a place or a position. When Jesus is in full control in our lives, reigning and ruling over our hearts, bending our wills to conform to His will, we can be at peace with His selection of the place and position of our service. When He makes clear our assignment, whatever its circumstances, our reply is, “Yes, Lord.”

In the spring of 2005, a young Muslim girl was walking across the campus of the largest university in her country. Since 1979, her homeland had been dominated by radical factions. The government, controlled by an extremist religious group, persecuted Christians, repressed the people (especially the women), and supported all sorts of terrorism worldwide. This college girl had become a Christian in response to a popular film that had been smuggled into her country and shown in a secret location on her campus. After giving her heart to Christ, she desired to share her faith with family and friends. She knew the cost could be her life. One morning during her prayer time she decided she would witness the next time God gave her an opportunity. Like Queen Esther, she vowed to God, “If I perish, I perish” (Esther 4:16). On the way to her first class she became strongly impressed to share her faith with the professor, but beyond being frightened, she had no idea what to say to him. She again promised the Lord her obedience, although she knew the result could be imprisonment or death.

When the class ended and all students had left, she approached him, sharing out of her heart about how Jesus had changed her life. She was amazed when he began to weep and share the account of a dream he had experienced the night before. God had gone before her and prepared his heart. He told her that in his dream he stood with a huge mass of people before the great God of the universe. He realized he was about to receive a sentence of eternal punishment. In his dream he began to run everywhere, searching frantically for Muhammed who could save him from punishment. Muhammed was nowhere to be found. The professor told his student, “I heard a loud voice in my dream saying, ‘Muhammed is not real. Jesus is the true and only Savior!’” He asked her how he could know Jesus as his Savior. This professor became a Christian.

A few days later, a similar experience happened as the young woman was being taxied to her campus. God spoke to her heart that she was to witness to the taxi driver. At first she hesitated, knowing he had radical opinions and could drive her directly to the secret police if provoked. But she again shared about Jesus.

Immediately he began to weep and stopped his taxi on a side street. He said, “Last night I saw Jesus in a dream. In my own language I heard him say He died to give me peace in my heart. What does this mean?” Within minutes the taxi driver had discovered the peace that “transcends all understanding” (Philippians 4:7).

While still in college this young lady began a house church in the city of her grandparents, a city of approximately seven hundred thousand people without a single church or gospel witness until she arrived. One young Christian placed herself absolutely at God’s disposal, choosing to obey at any cost.

God Uses Leaders Who Prevail in Prayer

“The prayer of a righteous man is powerful and effective.” (James 5:16)

Jesus lists three kinds of prayer in Matthew 7:7. He said we must learn to ask . This is the prayer in which we lay hold of God’s promises by faith. He said we must learn to seek . This is the prayer of dedication in which we seek to know God’s will. And He encourages us to knock (Maxwell 2003a, 12). This is the prayer of intercession whereby we stand in prayer for someone who cannot or will not pray for themselves. A leader continues to labor in this area during their service and in every life circumstance.

#### 2.2.1 — Describe how giftings, anointing, and personal confidence operate in the life of the leader.

God Uses Leaders Who Expect Results

“Without weakening in his faith, he faced the fact that his body was as good as dead—since he was about a hundred years old—and that Sarah’s womb was also dead. Yet he did not waver through unbelief regarding the promise of God, but was strengthened in his faith and gave glory to God, being fully persuaded that God had power to do what he had promised.” (Romans 4:19–21).

Hebrews 11:1 defines faith: “Now faith is being sure of what we hope for and certain of what we do not see.” Hebrews 11:13 describes men and women of faith and what they each had in common:

Vision: Each saw the promises from far off.

Confidence: Each was assured of the promises of God.

Passion: Each embraced and owned the promises as their own.

Resolve: They confessed that they were pilgrims on the earth.

Dreams: Their God-given dreams, not their memories, consumed them.

God Uses Leaders Who Serve in Attitude and Action

“Your attitude should be the same as that of Christ Jesus: Who, being in very nature God, did not consider equality with God something to be grasped, but made himself nothing, taking the very nature of a servant, being made in human likeness. And being found in appearance as a man, he humbled himself and became obedient to death—even death on a cross!” (Philippians 2:5–8)

In the passage above, Paul instructs us to embrace the same mindset that compelled Jesus to lead by serving in attitude and action. Although He was God, He did not cling to His position but rather to His purpose. His priorities were always aligned with His Father’s priorities; He was not conscious of position, but conscious of purpose. He knew the way to accomplish His purpose was to serve people, meeting them in their needs, right where they were. Leaders naturally arise when someone determines to serve, because (1) that need sparks passion from within; (2) the need causes one to respond with action; and (3) this action will likely move others to cooperate.

An attitude of humility is essential to servant leadership. Here are some thoughts on humility from the book, The Bible on Leadership: From Moses to Matthew—Management Lessons for Contemporary Leaders :

A leader is no more intrinsically important than his people, but his actions are.

No matter how much you achieve or how much acclaim you are given, you are still human.

Express sincere appreciation for your team. Where and who would you be without them?

Recognize the interdependence of yourself and your followers; the head is useless without the arms or the feet.

Honor the unique gifts of each member of your team.

As you rise higher, your mistakes have more impact and your need for a humble perspective actually increases.

Do not hold people to standards you are not meeting yourself.

To humble oneself is risky, but it usually pays off in increased credibility.

A leader is “greater” than others only insofar as he serves them.

(Woolfe 2002, 89).

God Uses Leaders Who Are Prompted by the Gifts Within

“Until I come, devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture, to preaching and to teaching. Do not neglect your gift, which was given you through a prophetic message when the body of elders laid their hands on you. Be diligent in these matters; give yourself wholly to them, so that everyone may see your progress. Watch your life and doctrine closely. Persevere in them, because if you do, you will save both yourself and your hearers.” (1 Timothy 4:13–16)

Leaders naturally arise when they identify their gifts and see opportunity to use them. Leaders who are used and led by God know the importance of finding their gifts for the sake of ministry. A leader should first identify the primary gift that God has placed in their life. Knowledge of your gift will make you want to exercise it so it is developed. It is important to intentionally find avenues to develop your gift. When you do, you are investing in your own growth while providing one of the best examples for other believers and the future of your role in leadership. Seeking will result in matching your gift with the right place . When you use your gift in an effective place of service, you will have a platform of influence. Maintain an awareness of this influence and serve accordingly. You can expect the fruits of your labors to eventually flourish because you have been faithful in the use of your gift.

God Uses Leaders Who Empower Others

“Jesus knew that the Father had put all things under his power, and that he had come from God and was returning to God; so he got up from the meal, took off his outer clothing, and wrapped a towel around his waist. After that, he poured water into a basin and began to wash his disciples’ feet, drying them with the towel that was wrapped around him.” (John 13:3–5)

In John 13, Jesus modeled a servant’s heart when He washed the disciples’ feet. Note what enabled Him to do this. It was a strong sense of security in His identity. Leaders who are not secure in their identity in Christ will eventually sabotage their leadership. Insecure leaders become their own worst enemy. They cannot share victories or sorrows. The Law of Empowerment teaches us that only secure leaders give their power to others. There is a distinct difference between those with a sense of security with self and those who lack that feeling of security. God Uses Leaders Who Depend on the Anointing of the Holy Spirit

“Do not get drunk on wine, which leads to debauchery. Instead, be filled with the Spirit. Speak to one another with psalms, hymns and spiritual songs. Sing and make music in your heart to the Lord, always giving thanks to God the Father for everything, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.” (Ephesians 5:18–20)

Peter was one of the most outspoken of the disciples and one of the most prominent leaders in the New Testament. Peter’s cowardly denial of Jesus disappoints us, and yet we admire his courageous leadership in the early church. In Acts 4 we read about the following three admirable qualities in Peter that were manifested after he had been filled with the Holy Spirit.

Spirit-Filled

We read in Acts 4:8 that Peter was “filled with the Holy Spirit.” Prior to Acts 2, we could say that Peter was filled with self . His life prior to Pentecost was dominated by self-centeredness, selfishness, and self-sufficiency, and that was the source of his inconsistency, instability, and even the denial of the Lord.

In Acts 2 Peter was emptied of his self-focus and filled with the Spirit of the resurrected Christ. In Acts 3, Peter and John are serving the Lord in a beautiful spirit of oneness. Their hearts are set ablaze with a mutual passion to reach a lost world. Their hearts are beautifully knitted together in love. No longer are they in a competition with each other, no longer seeking recognition and honor. Now they model genuine servanthood and exemplify disciples who desire that all praise and honor be given to the Lord Jesus.

Christ-Centered

In Acts 4:12 Peter continues to proclaim the name of Jesus, boldly declaring to the rulers, elders, and teachers of the law that “salvation is found in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given to men by which we must be saved.” The role of the Holy Spirit in our lives is to lift up Jesus.

Like all who experienced the purifying work of God in Acts 2, the central theme of Peter’s world now is Jesus, his Savior and Lord. He is consumed with a passionate desire to effectively proclaim Christ and His salvation to all who are lost.

In the same way, Spirit-filled disciples proclaim Jesus through these five representative words that begin with the letter l ; each represents an expression for serving the Lord:

Lips: He is the theme of their lives.

Lives: Their deeds are consistent with their words.

Love: Their motives and actions are controlled by a John 3:16 quality of love, love flowing through them.

Liberty: Their hearts overflow with a victorious spirit of praise. They are enabled to praise God in even the most difficult of circumstances.

Loot: Their lives are marked by biblical stewardship . They joyfully give their time, talents, and treasure to His service (Coker quoted by Carter 2000, 43).

Christ-Like

In Acts 4:13 the Scripture states that the enemies of the gospel “took note that these men had been with Jesus.” Yes, they saw Jesus in Peter and John. Their lives had become imitations of His life. The beauty of Jesus is seen in these common men who had made an uncommon surrender to the lordship of Christ.

God Uses Leaders Who Maintain Integrity

“Do you not know that in a race all the runners run, but only one gets the prize? Run in such a way as to get the prize. Everyone who competes in the games goes into strict training. They do it to get a crown that will not last; but we do it to get a crown that will last forever. Therefore I do not run like a man running aimlessly; I do not fight like a man beating the air. No, I beat my body and make it my slave so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified for the prize.” (1 Corinthians 9:24–27)

A heart of integrity is necessary for effective leadership. Consider these reasons:

Leadership functions on the basis of trust.

Integrity has high influence value.

Our tendency is to work harder on our image than on our integrity.

Integrity means the leader lives the truth in their own life before leading others.

A charismatic personality may draw people, but only integrity will keep them.

Integrity is a victory, not a gift.

You will only become what you are becoming right now.

Leaders are to live by a higher standard than followers.

(Maxwell 2003a, 15).

The best leader has the heart of a servant. A servant-leader serves the mission and serves those on mission with them. This type of leader’s objective is to lead as Jesus led, and they are willing to be treated as Jesus was treated. Some will follow your leadership; others will throw stones at you. The point is that the servant-leader does not seek a position; the goal of all seeking is to be like Jesus .

## 3 - The High Calling of Leadership

### Leadership Requires Accountability and Service

#### 3.1.1 — Explain the accountability and servant mentality required to be an effective spiritual leader.

Accountability

For societies where leadership qualifications are minimized to questionable standards, Paul provides another perspective, starting with accountability. “So then, men ought to regard us” (1 Corinthians 4:1). The word regard means “to reckon or account.” It comes from the same Greek root that gives us the English word logic . In Paul’s day, it was a bookkeeping term that meant “to calculate,” “to number,” or “to be precise.” The verb form of the word means “to put together in one’s mind various calculations.”

This is a sobering reminder that the life of a leader is one of accountability . Paul’s position is that accountability is a logical obligation for a leader to embrace. Conversely, the concept infers that for a leader to live a life void of accountability is illogical. It makes no sense at all. Tragically, many leaders live and lead without submitting to accountability. The church world is not excluded from this indictment. As an illustration we consider the role of a bookkeeper or an accountant. In today’s world, a bookkeeper serves an organization, whether religious or secular, by handling the financial accounts of business transactions. Likewise, an accountant is the person who inspects and audits financial records and makes reports.

Often, a financial audit can make or break the future of an organization. Many times professional bookkeepers and accountants are responsible for making sure the books of a business or ministry are balanced and accurate. An unwillingness to be audited has sometimes spelled doom and closed the doors of many organizations. People neither trust nor desire to build relationships with organizations that tend to cloak their financial dealings. Openness is expected and makes good business sense.

Paul is telling the Corinthians, in chapter 4, that Christian leaders are to be like open books—with nothing to hide. In other words, Christian leaders should be willing to be held accountable for their actions, first to God and then to their constituents. If a leader does not adhere to structures of accountability, the result will be a leader out of balance . An unbalanced leader is an unhealthy leader who will produce an unhealthy organization.

We know that Jesus was the only servant-leader who had a perfect record. Even in the lives of stellar leaders, those we are calling “opened-book leaders,” there exist liabilities, weaknesses, and vulnerabilities . If ever these liabilities or vulnerabilities are purposefully hidden and unchecked, they can cause a leader to fall.

When a leader agrees to be held accountable, they will realize a continual blessing. Many leaders have discovered the value of being a part of an “accountability group.” Such groups are beneficial, particularly for leaders who constantly face temptations to cut corners, make unwise choices, or neglect their personal or ministry commitments.

Accountability is essential in the life of an effective Christian leader. For instance, in 1 Corinthians 3–4, Paul relates four accountability relationships to underscore the importance of the issue.

First is the example of the masters and servants relationship (3:5). Paul and Apollos were leaders, yet they also were servants. Here is a vivid reminder that those in leadership must be held accountable in their dual roles as masters and servants. Then Paul mentions the farmer and soil relationship (3:6). He teaches a lesson regarding the concept of sowing seeds in soil to reap a harvest. For the seed to take root, the farmer must be responsible to plant at the correct time and in the correct place and to apply water and fertilizer. To reap a harvest, the farmer does not act alone; he depends on the elements of seeds, soil, sun, water, and fertilizer.

Another example of accountability is portrayed in the coworkers relationship (3:9). Anyone who works with others is more effective if they are in an accountability relationship. A worker who does not commit to the success of another worker diminishes production, lessens morale, and sours the attitudes of the organization.

The fourth accountability example Paul gives is that of the father and son relationship (4:14). Parents are accountable to their children and children to their parents. Healthy families understand and practice this two-way principle of accountability. Parents are accountable to provide safety, security, and loving nurture for their children. Children are accountable to parents for their conduct and obedience.

Whether in the marketplace or at home, the Christian leader must be held to a tangible level of accountability; if an accountability relationship is not in place at the leadership level, the organization will experience chaos, less productivity, and division among the people working for a common mission. Spiritual power comes when leaders are covered with an umbrella of accountability and when their relationships and lives are supported within fences of protection. Christian leaders are to be people with consistent and meaningful boundaries of accountability, endeavoring to live balanced lives. Followers deserve this kind of leader.

Service

Paul teaches the importance of service as his instruction continues in 1 Corinthians 4: “So then, men ought to regard us as servants” (v. 1). The word Paul uses for “servant” also means “minister.” The meaning comes from a Greek word which refers to attendants, slaves, and servants.

Originally, the word was used to describe those who were slaves on a ship, whose job it was to row the oars in the galley, the lowest part of a ship. A galley slave had the most menial job on the ship; he existed in squalid conditions. Over time, the word servant was used when referring to anyone under the authority of another. Paul is clear: Christian leaders are to be “servant” leaders.

Christian leadership is not about achieving power or authority. The late Ray Steadman wrote:

Our churches are not to be hierarchies of bosses, but “lower-archies” of servants, where Christians compete not to lead and rule, but to love and serve. . . . In the church we have popes, bishops, priests, reverends, chairmen, superintendents, and authorities of every description. We even have unofficial “church bosses” and “power brokers”—those individuals in the church who are feared and obeyed, even when they have no official title or job description in the church structure. But that is not the kind of authority Jesus modeled and taught to His church. He taught that those who serve, regardless of their title, are the ones who earn the mantle of authority in His church. (1993, 354)

In 1 Corinthians 14:40, Paul writes, “But everything should be in a fitting and orderly way.” The church needs organization, and God has given offices and titles for people to lead. However, the strength of leadership in the Christian community does not come with a title; it comes with an orientation of serving others before self.

It is appropriate to ask, “To whom is the servant to be loyal?” Paul says that above all else leaders are servants “of Christ” (4:1). Again, this is the image of the under-rowers of ancient Greece and Rome. Only the heavy laborers are the shepherds over the local bodies, serving under the headship of Jesus Christ. We can think of leaders as the galley slaves of Christ. In many circles, this is still a novel idea.

How does the local church or any Christian organization go forward? A body of believers progresses when servant leaders tirelessly and sacrificially provide the locomotion and acceleration, like those who row a boat in the lowest parts of a ship. Try to imagine a church without servant leadership. Perhaps you do not have to imagine; you may know of such a church. If you do, you know that body is not advancing.

A Christian leader will never serve people rightly until they serve the Lord rightly, and the only way to do that is to take on the form of a servant-leader. Our Lord demonstrated servant-leadership in a most vivid manner in John 13:1–16 when He washed His disciples’ feet. These eight points form His mode of leadership.

Christlike servant leaders

are motivated by love to serve others (vv. 1–2);

possess a security that allows them to minister to others (v. 3);

initiate servant ministry to others (vv. 4–5);

receive servant ministry from others (vv. 6–7);

resist what will interfere with their relationship with Jesus (vv. 8–9);

teach servanthood by their example (vv. 12–15);

live a blessed life (vv. 16–17); and

are guided by a philosophy opposite of that promoted by the world (vv. 18–19).

Every leader will be faced with the temptation to power up on people, force an issue, or feel there must be a win. But Jesus sets another standard, a higher standard. Those who are called to lead must remember that at the core of their ability to influence others for God is the essential need for a servant’s heart.

Accountability is acknowledging that our choices and decisions have an impact on others, and that impact must be considered carefully. A leader must demonstrate awareness that even their personal decisions affect others. Accountability often requires a leader to adhere to a higher standard than others.

**A:** Chaos in relationship, little or no productivity, division in homes and the organization, prideful behavior, and weak responses to temptations.

**Q:** How do you define servanthood ?

**A:** This will be a personal response, but should include a clear sense of putting the needs of others ahead of one’s own.

### Leadership Requires Able Management and Faithfulness

#### 3.2.1 — Describe the responsibility of being an effective steward, and demonstrate such responsibility.

Able Management

“Men ought to regard us as servants of Christ and of those entrusted with the secret things of God.” (1 Corinthians 4:1)

Some Bible translations speak of being “stewards of the mysteries of God.” The words “entrusted” and “steward” are interchangeable. A steward is someone who has been entrusted with grave responsibility or with valuable material possessions, perhaps even entrusted with individuals who belong to the master.

Often in the Bible when an individual is entrusted with something, it is in the context of being a house manager. In both the Old and New Testaments affluent owners would hire someone to manage their property. This manager’s responsibility extended during the owner’s presence or absence. Not only was the property or persons to be cared for, but there were other employees who needed supervision. Even the children of the owner were often to be cared for by the steward.

In Genesis 39, the role and responsibility of house manager was given to Joseph. He was the steward or manager in Potiphar’s home.

Joseph found favor in his [Potiphar’s] eyes and became his attendant. Potiphar put him in charge of his household, and he entrusted to his care everything he owned. From the time he put him in charge of his household and all that he owned, the Lord blessed the household of the Egyptian because of Joseph. . . . So he left in Joseph’s care everything he had; with Joseph in charge he did not concern himself with anything except the food he ate. (vv. 4–6)

Undoubtedly, Joseph had a budget, a schedule, events to plan, and staff meetings to supervise. His was a big job for an important person. There were challenges, temptations, and expectations. Being the manager of a home was not easy then, and it is not easy now. Anyone who manages a home with children knows that managing a home and a family is not trouble-free.

Paul speaks of the steward managing the “secret things” of God. It seems Paul is directly speaking in this context to those who have been called to positions where they must teach the Word of God and are to be sensitive to the Spirit of God. The words secret things and mysteries of God literally carry the idea of things only known by divine revelation. Paul is making a strong case for the importance of a leader taking God’s revealed Word and giving instructions from that Word to the household of God. In short, Paul is sharing the importance of a Spirit-empowered communicator being a Bible manager. None of the mysteries of the divine revelation are to be held back. All of it is to be taught, even the parts believers may never fully understand in this life. That is part of what makes them mysteries.

A Bible manager or steward is to present to the household of God all truth. The great mysteries of our faith, like the virgin birth, predestination, the Trinity, and the sovereignty of God are to be fully proclaimed even though they may not be fully understood.

Therefore, since through God’s mercy we have this ministry, we do not lose heart. Rather, we have renounced secret and shameful ways; we do not use deception, nor do we distort the word of God. On the contrary, by setting forth the truth plainly we commend ourselves to every man’s conscience in the sight of God. (2 Corinthians 4:1–2)

Unlike so many, we do not peddle the word of God for profit. On the contrary, in Christ we speak before God with sincerity, like men sent from God (2 Corinthians 2:17).

All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16).

Perhaps the reason many Christians experience spiritual malnutrition is that they are hearing from preachers who are giving them either an unbalanced diet of biblical truth or not managing the Bible well in their public proclamations. All leaders who teach the Bible need to ensure they are answering questions that society is asking today, not five, ten, fifteen, or twenty years ago.

While handling God’s Word well is not the only management requirement of a pastor, it certainly is the most serious of responsibilities. Other demands may include managing a staff, a congregation, a myriad of ministries, a demanding personal schedule, and carving out time for sermon preparation. A leader understands that the Word of God plays a critical role in their life, calling, and public ministry. When you get only one opportunity every week to influence your people from the pulpit, make sure you get the Bible manager duty right! A leader who cannot manage the mysteries of God will have a challenge managing the mysteries of a church, be they spiritual mysteries or mysterious aggravations. Being a student of the Word of God is essential to caring for the house of God.

Faithfulness

“Now it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful.” (1 Corinthians 4:2)

More than anything else, Paul stresses that Christian leaders are to be faithful and trustworthy. God does not require brilliance, cleverness, articulation, creativeness, or popularity. That may be what some pulpit committees require, but not God. All those characteristics can blend together for His glory and perhaps make someone a popular speaker. But what God requires is that His leaders be faithful, trustworthy souls.

In a church world filled with job opportunities, listed with all kinds of descriptions, it is refreshing to know that first and foremost God wants His leaders to keep their word, show up on time, be honest in their dealings, be sincere in their relationships, do their very best, and just love Him with a childlike love.

Paul commended faithfulness in people’s lives when he saw it:

In Timothy’s life—“For this reason I am sending to you Timothy, my son whom I love, who is faithful in the Lord. He will remind you of my way of life in Christ Jesus, which agrees with what I teach everywhere in every church” (1 Corinthians 4:17).

In the life of Epaphras—“You learned it from Epaphras, our dear fellow servant, who is a faithful minister of Christ on our behalf” (Colossians 1:7).

In Tychicus’ life—“Tychicus will tell you all the news about me. He is a dear brother, a faithful minister and fellow servant in the Lord” (Colossians 4:7).

In his own life—“I give a judgment as one who by the Lord’s mercy is trustworthy” (1 Corinthians 7:25).

The Lord Jesus spoke of a faithful servant in Matthew 24:45–47: “Who then is the faithful and wise servant, whom the master has put in charge of the servants in his household to give them their food at the proper time? It will be good for that servant whose master finds him doing so when he returns. I tell you the truth, he will put him in charge of all his possessions.”

When Jesus comes again, He is going to judge His servants according to their faithfulness to His commandments. When all is considered, God supplies His Word; God provides the Holy Spirit; God supplies all the spiritual gifts; and God supplies the power to do the work of the ministry. About the only other thing you and I must offer God, for Him to accomplish His eternal work, is our faithfulness.

If we meet those initial requirements, a life of fruitfulness will follow.

## 4 - The Leadership Test for Potential and Maturity

### The Purposes of Testing

#### 4.1.1 — State the varied purposes of testing, and identify such purposes in your own life.

If our lives were too easy, perhaps we would become lazy and complacent . Faith grows when it is tested. In the tests we learn to trust God completely; then our souls are firmly anchored in Him, the bedrock of our faith.

Joseph’s story is one about faith in the fiery crucible of life . In the middle of rejection, false accusations, and prison isolation, his faith grew stronger. Joseph’s story reveals that tests are vital to the process of building an effective Christian leader. The book of Genesis contains only three chapters about the creation of our world, but it contains thirteen chapters about the building of Joseph into the leader God could trust and use in a vital role of leadership. For instance, early in Joseph’s life God instilled several dreams within the boy’s heart (Genesis 37). God had marked him for greatness, but the journey to the realization of that dream was long and difficult.

First, Joseph’s status as Jacob’s favorite son garnered him major resentment from his brothers. When he unwisely shared his dreams with his brothers, Joseph clearly did not follow the principles for winning friends and influencing people. So when his brothers sold him into slavery and he was taken to a strange land, we can imagine his dreams became a distant memory. No longer did Joseph envision his brothers bowing before him. Instead, he probably anticipated a cruel taskmaster ready to beat him if he resisted commands.

Joseph now faced the ultimate test. Would he abandon his faith in God and toss his God-given dreams aside, or would he cling tenaciously to the God of Israel and all He promised? Adversity presses hard and taunts us: “Can God be trusted? A dream delayed is a dream denied!” However, Joseph did not become bitter. Throughout his years of testing he embraced the disciplines of servanthood, self-control, and suffering.

Joseph learned to work hard as a servant of Potiphar. The favorite son learned the role of servant, submitting to authority. He learned to follow instructions many years before God would trust him to give orders to others. He discovered the importance of followership before he was given a place of leadership .

In Potiphar’s house Joseph developed self-control. He learned that Satan will test a child of God at a point of vulnerability. The testing in our private lives may be the most difficult challenge of all. For instance, when we are alone in front of a computer connected to the Internet, how do we handle temptation? If God cannot trust us in private, why should He ever trust us in public ministry? Joseph was young, handsome, lonely, and far from the eyes of his parents. Yielding to his master’s wife could have meant his ticket to freedom, but he refused her advances. With God’s help, he ran from sexual temptation. Ironically, his courageous self-restraint led to his imprisonment.

As an innocent prisoner, Joseph learned trust and patience. Throughout all his trials, he was able to keep looking upward. He believed God’s hand was upon his life, though his circumstances seemed to indicate otherwise. His season of testing was long and severe, but because of his faithfulness throughout the entire ordeal, God placed him in a strategic leadership role where his dreams finally came true.

He learned self-control. He was given opportunity to demonstrate his integrity in a place where his former accountability no longer existed.

### The Definition and Blessing of Testing

#### 4.2.1 — Identify and explain what constitutes real testing in the life of the leader.

Testing as it relates to leadership can be defined as an opportunity which challenges leaders to demonstrate their level of maturity and to grow in faith and obedience. Every test a Christian leader encounters reveals the state of the inner person, the heart of the servant. A leader operates out of one of three different conditions of the heart, and their reaction or response will be determined by that inward state. Those conditions can be described as inward poverty, inward plateau, and inward progress.

When you fail to trust God and act obediently, you are reflecting a condition we call inward poverty . Sometimes a time of testing may reveal that you are immature and your growth has stagnated ; in this condition you are speaking or acting out of your inward plateau. This is when your life shows the need for growth because mediocrity in your spiritual leadership is evident. The third condition is called inward progress and it is our ideal . With inward progress, your heart is marked by mature faith and consistent obedience (Maxwell 2003b, 3).

Abraham was tested. God’s purpose was to check the condition of Abraham’s heart. We find the story recorded in Genesis 22:1–2, 9–13, and a commentary on this incident is in Hebrews 11:17–19. God had asked for the sacrifice of Abraham’s son, Isaac, the long-awaited, promised son. Although God told Abraham to offer Isaac as a sacrifice, Abraham still trusted God. This was Abraham’s test and he passed it successfully, proving he had settled the issues of obedience and lordship.

David, one of Israel’s greatest leaders, had to endure many tests throughout his lifetime. Listen to his insight and note how aware he was of the need for testing:

O righteous God, who searches minds and hearts. (Psalm 7:9)

Though you probe my heart and examine me at night, though you test me, you will find nothing; I have resolved that my mouth will not sin. (Psalm 17:3)

Test me, O Lord, and try me, examine my heart and my mind. (Psalm 26:2)

Search me, O God, and know my heart; test me and know my anxious thoughts. See if there is any offensive way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. (Psalm 139:23–24)

Tests are beneficial, and leaders should welcome them. They tell us the truth when other friends cannot, or will not, be so blunt. The apostle Paul closes his final letter to the Corinthians with these sobering words: “Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith; test yourselves” (2 Corinthians 13:5). And consider the positive results James tells us we should expect from tests, no matter how difficult they are at the time: “Consider it pure joy, my brothers, whenever you face trials of many kinds, because you know that the testing of your faith develops perseverance. Perseverance must finish its work so that you may be mature and complete, not lacking anything” (James 1:2–4).

Tests are the tools of spiritual formation for shaping us into God’s instruments. But we all usually need more than one lesson before things become clear about what God is saying to us or trying to do. This may mean we will experience times of failure in our roles of leadership. When a godly man or woman fails in a leadership role, it is important to understand that spiritual formation is only the first step in building an effective Christian leader. A leader may have failed, not because of some personal flaws in character (which is where we usually lay the blame), but because of deficiencies in what I call skill formation. Competence in the basic skills of leadership must be learned! Progress in this area requires commitment to a daily growth plan. This is how a skillful leader is developed daily. Skillful leaders feed on books, digital media, and other resources that will help grow skills in such areas as communication, relationships, motivation, positivity, team building, problem solving, and conflict resolution.

Then, if a leader wants to experience maximum impact for the Kingdom, they will endeavor to blend godly character and leadership skills into a focused, strategic plan for ministry. For instance, they may discover their God-given purpose or refuse to dabble in petty activities. Or a husband and wife in leadership will give themselves to those activities that strategically maximize their impact. Each learns to multiply their influence by training, equipping, and developing other leaders. Strategic formation becomes the priority; compound results are the outcome.

The Story of Erwin Patricio

In his book, Big Picture People, Doug Carter (Senior Vice President of EQUIP) shares the story of Erwin Patricio. His story illustrates the key components of growth toward mature leadership. The following is an excerpt from Carter’s book.

Erwin Patricio was a member of the Tohono O’odham tribe (also known as the Papago tribe), a group of about 10,000 Native Americans who live on a reservation that sprawls across the southern Arizona desert.

By age 30, Erwin had earned the name “the reservation drunkard.” He was a sixth grade dropout who had become an alcoholic and a failure at everything he had touched in life. But one hot afternoon a missionary visited his home. Erwin treated the missionary with utmost contempt, even threatening to kill the missionary if he returned to Erwin’s adobe hut. But, even in the face of threats upon his life, the missionary persisted. Soon Erwin stumbled into a little Indian church and gave his heart to Christ. Erwin was wonderfully transformed by the power of the gospel. Old things passed away and all became new! Within a few weeks he became convinced that God was calling him to preach the gospel.

He applied for admission into a very fine Bible college in New Mexico. The registrar at the Bible college mistakenly overlooked the fact that Erwin had not attended high school. You will recall that my Indian friend had quit school in the sixth grade! She admitted him to the freshman class. Four years later he graduated as the valedictorian of his class, and shortly after he completed college, I heard Erwin preach. The first time I heard his personal testimony I felt very strongly that the Holy Spirit was directing me to ask him to prayerfully consider becoming the chaplain at the school I directed for Native American youth. Almost immediately he accepted the challenge and moved his family to the campus of the Southwest Indian School.

He and I spent hours in prayer and in discussions of ministry issues. I developed a special love and appreciation for this deeply spiritual man. I admired his devotion to Christ, his faithfulness to the Word of God and to prayer, his burden for the lost, and his passion to make a difference. He was very Christlike in character, yet he often failed when placed in a leadership role. As I observed his struggles, I was reminded that a sterling character and deep love for the Lord are not enough to make one effective in ministry.

After several conversations with Erwin, I committed myself to a mentoring role in his life. We did ministry side by side. What an incredible journey we took together! Years later, Rev. Kevin Myers, a dear friend and pastor in the Atlanta area, shared with me a leadership development model that perfectly described my journey with Erwin. Pastor Kevin talked about a baseball diamond with the bases as three key steps in the process of growth as a leader: calling as home plate, character as first base, community as second base, and competence as third base.

Erwin was a giant at first base, but had he remained at first base his impact for Christ’s kingdom would have been very minimal. I did my very best to help him work on second base issues. He worked hard to improve his communications skills and his people skills. He developed excellent vision casting, team-building, and conflict resolution skills. Though as his skills improved, a new problem arose. He was pulled in a thousand directions. Soon he was dabbling in countless projects and ministries. Rather than doing a few things exceedingly well and leaving a mark, he was dabbling in 40 things and leaving a blur.

I began to discuss third base issues with Erwin. He needed to move his leadership to third base. Third base is where a leader gains perspective, learns to focus, establishes priorities, shifts from activity to accomplishment, and moves from success to significance by a God-inspired strategy to make a maximum impact for Christ and His kingdom. He has to be more than a doer— he has to become a developer of others. (Carter 2000, 33–34)

These five truths should be embraced by every minister if they are to keep growing to be like Christ:

1. We all experience tests at each stage of our growth.

2. Our goal should be to pass every test.

3. Testing always precedes promotion.

4. Self-promotion and human promotion cannot replace divine promotion.

5. Just as a product is never used until it is tested, so will it be with each of us.

**A:** Poverty—the test reveals our failure to trust God and act obediently. Plateau—the test reveals we are not growing but have settled for mediocrity. Progress—the test reveals we have grown as a leader and are responding with increasing maturity.

**Q:** What is the difference between spiritual formation and skill formation?

**A:** Spiritual formation is the interior development of the leader’s character and righteousness, while skill formation focuses on their competence in the various tasks.

**Q:** Explain the three “bases” of a leader’s development as described in the story of Erwin Patricio.

**A:** ( Calling is home plate.) First base is character development. At this base the leader’s internal life is the focus and becomes the foundation for all that follows. Here the leader learns to maximize their success by focusing on the area of their giftings and the mentoring relationships they must establish to develop others.

### The Ten Tests of Leadership Development

#### 4.3.1 — Identify and describe the ten tests that can develop leaders.

Our parents’ words were true: Anything worth having is worth working for. Obviously the leadership preparation process will be hard work, and it will be slow and painful at times. Listed here are ten categories of tests we may be put through on the journey to becoming effective servant-leaders (Maxwell 2003b, 5–7). If we know the purpose of each test and its biblical basis, then we can more easily weather the discomfort. God is setting in motion His plan. Our tests do not take Him by surprise; He is in control of them.

1. Faith Test. This is a test in small things that comes to determine our faithfulness and potential for greater opportunities.

Be very careful, then, how you live―not as unwise but as wise, making the most of every opportunity, because the days are evil. (Ephesians 5:15–16)

Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much. (Luke 16:10)

2. Motivation Test. This test comes to the one who is doing what is right, to examine why they are doing the tasks.

“Does Job fear God for nothing?” Satan replied. “Have you not put a hedge around him and his household and everything he has? You have blessed the work of his hands, so that his flocks and herds are spread throughout the land. But stretch out your hand and strike everything he has, and he will surely curse you to your face.” (Job 1:9–11)

When you pray, do not be like the hypocrites, for they love to pray standing in the synagogues and on the street corners to be seen by men. I tell you the truth, they have received their reward in full. But when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you. (Matthew 6:5–6)

3. Stewardship Test. This test proves how strategically and generously we handle the resources we control.

He told them this parable: “The ground of a certain rich man produced a good crop. He thought to himself, “What shall I do? I have no place to store my crops.” Then he said, ‘This is what I’ll do. I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods. And I’ll say to myself, “You have plenty of good things laid up for many years. Take life easy; eat, drink and be merry!’”

But God said to him: “You fool! This very night your life will be demanded from you. Then who will get what you have prepared for yourself?” This is how it will be with anyone who stores up things for himself but is not rich toward God. (Luke 12:16–21)

His master replied, “Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Come and share your master’s happiness!” (Matthew 25:21)

4. Wilderness Test. This test shows our spiritual dryness and reveals our willingness to change and grow.

He led you through the vast and dreadful desert, that thirsty and waterless land, with its venomous snakes and scorpions. He brought you water out of hard rock. He gave you manna to eat in the desert, something your fathers had never known, to humble and to test you so that in the end it might go well with you. (Deuteronomy 8:15–16)

5. Credibility Test. This test reveals the depth of our integrity, to see if we will compromise under pressure.

The Lord said to Samuel, “Do not consider his appearance or his height, for I have rejected him. The Lord does not look at the things man looks at. Man looks at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart.” (1 Samuel 16:7)

When Peter came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he was clearly in the wrong. Before certain men came from James, he used to eat with the Gentiles. But when they arrived, he began to draw back and separate himself from the Gentiles because he was afraid of those who belonged to the circumcision group. The other Jews joined him in his hypocrisy, so that by their hypocrisy even Barnabas was led astray. (Galatians 2:11–13)

6. Authority Test. This test comes to expose our attitude and willing submission toward God-given authority.

The men said, “This is the day the Lord spoke of when he said to you, ‘I will give your enemy into your hands for you to deal with as you wish’” [meaning Saul as he slept soundly in a cave]. . . . He said to his men, “The Lord forbid that I should do such a thing to my master, the Lord’s anointed, or lift my hand against him; for he is the anointed of the Lord.” With these words David rebuked his men and did not allow them to attack Saul. (1 Samuel 24:4, 6–7)

As for those who seemed to be important—whatever they were makes no difference to me; God does not judge by external appearance—those men added nothing to my message. On the contrary, they saw that I had been entrusted with the task of preaching the gospel to the Gentiles, just as Peter had been to the Jews. (Galatians 2:6–7)

7. Forgiveness Test. This test comes to show our willingness to forgive others for offenses.

Make every effort to live in peace with all men and to be holy; without holiness no one will see the Lord. See to it that no one misses the grace of God and that no bitter root grows up to cause trouble and defile many. (Hebrews 12:14–15)

When you stand praying, if you hold anything against anyone, forgive him, so that your Father in heaven may forgive you your sins. (Mark 11:25–26)

8. Warfare Test. This test exposes our ability to fight the good fight of faith and to overcome every adversity we face.

God said, “If they face war, they might change their minds and return to Egypt.” (Exodus 13:17)

If you have raced with men on foot and they have worn you out, how can you compete with the horses? If you stumble in safe country, how will you manage in the thickets by the Jordan? (Jeremiah 12:5)

9. Time Test. This test reveals the quality of your response to God-given opportunities and your commitment to faithful service until the task is completed.

If you remain silent at this time, relief and deliverance for the Jews will arise from another place, but you and your father’s family will perish. And who knows but that you have come to royal position for such a time as this? (Esther 4:14)

Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up. (Galatians 6:9)

Do not let this Book of Law depart from your mouth; meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do everything written in it. (Joshua 1:8)

10. Lordship Test. This test usually occurs in one of your areas of strength.

You find it difficult to trust God because you can rely on your own ability. It reveals your heart response to whom or what has the final authority in your life. It is clear in the passage below that Jesus’ command made no sense to Peter, a skilled fisherman. Nevertheless, he obeyed. His own knowledge and ability was surrendered to the authority of Jesus, the Lord of his life.

When He had finished speaking, he said to Simon, “Put out into deep water, and let down the nets for a catch.” Simon answered, “Master, we’ve worked hard all night and haven’t caught anything. But because you say so, I will let down the nets.” When they had done so, they caught such a large number of fish that their nets began to break. So they signaled their partners in the other boat to come and help them, and they came and filled both boats so full that they began to sink. (Luke 5:4–7)

Do not let these tests discourage you from fulfilling God’s leadership plans for your life. Yes, your deepest emotions will be activated by these tests, but pray, persevere, and celebrate your victories.

**Q:** Why might the test of small things be one of the most difficult tests for a leader?

**A:** Leaders feel compelled to accomplish big things. Often a leader has more of a big picture mentality and may lack patience with smaller things. Leaders often fail to realize that the smaller assignments lead to trust with larger assignments.

**Q:** Explain the stewardship test and how your own stewardship has been tested.

**A:** Stewardship tests reveal our sense of ownership and responsibility. A steward is one who manages resources that are owned by another. Being faithful with what God has entrusted to us is essential. God may test stewardship by taking away what is rightfully His or by giving us more to see how we will manage it. There will also be a personal response.

**Q:** What is the forgiveness test, and when have you experienced this test?

**A:** The forgiveness test determines how easily we are offended and how quickly we are willing to forgive. Clearly this test will require that we be offended in some way, thus experiencing something negative. There will also be a personal response.

**Q:** What is the warfare test and why is the warfare test necessary?

**A:** The warfare test provides us with the opportunity to publicly demonstrate what we have developed in private. We must face the battle in order to prove ourselves. In the warfare test, both our dependence on God and our faith in Him are often tested.

## 5 - The Mission of the Church

### Seeking God’s Direction

#### 5.1.1 — Describe the importance of seeking God’s direction rather than depending on personal ambition.

“Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint.” (Proverbs 29:18)

Vision is not self-created but God-revealed. Leaders must seek His guidance to know where to go and how to lead His people.

True vision in leadership does not come from personal ambition, clever strategies, or human ingenuity alone. Biblical leadership is marked by dependence on God’s direction. Proverbs 29:18 reminds us that without God’s revelation, leaders may guide people into confusion, stagnation, or even destruction.

Moses, Joshua, Nehemiah, and Paul all modeled this truth. None of them led by their own ideas; they sought God’s will first and then obeyed what He revealed. Moses did not launch out with his own plan to free Israel. He encountered God in the wilderness, received His vision, and followed His instructions step by step (Exodus 3–4). Nehemiah wept, prayed, and fasted before building the wall (Nehemiah 1). Paul received his mission through divine encounter and the Spirit’s direction (Acts 9; 16:6–10). In each case, the vision to move forward was not self-generated but heaven-sent.

As Christian leaders, we must resist the temptation to rely solely on our experiences, talents, or preferences. Instead, we are called to posture ourselves in humility, seeking God’s presence through prayer, fasting, and His Word. When we align with His heart, He reveals the direction we need. Vision is not simply about what we want to accomplish but about what God desires to accomplish through us.

The Shepherd and the Sheep (John 10:3–4)

Jesus says His sheep recognize His voice and follow Him. Just as sheep cannot set their own course but depend on their shepherd’s direction, leaders are not called to create their own vision but to follow God’s revealed plan. Without His leading, we risk scattering the flock.

Historical Figures

William Carey— The Father of Modern Missions

Carey, a cobbler in England, felt a stirring for global missions. Rather than rushing forward, he sought God’s guidance through Scripture, prayer, and study. Out of that dependence came the vision that birthed the modern missionary movement. His famous motto: “Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God.” He didn’t invent the vision; he aligned with God’s heart for the nations.

George Müller— The Orphanages in Bristol

Müller never set out to build orphanages as a personal ambition. He prayed for God’s guidance to show the world He could still answer prayer. Each expansion of his ministry came only after seasons of prayer and waiting for God’s provision. His vision was God-given, not man-made.

Martin Luther— Reformation Leadership

Luther did not wake up with a personal plan to split from the church. His vision emerged from deep time in God’s Word, wrestling with the truth of justification by faith. His reliance on Scripture ensured his vision aligned with God’s revelation rather than human opinion.

Modern Leadership

Chick-Fil-A and Truett Cathy’s Leadership

Truett Cathy’s vision for his business was not just financial success but glorifying God through service. He sought God’s guidance for big decisions, including the radical choice to close on Sundays. That decision was not a clever business strategy—it was made in obedience to God’s leading. Over time, it became a hallmark of the company’s culture and a testimony of vision rooted in God’s direction.

A compass always points north, no matter the landscape. Leaders without God’s direction are like travelers wandering with no compass. When God reveals His vision, it gives clarity, orientation, and purpose—even if the terrain is unfamiliar.

Practical Application

Spend time in prayer and fasting when seeking vision.

Vision from God is not discovered through human strategy but through spiritual intimacy. Prayer and fasting position your heart to hear clearly from the Holy Spirit, quieting distractions and aligning your desires with God’s will. Before acting or planning, take intentional time to seek God’s presence and listen for His direction.

Test vision against God’s Word to ensure alignment.

Every divine vision will always be consistent with Scripture. God never contradicts His Word. As you sense direction or clarity, compare it with biblical principles to confirm its validity. Ask: Does this reflect God’s character? Does it glorify Christ? Is it rooted in love, humility, and service? Scripture serves as both the foundation and the filter for true vision.

Remain flexible, allowing God to refine or redirect the vision.

God often reveals vision progressively—one step at a time. As you move forward in obedience, be willing to adjust when He brings new insight or circumstances change. Holding the vision with open hands demonstrates trust in God’s sovereignty. Flexibility is not a lack of faith; it is faith in motion—allowing God to shape the outcome according to His perfect plan.

Reflection Questions

1. Do I tend to create my own vision or seek God’s?

2. What practices help me discern God’s direction?

3. Where might God be inviting me to realign my vision with His?

**Q:** Why is true vision in leadership considered God-revealed rather than self-created?

**A:** True vision is God-revealed because leaders must seek His guidance to know where to go and how to lead. Human ambition, clever strategies, or personal ideas alone are not sufficient; without God’s revelation, leaders risk guiding people into confusion, stagnation, or destruction. Biblical examples include Moses, Nehemiah, and Paul, who all sought God’s direction before acting.

**Q:** Give two practical ways Christian leaders can seek God’s direction when pursuing vision.

**A:** Spend time in prayer and fasting to align with God’s heart, test the vision against Scripture to ensure it aligns with God’s Word, and remain flexible for His refinement or redirection.

### Aligning Vision with God’s Mission

#### 5.2.1 — Explain the importance of basing the church’s vision on the Great Commission and the Great Commandment.

“Go and make disciples of all nations.” (Matthew 28:19)

All Christian vision must serve God’s mission: making disciples and advancing His kingdom. Anything outside this misses the heart of God.

A vision that does not align with the Great Commission risks diverting focus from God’s primary purpose. It is essential for leaders to ensure that their vision reflects this mission.

Evaluate Every Vision Against the Great Commission and Great Commandment.

A Christian leader’s vision must be measured against God’s ultimate purposes: making disciples of all nations (Matthew 28:19–20) and loving God and others (Matthew 22:37–39). If a vision does not contribute to these, it risks being misdirected, no matter how appealing or ambitious it may seem. Evaluating your vision through these lenses ensures it aligns with eternal priorities rather than temporary success.

The Great Commission as a Benchmark

The Great Commission is a universal call to disciple making, encompassing evangelism, teaching, mentoring, and equipping believers. Leaders should ask themselves:

Does this vision intentionally help people grow in Christ?

Does it equip believers to share their faith and live obediently?

Does it extend beyond personal or local gain to reach the wider community or even nations?

Example: A church plans to launch a new community outreach program. If the program simply provides meals but never engages participants spiritually, it might meet a social need but does not fully serve the Great Commission. To align with God’s purposes, the program could include Bible teaching, mentorship, or discipleship opportunities, ensuring it helps people encounter Christ, not just address physical needs.

The Great Commandment as a Lens

The Great Commandment emphasizes love for God and others. Vision evaluation should include relational and spiritual impact:

Does this vision foster love for God through worship, obedience, and devotion?

Does it cultivate genuine love and care for people, not just efficiency or numbers?

Does it create environments where people experience God’s love and learn to extend it to others?

Example: A ministry leader dreams of launching an ambitious media campaign. Before moving forward, they pause to ask: “Will this serve God and bless others, or is it primarily a platform to increase my personal influence?” By using the Great Commandment as a filter, the vision is refined to ensure it serves God’s people, not personal ambition.

Practical Steps to Evaluate Vision

Every vision should be a response to God’s mission, not personal preference. By evaluating visions against the Great Commission and Great Commandment, leaders ensure their efforts glorify God, bless others, and produce lasting Kingdom impact. Consider the following thoughts to evaluate your vision.

Prayerful Reflection: Regularly seek God’s guidance to discern whether your vision aligns with His purposes.

Scripture Check: Compare the vision’s outcomes with biblical priorities (disciple making, worship, love).

Mentorship and Accountability: Discuss the vision with trusted leaders or mentors who value Kingdom impact over personal success.

Outcome Focused: Ask: “Will this lead to transformed lives, stronger faith, and more disciples, or just visibility and growth metrics?”

A vision that passes this test is not only spiritually sound—it has eternal significance.

Avoid Visions Centered on Personal Fame, Comfort, or Ambition

A vision driven primarily by personal gain—whether it is recognition, wealth, or influence—can subtly shift a leader’s focus from God’s mission to self-interest. This misalignment often results in decisions that prioritize short-term success, popularity, or organizational expansion over the spiritual health and growth of people. In practical terms, leaders may chase impressive attendance numbers, social media engagement, or financial milestones, believing these metrics reflect success, while the deeper work of disciple making is neglected.

The Metric-Obsessed Church

Imagine a church whose leadership is consumed with seeing numbers rise—attendance, online viewers, or donations. Programs are designed for maximum appeal, with flashy events and entertainment that attract crowds. Yet small groups, mentoring, and intentional discipleship programs are underfunded or ignored. The church grows in size, but members remain spiritually immature, relationships are shallow, and few are equipped to share the gospel outside the church walls. Here, the vision may look impressive on paper, but it fails to fulfill the Great Commission which is about making disciples, not just gathering spectators.

Personal Ambition in Ministry

Consider a pastor or ministry leader who desires recognition as a dynamic leader. Decisions are made to boost visibility—speaking at conferences, publishing books, or hosting high-profile events—but little attention is given to coaching other leaders, mentoring volunteers, or walking alongside believers in their spiritual journey. Over time, the ministry becomes more about the leader’s platform than God’s mission, creating a culture of self-promotion rather than service.

True Christian vision is measured not by how impressive it looks but by how effectively it advances God’s mission—making disciples and glorifying Him. Personal gain is not inherently wrong, but when it becomes the driving force, it diverts energy and resources away from disciple making, leaving the vision spiritually hollow.

Clarify How Your Vision Practically Contributes to Disciple Making

A Christian leader’s vision is only as strong as its ability to produce tangible disciple-making outcomes. A vision without practical steps risks becoming abstract, inspiring in theory but ineffective in reality. Clarifying how your vision directly contributes to disciple making helps ensure that every program, initiative, or decision advances God’s mission.

Translate Vision into Actionable Steps

A clear vision should answer the question: “How will this help people follow Christ more closely?”

Practical steps might include:

Structured Small Groups: Develop small, intentional communities where believers study Scripture, share life, and encourage accountability.

Mentorship Programs: Pair experienced believers with new converts to guide them through spiritual growth, prayer, and evangelism. This creates multiplication where disciples are equipped to make more disciples.

Training and Workshops: Offer courses or workshops on evangelism, Bible literacy, leadership, or service so believers are empowered to serve effectively in their communities and beyond.

Make the Path Visible

A vision that is too vague leaves people unsure how to participate. By clarifying practical steps, leaders give members a clear road map for action:

A church’s vision is “to develop lifelong followers of Christ.” To make this practical, leaders might outline a three-year road map:

Year 1— Focus on foundational discipleship: Bible study, prayer, and spiritual habits.

Year 2— Leadership development: equipping members to mentor others.

Year 3— Outreach and multiplication: sending disciples to start new groups or serve in mission fields.

This type of vision casting shows believers exactly how their involvement contributes to the vision.

Measure Progress in Disciple Making, Not Just Numbers

Traditional metrics like attendance or donations are easy to track but do not capture spiritual growth. Instead, measure the number of new believers being discipled and the number of believers actively mentoring others. Are participants showing measurable growth in obedience, prayer, and love for God and others?

Illustration: A church launches a youth mentoring program. Instead of counting only how many youth attend, leaders track how many engage in one-on-one discipleship, participate in service projects, or share their faith. These practical indicators show the vision is truly advancing the Great Commission.

A vision becomes alive and effective only when it translates into practical, actionable steps that produce disciples who grow in faith and go on to disciple others. Clarifying this connection ensures the vision is not just inspirational but operational, tangible, and aligned with God’s mission.

Aligning Personal Vision with God’s Mission

Leaders should regularly reflect on their personal and organizational vision, ensuring it aligns with the Great Commission. This alignment ensures that efforts are directed towards God’s purpose, fostering a community committed to making disciples and advancing His kingdom.

Reflection Questions

1. How does my current vision align with the Great Commission?

2. Are there parts of my vision that are more about me than about God?

3. How can I reframe my leadership goals to serve God’s mission more directly?

**Q:** Why is it essential for Christian leaders to evaluate their vision against the Great Commission and Great Commandment?

**A:** Evaluating vision against the Great Commission and Great Commandment ensures the vision aligns with God’s ultimate purposes—making disciples of all nations and loving God and others. This prevents the vision from being misdirected by personal ambition or temporary success and ensures it produces eternal, Kingdom-focused impact.

**Q:** What practical steps can leaders take to ensure their vision effectively contributes to disciple making?

**A:** Translate the vision into actionable steps, such as structured small groups, mentorship programs, and training workshops. Make the path visible with clear road maps showing how members can participate. Measure progress by disciple-making outcomes (e.g., new believers discipled, mentoring relationships, spiritual growth) rather than just attendance or financial metrics.

#### 5.2.2 — Explain and demonstrate ability to apply the four tests of a great mission.

Long-Term Direction and Stability for a Church

Often pastors take churches on a spiritual and emotional roller coaster. The result is that churches suffer an identity crisis. No matter who the pastor is or what changes are made reflecting the vision of the church, the mission should remain the same. It provides stability for a local church so that they understand what they are to do as a body of Christ.

A Church’s Biblical Philosophy

The mission of the church is not up for vote or discussion; it must reflect what the Word of God says the church is to be. In fact, we do not get to decide the mission. In Chapter 6 we will discuss the vision of the church, which is different for every church. However, essentially, the mission is the same for every Christian church. A biblical mission statement says that the ministry is grounded in the written Word of God and exists to fulfill what the Scripture teaches the local church is to be and do.

The Heart of Your Ministry

The mission should be the pulse of the church. Everything we do must be to fulfill the mission. In fact the church should not be involved in doing anything that does not advance the mission of the church.

Four Tests of a Great Mission

Use these four questions to test your mission statement for appropriateness and effectiveness, and then revise it if necessary.

1. Can your congregation quote it?

A mission statement is not a slogan, although slogans can be effective in advertising and political campaigns. The mission must be a clearly articulated statement that is able to be understood by an elementary-age child in the church. If it needs to be explained after it is stated, then it needs revision.

2. Do your ministries line up with it?

All ministries of a church must in some way help to fulfill the mission. It is easy to place a lot of activities on a church calendar and never fulfill the mission of a church. That is why successful churches fill their schedule with meaningful events and processes that help them to fulfill the mission.

3. Does the Bible affirm it?

The ultimate question for every mission statement should be this: Is it biblical? It must clearly state what the Scripture teaches regarding why the church exists. This is probably the most important question that must be answered.

4. Does it matter to your church?

### Wisdom from Above

#### 6.1.1 — Identify the biblical foundation of wise decision-making and why leaders must rely on God’s wisdom rather than human understanding.

“Give your servant a discerning heart to govern your people and to distinguish between right and wrong.” (1 Kings 3:9)

True leadership requires more than strategy, knowledge, or charisma—it requires wisdom from above. Human reasoning may solve problems temporarily, but it cannot fully grasp the complexities of God’s purposes. Spiritual leaders are called to seek the Lord’s wisdom which produces purity of heart, unity among people, and decisions that bear lasting fruit for God’s kingdom. When leaders walk in God’s wisdom, they not only guide people effectively but also reflect Christlike character in every decision.

Heavenly wisdom contrasts sharply with worldly wisdom, which is often driven by ambition, pride, or fear. The fruit of worldly wisdom is division, confusion, and self-centered outcomes. In contrast, wisdom from God always produces harmony, mercy, and spiritual fruit that glorifies Christ.

When Solomon became king, he faced the overwhelming responsibility of leading God’s people. Instead of asking for wealth, military strength, or long life, Solomon prayed, “Give your servant a discerning heart to govern your people and to distinguish between right and wrong” (1 Kings 3:9). God was pleased with this request and granted him wisdom beyond measure.

One of the first demonstrations of this wisdom came when two women brought a child before him, each claiming to be the mother. Solomon’s wise ruling revealed the true mother, astonishing the nation. This story highlights that godly leaders should first seek wisdom from above because it equips them to make decisions that bring justice, peace, and clarity.

Wisdom Is God’s Gift, Not Human Achievement

James reminds us that anyone who lacks wisdom can simply ask God, and He gives generously. Leadership is filled with crossroads and dilemmas, and human intellect often reaches its limits. God’s wisdom is not earned through striving but received in humble dependence on Him.

The Character of Wisdom Reveals Its Source

James 3:17 outlines qualities that distinguish heavenly wisdom from worldly cleverness. Pure motives, peace-seeking, and sincerity all show that a leader is being guided by God rather than selfish ambition. Leaders must test their decisions by these qualities to ensure they are acting in alignment with the Spirit of God.

Wisdom Produces Righteous Outcomes

Earthly wisdom often produces rivalry, pride, or short-term success, but wisdom from above results in long-term peace, justice, and righteousness. It not only solves problems; it builds people, strengthens faith, and glorifies God.

Practical Application

Begin Every Decision with Prayer

Before making strategic plans, hiring staff, launching ministries, or addressing conflicts, leaders must first seek God in prayer. This act acknowledges dependence on Him and creates space for the Holy Spirit to provide clarity and direction. Prayer not only invites wisdom but also calms anxious hearts, allowing decisions to be made with peace rather than pressure.

Evaluate Options Against God’s Standard of Wisdom

James 3:17 provides a practical checklist for discernment: Is this decision pure in motive? Will it promote peace or fuel division? Does it reflect consideration and humility? Will it bear good fruit? Is it impartial and free from favoritism? Is it sincere and transparent? By weighing options against these qualities, leaders can distinguish between God’s wisdom and worldly impulses.

Resist the Urgency of Pressure

Many poor decisions are made under the tyranny of urgency. Leaders are often pushed to act quickly to meet demands, appease people, or avoid discomfort. However, wisdom from above requires patience and trust. Waiting on the Lord—whether through prayer, counsel, or further reflection—often brings clarity that hurried choices miss. A leader grounded in heavenly wisdom is not swayed by pressure but guided by peace.

Surround Yourself with Godly Counsel

Proverbs 15:22 teaches, “Plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed.” God often imparts wisdom through the voices of Spirit-filled advisors, mentors, or team members. Wise leaders invite godly counsel, listen with humility, and test what they hear against Scripture.

Model Wisdom in Action

A leader’s decisions set the tone for the organization or ministry. When followers see their leader act with purity, peace, and humility, they learn to trust not only the leader but also the God the leader serves. Wisdom is both taught and caught—leaders who consistently model it will inspire others to pursue it.

Wisdom is an essential quality for effective Christian leadership, but it cannot be gained merely through experience, intellect, or clever strategy. Leaders are called to seek this wisdom in prayer, trusting that God will provide clear direction when human reasoning falls short. Unlike worldly wisdom, which often leads to pride, rivalry, or short-term gain, heavenly wisdom reflects God’s character and produces lasting fruit for His kingdom.

### Balancing Faith and Practicality

#### 6.3.1 — Describe how leaders must cultivate the ability to balance bold faith with wise stewardship.

“In his heart a man plans his course, but the Lord determines his steps.” (Proverbs 16:9)

Leaders must cultivate the ability to balance bold faith with wise stewardship. Faith does not mean acting impulsively or ignoring potential consequences; it means trusting God’s guidance even when the outcome is uncertain. At the same time, practicality does not indicate a lack of trust in God—it reflects a commitment to responsible action and careful planning. Both qualities are essential for godly decision making because leadership often requires navigating complex situations where resources, timing, and human dynamics matter.

True leadership is the ability to discern when to take courageous steps that require reliance on God and when to apply prudence to ensure those steps are effective. Bold faith moves the vision forward, inspiring others to trust in God’s provision and step into opportunities they might otherwise avoid. Practicality ensures that faith is grounded in tangible action, avoiding unnecessary risk or mismanagement. A leader who neglects either aspect risks failure—faith without planning can become presumption, and planning without faith can become stagnation.

In practice, this balance calls for intentional reflection, prayer, and seeking counsel. Leaders must weigh God’s promises against the realities of their context, using wisdom to allocate resources, prepare for challenges, and anticipate outcomes while remaining flexible to God’s direction. The integration of faith and practicality transforms ordinary decisions into acts of godly stewardship, allowing leaders to act confidently, responsibly, and effectively.

Joseph in Egypt (Genesis 41)

Joseph interpreted Pharaoh’s dreams through God’s wisdom and then took practical steps to store grain during the seven years of plenty. His faith in God’s plan guided his actions, but his strategic foresight prevented famine. Leaders must recognize that God often calls them to act practically in alignment with divine revelation.

David Facing Goliath (1 Samuel 17)

David displayed remarkable faith in God when confronting Goliath, yet he also chose five smooth stones and a sling, tools suited for the task. Faith inspired courage, and practical preparation ensured effectiveness. Bold faith and careful planning are not opposed—they complement one another in godly leadership.

Paul and the Missionary Journeys (Acts 16–20)

Paul relied on the Holy Spirit’s guidance to choose where to minister, but he also organized finances, travel arrangements, and team logistics. His ministry shows that effective leaders trust God while using practical wisdom to accomplish their mission.

Seek God in prayer, then map out the steps needed to achieve your goals, leaving room for divine adjustment.

Avoid extremes.

Presumption without planning may lead to failure, while planning without prayer can result in misaligned priorities.

Regularly evaluate motivations.

Ask yourself if faith or fear is driving your choices. Leaders guided by fear may delay action, while leaders driven by reckless faith may overlook necessary details.

Combine courage with preparation.

Bold actions should be informed by realistic assessment of resources, risks, and timing.

Faith without preparation can falter, and planning without faith can stagnate. Effective leaders, like Nehemiah or modern project leaders, step forward boldly under God’s direction while implementing practical strategies that honor His provision and protect their people and resources. True leadership is not a choice between faith and practicality—it is the art of integrating both.

## 7 - A Biblical Model of Ministry

### Stewarding God’s Resources

#### 7.1.1 — Explain the importance of faithfully stewarding what God entrusts to us.

“Now it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful.” (1 Corinthians 4:2)

Christian leadership is not about ownership but about stewardship. The world often measures leadership by power, position, or possessions, but in the kingdom of God, leadership is measured by faithfulness. Leaders are entrusted with resources, influence, and people—not for personal gain but for God’s purposes.

This trust is sacred, because what leaders manage ultimately belongs to the Lord. The church, the gifts of the Spirit, the finances, and even the opportunities of ministry are not ours to control but God’s to direct. Leaders are temporary caretakers of eternal treasures.

The apostle Paul reminds us that “it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful” (1 Corinthians 4:2). Faithfulness, not fame or results, is the true standard by which God evaluates His leaders. While achievements and accomplishments may earn recognition from people, accountability to God is the ultimate measure of leadership success.

Leadership is less about building one’s own legacy and more about stewarding God’s kingdom with humility, integrity, and care. At the end of the day, leaders will not give an account for how much they acquired but for how faithfully they managed what God placed in their hands.

Joseph in Egypt (Genesis 39–41)

Joseph’s life is one of the clearest biblical models of stewardship. Though he began as a slave in Potiphar’s house, Joseph managed his master’s household so faithfully that Potiphar entrusted him with everything he owned. Even when falsely accused and imprisoned, Joseph continued to steward his responsibilities with excellence until he was elevated to oversee the prison itself. Eventually, Pharaoh placed him in charge of the entire land of Egypt, giving him authority over the nation’s grain during seven years of abundance and seven years of famine. Joseph never treated his position or resources as his own; instead, he recognized them as trusts from God.

Joseph’s integrity, diligence, and God-centered perspective not only preserved Egypt but also saved surrounding nations—including his own family—from starvation. Joseph teaches leaders that stewardship is about serving faithfully in every season, whether small beginnings or great influence, always remembering that resources are managed for God’s purposes, not personal advancement.

Modern Parallel

Joseph’s story mirrors the role of a leader today who manages a company or organization with integrity, even under pressure. For instance, a CEO who resists the temptation to cut ethical corners during a financial crisis but instead manages resources wisely and transparently often not only saves the company but also protects employees and communities. Like Joseph, such leaders recognize that their influence is entrusted, not owned, and they use it to bless others beyond themselves.

The Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25:14–30)

Jesus’ parable highlights the responsibility of stewardship in God’s kingdom. Three servants were given different amounts of their master’s wealth to manage. Two invested wisely and produced increase, while one buried his talent out of fear and laziness. The master’s response shows that God rewards faithfulness, not comparison. The one who hid his talent was not condemned for losing money but for failing to act on what had been entrusted to him.

Leaders today must realize that God is less concerned with how much they have been given and more with how they use it. Whether large or small, every opportunity, relationship, and resource is meant to be cultivated and multiplied for God’s glory. The parable also reveals the seriousness of wasted stewardship—doing nothing with what God has entrusted is itself an act of unfaithfulness. Leaders who serve with courage, creativity, and accountability hear the words every steward longs for: “Well done, good and faithful servant.”

Modern Parallel

This parable applies directly to leaders who maximize—or waste—their opportunities. For example, a church leader who uses limited resources to launch community programs that impact lives is stewarding talents well. Conversely, a leader who has abundant resources but fails to act boldly, hiding behind excuses or fear, reflects the unfaithful servant. The principle remains: God is not impressed with how much you start with but how faithfully you invest what you have been given.

Moses at Meribah (Numbers 20)

Moses provides a sobering example of the weight of stewardship. When Israel once again complained of thirst in the wilderness, God instructed Moses to speak to a rock so water would flow. Instead, Moses, frustrated with the people, struck the rock twice with his staff. Though water still came forth, Moses had failed to honor God’s specific command. His actions misrepresented God’s holiness before the people, and as a result, he was forbidden from entering the Promised Land.

This account reminds leaders that stewardship is not just about results but about obedience. Even if outcomes appear successful, disobedience dishonors God and carries consequences. Moses’ failure at Meribah teaches that leaders must steward not only resources but also God’s instructions with accuracy and reverence. Leadership is never about bending God’s word to our will—it is about faithfully representing His authority and character.

Modern Parallel

Many leaders today fall into the same trap Moses did—allowing frustration or pressure to lead them into compromise. A ministry leader might deliver a powerful program but cut ethical or spiritual corners to get results, or a business leader might hit financial targets but do so in ways that misrepresent the organization’s values. Like Moses, they may still “produce results,” but unfaithful methods dishonor God and erode trust. This example warns leaders that stewardship is not measured only by outcomes but by obedience and faithfulness to God’s ways.

Practical Application

Treat every resource as belonging to the Lord.

Faithful stewardship begins with perspective. When leaders view resources as “theirs,” pride, control, or selfishness easily take root. But when we shift our mindset to see everything—finances, people, opportunities, and even our own influence—as belonging to God, it changes the way we lead. Money is not just for spending but for investing in kingdom purposes. Time is not ours to waste but a gift to be used wisely. People under our care are not tools for personal ambition but souls entrusted to us for growth, guidance, and protection.

Example: A pastor who sees the congregation as “his church” may lead in a way that builds his personal brand. But one who sees it as God’s church will make decisions with humility, accountability, and a focus on the mission of Christ rather than personal recognition. Leaders who carry this perspective free themselves from entitlement and carry instead a spirit of gratitude and responsibility.

Avoid waste and misuse of what God has entrusted.

Wasting resources—whether money, time, energy, or people’s gifts—is a sign of poor stewardship. Leaders must learn to recognize not just financial misuse but also the subtle ways waste creeps in: unproductive meetings that drain energy, unused skills in team members who could be empowered, or funds spent on things that do not advance God’s purposes. Waste dishonors God because it communicates carelessness toward His blessings. Misuse, on the other hand, often comes when leaders exploit resources for selfish ends or short-term gain which can cause lasting damage to both mission and trust.

Example: In business, a leader who invests company profits into personal luxuries rather than employee development or mission growth is guilty of misuse. In ministry, a church that pours money into unnecessary extravagance while neglecting community needs is practicing wasteful stewardship. By contrast, leaders who prayerfully and wisely allocate resources show honor to God by using what He has entrusted with care and purpose.

Develop systems that honor stewardship and accountability.

Good intentions are not enough—leaders must create structures that safeguard stewardship. Systems for accountability in finances, decision making, and people management ensure that resources are not only well handled but transparently so. This prevents corruption, protects leaders from temptation, and builds trust among followers. Accountability is not a sign of weakness or lack of trust; it is a safeguard of wisdom. Even Moses had seventy elders to help him judge Israel, and the apostles in Acts appointed deacons to oversee the daily distribution of food. God’s design for leadership has always included shared responsibility and accountability.

Example: A church that uses multiple signatories for financial transactions, publishes transparent budgets, and regularly communicates with members fosters confidence in its stewardship. A business leader who invites external audits and empowers a diverse leadership team ensures that no single person has unchecked control. These systems reflect humility—acknowledging that leaders are not perfect—and demonstrate integrity in stewarding God’s resources.

Faithful leadership is less about how much we accomplish and more about how we manage what God entrusts to us. One day, each leader will stand before God to give an account. That moment should not stir fear but inspire diligence, humility, and joy. When leaders live as stewards rather than owners, they walk in freedom—knowing the results belong to God, while their role is simply to be faithful.

Time as a Sacred Resource

Time is one of the most valuable and limited resources God entrusts to us. Unlike money or possessions, once time is spent, it cannot be regained. Scripture reminds us to make “the most of every opportunity, because the days are evil” (Ephesians 5:16).

Wise leaders recognize that every moment carries eternal weight, and they manage their time not merely for efficiency but for Kingdom impact. This means evaluating daily choices through the lens of eternity—asking whether their time is being invested in what glorifies God, builds up others, and advances His mission.

Intentional Stewardship of Time

Leaders who steward their time well do so with intentionality. They establish priorities that align with God’s purposes rather than being driven solely by urgent demands or cultural expectations. Jesus modeled this balance during His ministry, often withdrawing to pray, choosing to focus on discipling a few and refusing to be rushed by the crowd’s agenda.

In the same way, leaders today must learn to say no to distractions and lesser pursuits so they can say yes to what matters most. Ultimately, wise time management is not just about productivity—it is an act of faithful obedience, a commitment to invest each moment in ways that reflect God’s priorities and leave a lasting legacy.

### Financial Integrity

#### 7.3.1 — Explain the importance of financial integrity.

“We are taking pains to do what is right, not only in the eyes of the Lord but also in the eyes of men.” (2 Corinthians 8:21)

Financial integrity builds trust. Leaders must handle money transparently, honestly, and with accountability to God and people. Financial integrity is more than just maintaining proper accounting practices—it is the foundational element that underpins trust, credibility, and influence in leadership. A leader is entrusted with resources not solely for operational purposes, but as a steward of God’s provision. This stewardship carries a profound responsibility: every decision involving money should reflect honesty, transparency, and accountability. When leaders handle finances with integrity, they demonstrate that their character is consistent and reliable, and that they place God’s standards above personal gain or convenience. Such conduct not only honors God but also reinforces the ethical culture of the organization, setting a model for others to follow.

The impact of financial integrity extends far beyond spreadsheets and budgets. Leaders who manage resources faithfully inspire confidence in those they lead, whether it be a team, a congregation, or a wider organization. People are naturally drawn to leaders who act consistently, who avoid even the appearance of impropriety, and who communicate openly about financial decisions. Trust, once established, enables greater collaboration, unity, and shared commitment toward the mission.

On the other hand, misuse, negligence, or opaque handling of resources can rapidly erode trust, causing relational damage and compromising the effectiveness of the organization. Biblical teaching consistently underscores this principle: leaders are accountable to God for how they manage what has been entrusted to them (Luke 16:10–12). In essence, financial integrity is not merely a managerial requirement—it is a spiritual imperative , shaping both leadership influence and the long-term health of the ministry or organization.

The Bible emphasizes stewardship and accountability: “Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much” (Luke 16:10). Leaders are called to be faithful with all resources, recognizing they are ultimately accountable to God for how they manage what has been entrusted to them.

Practical Application

Keep accurate, up-to-date, and transparent financial records.

Accurate record-keeping is the backbone of financial integrity. Leaders should ensure that all income, every expense, and all donations are properly documented and easily accessible for review. Transparent records allow stakeholders—team members, boards, donors, or congregants—to see how resources are being used and provide reassurance that funds are handled responsibly. Maintaining current records also equips leaders to make informed decisions, plan budgets effectively, and respond to questions or concerns quickly and confidently.

Avoid even the appearance of personal enrichment or favoritism.

Integrity is not only about actual honesty but also about how actions are perceived. Leaders must be vigilant to prevent even the appearance of misusing funds for personal gain or showing preferential treatment in financial decisions. By clearly separating personal finances from organizational resources, communicating policies openly, and applying rules consistently, leaders maintain credibility and protect the trust placed in them. People are more likely to follow and support leaders whose character is unimpeachable both in reality and in perception.

Model generosity and faithful giving as a reflection of stewardship.

Leaders set the tone for financial culture through their behavior. When a leader practices generosity—giving of time, resources, and money—they model what it means to be a faithful steward. This not only encourages others to give cheerfully but also reinforces that the resources entrusted to the organization exist to serve a higher purpose, not to serve individual agendas. Generosity communicates that stewardship is about advancing God’s kingdom and meeting real needs, rather than personal accumulation.

Establish checks, audits, or accountability structures to ensure honesty and transparency.

Even with the best intentions, mistakes and mismanagement can occur. Leaders who implement accountability measures—such as regular audits, oversight committees, or financial review boards—create safeguards that protect the organization, its resources, and its reputation. These structures demonstrate a proactive commitment to transparency and foster a culture where honesty is expected and valued. Accountability also provides opportunities for learning, correction, and continuous improvement in financial stewardship practices.

Cultivate a culture of trust, respect, and Kingdom-focused stewardship.

By prioritizing financial integrity in all areas, leaders build an environment where trust thrives, decisions are respected, and people feel secure in contributing their time, talent, and resources. This culture extends beyond finances—it influences organizational morale, unity, and overall effectiveness. When resources are stewarded faithfully and transparently, the organization can operate confidently, pursue its mission boldly, and maximize impact for God’s kingdom. Integrity in financial management becomes a visible reflection of the leader’s character and a tangible demonstration of obedience to God’s principles.

Leadership Financial Integrity Checklist

 Accurate and Transparent Records

All income and expenses are recorded promptly and accurately.

Financial statements are up to date and easy to access for review.

Budget reports are shared regularly with stakeholders, team members, or boards.

 Avoid Conflicts of Interest

Personal finances are completely separate from organizational funds.

No financial decisions favor friends, family, or personal interests.

Policies and procedures are applied consistently to all.

 Model Generosity and Stewardship

I regularly give of my resources in alignment with Kingdom priorities.

I communicate the purpose of giving and stewardship to encourage others.

My financial behavior reflects faithfulness, integrity, and service.

 Accountability and Oversight

Regular audits or financial reviews are conducted by an independent party or oversight committee.

Clear reporting lines and approvals exist for all significant financial decisions.

Systems are in place to detect errors, misuse, or irregularities.

 Cultivating Trust and Kingdom-Focused Culture

Decisions involving resources are transparent and explained clearly.

Team members and stakeholders are encouraged to ask questions and provide feedback.

Resources are always aligned with organizational mission and God’s purposes.

I reflect on my financial stewardship regularly to ensure it aligns with integrity and Kingdom impact.

Consider the ministry leader who faithfully reports every donation and expense, even when no one is watching. Their team observes this diligence, and donors trust that their contributions are making a real impact. In contrast, a leader who manipulates or hides financial records quickly loses credibility. Even if the misuse seems small—trust, once broken, is difficult to restore.

In summary, financial integrity is not simply a matter of bookkeeping—it is a reflection of a leader’s character, values, and commitment to God’s principles. By keeping accurate records, avoiding conflicts of interest, modeling generosity, and implementing accountability structures, leaders cultivate trust, credibility, and a culture of ethical stewardship. When financial decisions are made transparently and responsibly, resources are maximized for Kingdom impact, and the organization thrives under God-honoring leadership. Ultimately, a leader who prioritizes integrity in all financial matters demonstrates that faithful stewardship is both a practical responsibility and a spiritual calling.

### Responsibility to Care for People

#### 7.4.1 — Describe true leadership as it relates to the care of people.

“I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” (John 10:11)

True leadership is fundamentally relational, reflecting the heart and character of the Good Shepherd described in John 10:11. Unlike a manager who may focus primarily on tasks, schedules, or measurable outcomes, a shepherd-leader understands that people are entrusted to them as living souls with intrinsic value. Leadership is therefore not transactional—it is deeply personal, spiritual, and eternal in scope. Every decision, action, or initiative must be made in light of how it could impact individuals under one’s care, not just organizational goals.

Leaders who mirror the Good Shepherd recognize that their primary responsibility is to guide, protect, and nurture. Guidance involves helping others navigate both practical and spiritual challenges, providing direction that aligns with God’s truth. Protection entails safeguarding individuals from physical, emotional, or spiritual harm, whether that comes from external threats, internal conflict, or personal struggles. Nurturing focuses on helping people grow in character, faith, and capacity, fostering an environment where their gifts can flourish and their weaknesses can be supported.

This relational, sacrificial mindset means leadership is measured not merely by efficiency, statistics, or results, but by the health, growth, and maturity of the people being led. A leader’s success is revealed in the spiritual, emotional, and moral well-being of their team. True leaders are willing to invest time, energy, and even personal comfort to shepherd those under their care, just as Christ laid down His life for the sheep. In doing so, leadership becomes more than a role—it becomes a ministry, a calling to serve God through the faithful stewardship of people’s lives.

Practical Application

Know the people you lead personally

Effective leadership begins with genuine relationships. Take time to understand each individual’s gifts, challenges, and needs. This allows guidance to be tailored, encouragement to be meaningful, and trust to be established.

Protect those you lead spiritually and emotionally

Like a shepherd guarding sheep, a leader must be vigilant against influences that can harm their team’s spiritual health, morale, or emotional stability. This may include counseling, providing wise guidance, or intervening to prevent relational or ethical harm.

Lead with compassion and humility

People are not resources or statistics—they are God’s children, each with intrinsic value. Compassionate leadership seeks to serve rather than dominate, to lift rather than pressure, and to prioritize the long-term flourishing of individuals over short-term gains.

Invest sacrificially in others

Sometimes leadership calls for personal sacrifice—time, comfort, or even reputation—to ensure the welfare of those under your care. Sacrificial leadership models Christ’s example, demonstrating love that motivates loyalty, trust, and growth.

Just as a shepherd walks ahead to guide but remains ready to step into danger to protect the flock, leaders are called to proactively anticipate needs and respond with courage, wisdom, and selfless love. Modern leaders who mentor, advocate, and defend their teams cultivate a culture of safety, trust, and spiritual maturity.

Shepherd-Leadership in Practice

True shepherd-leadership is not theoretical—it is demonstrated through intentional, tangible actions that reflect care, guidance, and protection for those under a leader’s responsibility. Here are some modern examples:

Personal Investment in Team Members

A leader who meets regularly with team members one-on-one, not just to review tasks but to understand their challenges, aspirations, and personal well-being, exemplifies shepherding. For instance, a ministry leader might meet weekly with volunteers to pray for them, celebrate victories, and discuss struggles, ensuring each person feels seen and valued.

Protecting Against Harm

Shepherd-leaders actively guard their people from harmful influences or toxic environments. In a workplace, this might mean addressing conflicts proactively, defending employees from unfair treatment, or creating policies that ensure emotional safety. In a church or ministry context, it could involve mentoring young leaders to prevent burnout or guarding against spiritual deception.

Sacrificial Support

Leaders often model Christlike sacrifice by putting others’ needs above their own comfort. A manager who rearranges their schedule to coach an employee through a personal crisis, or a pastor who spends late nights counseling struggling members, demonstrates a willingness to lay down time, energy, or personal convenience for the good of others.

Encouraging Growth and Development

A shepherd-leader cultivates an environment for spiritual, emotional, and professional growth. Examples include providing training opportunities, offering constructive feedback with care, and celebrating progress. In a church, this might mean discipling a young leader to develop teaching or counseling skills, even when it requires extra mentoring time.

Modeling Integrity and Compassion

Shepherd-leaders set the tone for behavior by living out humility, honesty, and empathy. When a leader admits mistakes, seeks reconciliation, or prioritizes people over policies, it reinforces that the team’s well-being matters more than rigid procedures or personal agendas.

In all these ways, modern shepherd-leadership mirrors Christ’s example—leaders guide, protect, and nurture, not out of obligation but out of love and responsibility for the people God has placed under their care. The lasting impact of such leadership is seen not in numbers or programs but in transformed lives, strengthened faith, and a culture of trust and mutual respect.

## 8 - The Jethro Principle of Leadership

### Courage to Face Opposition

#### 8.1.1 — Explain ways to overcome fear in the face of opposition.

“Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Do not be terrified; do not be discouraged, for the Lord your God will be with you wherever you go.” (Joshua 1:9)

In a 1931 Collier’s magazine article, Winston Churchill wrote, “Courage is rightly esteemed the first of human qualities . . . because it is the quality which guarantees all others.” Without courage, even the most brilliant strategies remain unused, and the most gifted leaders remain silent.

Think of Joshua standing before the Jordan River with the entire nation of Israel behind him. Before him was a land filled with fortified cities, giant warriors, and endless unknowns. Behind him was a restless people prone to complaining and fear. The weight of leadership must have been overwhelming. Yet God’s words cut through the uncertainty: “Be strong and courageous.”

Every leader eventually comes to a “Jordan River moment”—a time when fear whispers, “You are not ready. You are not enough. This is too big for you.” But in those moments, God calls His leaders to courage rooted not in their own strength but in His unshakable presence.

Godly leaders face opposition with courage, not fear. Leadership often requires moving against the current, challenging the status quo, or standing for truth when compromise seems easier. Joshua’s commissioning reminds us that courage is not merely the absence of fear but the presence of faith. Courage flows from the conviction that God is with us and His purposes cannot fail. True leadership often involves standing firm when others shrink back.

Courage is not recklessness; it is faith in action. It means standing firm when truth is unpopular, leading forward when others hesitate, and believing God’s promises when circumstances look impossible. True leadership requires conviction—an inner strength born from the assurance that God is present and His purposes cannot fail.

Joshua’s story shows that courage is not a personality trait reserved for the fearless. It is a command from God, available to every leader who leans on Him. Courage is obedience in the face of fear.

When Joshua was commissioned, God repeated the command to “be strong and courageous” three times in just four verses (Joshua 1:6–9). This repetition reveals two truths: 1) Joshua likely struggled with fear and insecurity (just as every leader does). 2) God knew courage would be the defining quality Joshua needed for the journey ahead.

Opposition is inevitable for leaders. Jesus warned His followers, “In this world you will have trouble. But take heart! I have overcome the world” (John 16:33). Courage, then, is not optional for Christian leadership—it is a necessity.

Practical Application

Pray for Boldness in the Face of Challenges

Courage begins in the presence of God. Before David faced Goliath, he first spent time in the fields with God, cultivating trust and intimacy in prayer. Prayer does not always remove the challenge, but it transforms the leader’s heart to face the challenge differently. When you pray for boldness, you invite God to exchange your fear for His strength, your uncertainty for His assurance.

The early church understood this well. In Acts 4:29, after being threatened by authorities, the disciples prayed, “Lord, consider their threats and enable your servants to speak your word with great boldness.” Their prayer was not for the removal of opposition but for the courage to stand firm in the midst of it. That same prayer remains powerful for leaders today.

Daily prayer re-centers leadership in God’s power rather than human ability. It is in prayer that courage is birthed, renewed, and sustained. A leader who prays will never face opposition alone.

Remember God’s Presence Is Constant, Even When Circumstances Feel Overwhelming

The greatest weapon against fear is the unshakable truth of God’s presence. Joshua was not promised a life free from battles, but he was promised God’s abiding presence in every battle. Fear magnifies the obstacle before us; faith magnifies the God who is with us.

Leaders often feel isolated, carrying burdens unseen by others. Yet Scripture reassures us: “Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me.” (Psalm 23:4). The promise of God’s presence transforms valleys into pathways and opposition into opportunities.

When circumstances feel overwhelming, remind yourself and those you lead that God has not stepped away. His presence is not seasonal or conditional—it is constant, even in the moments when you feel least aware of it. This awareness breeds confidence and steadiness in leadership because you know you never walk into a meeting, crisis, or confrontation alone.

Take Decisive Action Rather Than Avoiding Opposition

Fear thrives on delay. The longer a leader hesitates, the more intimidating the challenge becomes. Courage is strengthened not in avoidance but in decisive, faith-filled action. Joshua could not simply stand on the banks of the Jordan River and wait for the waters to part; he had to step forward in obedience before God performed the miracle (Joshua 3:15–16).

Leaders must learn that bold steps, even small ones, weaken the grip of fear and create momentum. Decisive action communicates faith not only to yourself but also to those you lead. It inspires confidence, rallies support, and builds resilience in your team.

Indecision, on the other hand, often signals uncertainty and breeds doubt. People will hesitate to follow a leader who hesitates to lead. But when you take a step—however small—in faith, it sets an example for others to follow. Leadership courage is contagious; your obedience can spark boldness in those around you.

### Navigating Conflict with Grace

#### 8.2.1 — Identify the five ways to navigate conflict.

“If your brother sins against you, go and show him his fault, just between the two of you. If he listens to you, you have won your brother over. (Matthew 18:15)

Conflict is not a matter of if but when. No matter how healthy, loving, or aligned a group of people may be, conflict will inevitably arise because we are human—each with different personalities, perspectives, and preferences. Misunderstandings happen, words get misinterpreted, and even well-meaning actions can cause unintended hurt. In fact, the absence of visible conflict is not always a sign of health; sometimes it signals avoidance, suppression, or fear of honest communication.

What distinguishes Christlike leadership is not the ability to prevent all conflict, but the ability to respond in a way that reflects the heart of Jesus. Leaders set the tone for how disagreements are approached. If they model avoidance, issues fester beneath the surface. If they respond harshly or defensively, relationships fracture and trust erodes. But when leaders choose the way of Christ, they demonstrate that conflict can be redeemed into an opportunity for growth, reconciliation, and stronger unity.

Jesus provides a clear and practical pattern for addressing conflict: go directly to the person involved, do it privately, and seek restoration rather than retaliation. His wisdom recognizes both the dignity of the other person and the value of the relationship. Private conversations protect against embarrassment and gossip. Direct communication eliminates confusion and hearsay. And the goal of reconciliation keeps the conversation focused not on proving a point but on winning back a brother or sister in love.

Modern Day Examples

Workplace

Two team members disagree over how a project should be executed. Instead of venting to others in frustration, a leader quietly brings both individuals together to listen, clarify intentions, and help them find common ground. The result is not only a smoother project but also renewed respect between colleagues.

Ministry Team

A volunteer feels overlooked when their contributions are not acknowledged. Rather than ignoring the tension or letting gossip spread, the leader has a private conversation to affirm the volunteer’s value, apologize for the oversight, and restore their sense of belonging.

Family

A parent and teenager clash over boundaries. Instead of resorting to shouting or silent treatment, the parent calmly sits down with their child, listens to their perspective, and explains the reasoning behind the boundaries. This approach models humility and respect, and strengthens the parent-child relationship.

Leaders who navigate conflict with grace create environments where people feel safe to speak honestly, where mistakes do not mean rejection, and where reconciliation is possible even after offense. In these kinds of cultures, trust is strengthened because people know they will be heard fairly. Respect is deepened because issues are handled with maturity and integrity. And unity is preserved—not through uniformity or silence but through Christlike love and intentional restoration.

Practical Application

Address conflicts quickly.

Unresolved conflict rarely disappears on its own—it tends to grow, spread, and harden hearts. Scripture reminds us not to let the sun go down on our anger (Ephesians 4:26) because delay gives the enemy room to sow division. Leaders must resist the temptation to ignore issues in hope they will fade away. Instead, deal with conflicts promptly and directly. Doing so communicates value for the relationship and prevents bitterness from taking root. Quick action does not mean rushed or careless words—it means intentional, timely, Spirit-led engagement.

Listen actively before responding.

Too often we enter difficult conversations with a plan to defend ourselves or prove our point rather than to understand. Active listening means slowing down, giving the other person your full attention, and seeking to understand not only what they are saying but why they feel the way they do. Ask clarifying questions, reflect back what you have heard, and show genuine empathy. Even if you disagree, people feel honored and respected when they know they have been heard. As James 1:19 teaches: “Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry.”

Pursue restoration, not victory.

The measure of a successful conflict resolution is not who “won” the debate but whether the relationship has been restored. Winning arguments may inflate the ego, but restoring relationships advances the Kingdom. Christlike leaders enter conflict with the goal of healing, not scoring points. This often requires humility—owning your part in the conflict, offering forgiveness, and releasing the need to be “right.” True leadership values people over pride, reconciliation over recognition, and unity over being proven correct.

Protect confidentiality.

One of the greatest temptations in conflict is to bring others into the situation unnecessarily—whether through venting, gossip, or rallying support. Leaders must guard against this by keeping conversations private and honoring the dignity of all involved. Gossip not only damages reputations but also erodes trust across the team. By following Jesus’ directive to go to them privately (Matthew 18:15), leaders model respect and create a culture where people know their struggles will be handled with integrity.

Invite God into the process.

Conflict resolution is not merely a human skill—it is a spiritual practice. Before entering a hard conversation, leaders should pray for wisdom, humility, and a heart that reflects Christ. During the discussion, they should remain sensitive to the Holy Spirit’s guidance. Afterward, they should continue praying for healing and unity. By relying on God, leaders avoid leaning solely on their own words or strategies and instead allow Him to bring lasting reconciliation.

#### 8.2.2 — Describe the differences between dumping and delegating, and formulate a process to apply the principles learned.

When ministers decide to become leaders, they make an important decision that will entirely change the future of their ministry. Their focus is shifted from evaluating their own self accomplishments to evaluating themselves based on what they can accomplish through others.

In Exodus 18:17–27, we are reintroduced to one of Israel’s patriarchs, Moses. In the same passage, we are also introduced to his father-in-law Jethro. While Moses was the leader, God used Jethro to teach Moses some valuable lessons in leadership. As the passage begins, Moses is a nurturer. By the time the passage concludes, Moses is an equipper.

Because Moses was their leader, the children of Israel looked to Moses for help and counsel. In fact, there were so many people seeking his counsel that a long line formed. When Moses took his seat in the morning, he stayed there until the evening. Moses was listening to people’s disputes, giving advice, making judgments, and explaining to people the meaning of the Word of God. Jethro is observing his son-in-law at work and makes some keen observations. The following verses take us inside the situation:

“Moses’ father-in-law replied, ‘What you are doing is not good. You and these people who come to you will only wear yourselves out. The work is too heavy for you; you cannot handle it alone. Listen to me now and I will give you some advice, and may God be with you’” (vv. 17–19).

Delegation: a Safeguard from Tragic Endings

Herein lies some of the greatest advice Moses would ever receive: initiate the Jethro Principle. This is a principle that teaches the importance of delegation and empowerment. Perhaps no other principle of leadership so precisely defines successful pastoral leadership (and other arenas of leadership for that matter) than the “Jethro Principle.” The leader must learn how and when to delegate!

When individuals surrender their lives to a full-time Christian vocation, they enter an arena filled with on-going, unending, seemingly infinite problems and complexities. Ministry should be Christ-centered, but it is also “people-focused.” Without people, there would not be the structural outlets to release spiritual gifts or meaningful channels to serve others. But how well are people being served when their leader is weary, overworked, and underappreciated?

The demands on pastors in local churches are enormous. Pastors are like members of an emergency trauma center at your local hospital. These trained and talented people care for hurting souls on a regular basis, many of whom are suffering because of a genuine crisis in their lives. Long before there were trauma centers, “crisis care” was a normal part of the minister’s daily work environment. Many pastors are doing the best they can, but often wonder why they are living in the haze of a twilight zone. Some sit all day and listen to a long list of problems with no end in sight. Like Moses, what they are doing is not good for them.

Mark Coppenger, a pastor in Evanston, Illinois, recently wrote about crisis, the church, and the days in which we live:

The church is always in crisis mode. A new convert throws her live-in boyfriend out of the house, and now she has to make up the difference in the rent. You get word that a junior high Sunday school teacher is telling her class that without a “special act of God” they’re second rate Christians. A van load of youth are injured on the way to church camp. The local plant closes and three of your deacons are out of work. A staff member quits in a flurry of recrimination. A prominent member shows up on the DUI list of the local paper. A business meeting ignites over item III, Section B, in the budget. And that is just this week. (Coppenger 2001, under “Leading in Troubled Times”)

How do you suppose Moses felt at the end of the day after hearing all those problems from all those people? Thank God for Jethro! This dear man comes alongside his son-in-law and gives him some great advice: first let go and accept the help of others. Then Moses was able to get back to what was his primary assignment.

Our Priorities: the Word of God and Prayer

“Jethro told Moses, ‘Listen now to me and I will give you some advice, and may God be with you. You must be the people’s representative before God and bring their disputes to him. Teach them the decrees and laws, and show them the way to live and the duties they are to perform’” (vv. 19–20 ). This wise and practical man reminded Moses that he needed to be the people’s representative before the Lord. He told him that the priorities should rest in teaching God’s law and statutes, interceding in prayer, and instructing the people in how to live holy lives. He explained that the shared efforts would result in the multiplication of completed work. The one-on-one method that Moses was using had drained his strength and that of the people.

Surround Yourself with Qualified People

Jethro gave further advisement:

‘But select capable men from all the people—men who fear God, trustworthy men who hate dishonest gain—and appoint them as officials over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens. Have them serve as judges for the people at all times, but have them bring every difficult case to you; the simple cases they can decide for themselves. They will make your load lighter, because they will share it with you. If you do this and God so commands, you will be able to stand the strain, and all these people will go home satisfied.’ (vv. 21–23)

Moses was learning how to delegate and how to teach others lessons he was still learning himself. He needed help, and Jethro said, “This is the way to do it.” The plan was for Moses to use his time for administrating the most important and pressing issues. The non-essential things, though still important, could be managed by other capable people on Moses’ team. Do not neglect the result of this managerial arrangement: Moses could perform at a higher level and have more strength at the end of the day. Others could be utilized under his leadership and most importantly, the people’s needs would be met and they would be at peace.

Look at the contrast in the two types of positions: the nurturer versus the leader. The difference is clear.

The following is a story in my personal transition from a leader who assumed the full role of nurturer to one who was changed as I learned to apply the principles taught by Jethro to my own ministry and my sphere of influence.

In my church, I made the hospital visits to every member who was sick. Then I found a member of the church who loved ministering to people. I asked him if he would like to visit hospital patients with me sometime, and he heartily agreed, saying he would love to go. So, we arranged some times to visit together. I taught him how to minister in a hospital, including all the dos and do nots of that ministry. Three or four months later, while I was speaking at an out-of-town event, I was informed that one of our members was taken to the hospital. I called the man I had trained, filled him in on the situation, and asked him if he would visit that person in my absence. He was glad to do it. I knew he said yes without hesitation because by then he knew how to conduct a hospital visit; he had been trained and equipped to do ministry in that environment.

Together, this man and I developed a hospital ministry team in our church. He found other people to go with him, and he would train them as I had trained him. When I left that church years later, there were seventy-two workers on our hospital ministry team! Until a pastor changes what he or she is doing, he or she will be the only person visiting the hospitals. This is one principle every pastor needs to remember: never do ministry alone.

The Twin Realities in the Jethro Principle

From this story of Jethro advising Moses comes two huge realities that cannot be overlooked if we want to affect change in our own ministries. First is the fact that Moses changed his way of thinking. In John Maxwell’s book Thinking for a Change , he writes: “If you change your thinking, you will change your life” (2003, 3). Moses’ life was forever changed because he began to think differently about how he approached his work, his time, his people, his family, his energy, even his calling as the leader of the nation of Israel. Maxwell summarizes three reasons why good thinking is so important; namely, it creates the foundation for good results; it increases the leader’s potential; and it produces more good thinking once you develop the habit (2003, 3).

All three points are valid. Through delegation, Moses and the entire nation of Israel began to lay the foundation for good results. No nation has too many good leaders. By executing his vision for the nation through empowering other leaders around him, Moses was modeling a principle that would influence Israel for generations.

Thinking differently also increased Moses’ own potential. Through his new leadership structure he secured more time to be alone with God, dream big dreams for his nation, develop his own skills, and think through challenges that were unique to his role.

Additionally, Moses made these leadership changes and then stayed with them! In other words, he started doing things differently, too. It is not easy to let go of the old ways. After all, we know what the other way will produce. This is why a change of perspective on what you, as a leader, should be doing and handling is so necessary. Moses realized that he was even able to think more clearly. It was only when he began to take on more than he should that he got weary, angry, impatient, and fell back into the old way of behaving. This should give each of us a stronger incentive to make good thinking a habitual way of life.

So, Moses changed his way of working, drastically . Before receiving Jethro’s advice, he had gone to work with no end in sight. Perhaps there were times when the line of people was as far as his eye could see and each concern presented its own challenge, so the people would keep waiting and waiting. After Jethro stepped in, things got better organized; they started flowing. Now Moses only had to handle big items. And frankly, that is exactly what Moses needed to be doing. In truth, that is where pastors need to be, focusing on the big items that are before them and their people. Instead, many pastors are so bogged down in taking care of the little things that they lose sight of the greater reason God called them into the ministry.

So how do we, as growing leaders, put a new mentality about change into practice in our churches (or future churches), our organizations of Christian service, and our communities of influence and service? I offer the following suggestions on ways of changing how you work in the ministry.

1. Practice restraint. When equipping others, a leader needs to exercise restraint and give those being trained opportunities to succeed and fail. The natural tendency will be for the experienced leader / trainer to fix-it. But the wise equipper gives room for those in the learning process to fail.

2. Become adaptable. The world around us is changing rapidly. And with rapid change comes the need for leaders, while being firm and fixed in their values, to be flexible and adaptable in their approaches.

3. Plan for personal and corporate long-term health. When leaders make changes in the way they work, they can expect some changes in their personal health and the health of the church. God designed us to delegate. Life with all of its challenges is just too difficult to handle alone. One writer says this in reference to how a minister should handle the work of the ministry: “He (God) never meant for us (the individual) to tackle a task that required more than one person to accomplish it.”

Dumping Versus Delegating

You might find at this point in the lesson a renewed sense of joy about the ministry. You are already thinking about various ones to whom you can delegate some of your responsibilities. However, be careful that you are not dumping responsibilities instead of delegating them.

There is a difference. Dumping is when you give someone a job or responsibility that you do not want. You say, “God bless you,” and leave them with it. That is it. Often in their excitement of passing along responsibilities, senior leaders will hand down tasks without training and equipping those on the receiving end, be it associates or laypeople. If your ushers are not trained in the basics of being an usher, they will think their greatest ministry contribution is holding up the back wall of the auditorium as they lean against it. If you ever go to a church where ushers do not greet people and serve the worshippers in attendance, you are in a church that does not place a high priority on usher training. When leaders fail to teach the workers, each worker will make up his or her own rules. This is the real danger of dumping.

On the other hand, delegating is where you pass the baton on to another; but before, during and soon after you have made the transfer, you spend time with the recipient, teaching, training, and modeling the ministry before him or her. As people are equipped, they become more comfortable on their own and soon they are training others, just as they were trained. Remember those ushers who are holding up the back walls, and find the motivation to choose delegation and development.

A final word about the Jethro principle: There is a distinct five-step development process. It goes like this:

1. I do it while you watch.

2. We do it together.

3. You do it while I watch.

4. We evaluate.

5. You do it while another watches .

Tim Elmore (2001), vice president of leadership development for EQUIP, says that “Nearly every lasting movement in history endured because the first group of leaders reproduced their leadership and values into a second generation of leaders. It became a movement because it was about multiplication and not addition.”

### Trusting God in Uncertainty

#### 8.3.1 — Explain how trusting God in uncertain circumstances distinguishes a Christlike leader.

“Do not fear, for I am with you; do not be dismayed, for I am your God. I will strengthen you and help you; I will uphold you with my righteous right hand.” (Isaiah 41:10)

Uncertainty is an inherent part of life and leadership. No matter how experienced, knowledgeable, or skilled a leader may be, there will always be situations beyond human control or understanding. These can range from organizational shifts, financial crises, and unexpected market changes to personal challenges, health concerns, or team conflicts. Such moments test a leader’s composure, judgment, and reliance on God.

Trusting God in these circumstances distinguishes a Christlike leader. Leaders who depend on God rather than their own insight demonstrate to their teams that faith is stronger than fear. This trust is active, not passive. It involves making decisions thoughtfully, moving forward with courage, and remaining steady, knowing God’s presence and promises form a solid foundation even when the path is unclear. Leaders who lean on God model confidence grounded in eternal truth rather than in temporary circumstances.

Faith under pressure is contagious. When a leader demonstrates calm, clarity, and confidence rooted in God, it reassures the team and encourages them to respond in faith instead of panic. Teams often mirror the emotional state of their leaders; a leader who navigates uncertainty with prayerful patience, wise counsel, and visible trust in God inspires resilience, creativity, and collaboration.

It is important to remember that leadership is not the absence of uncertainty—it is the faithful navigation of it. True leadership means acknowledging the unknown, facing it with courage, and guiding others with integrity and vision, all while keeping God at the center. By embracing uncertainty with a posture of dependence on God, leaders transform challenges into opportunities for growth, deepen trust within their teams, and cultivate a culture where faith—not fear—drives action.

Leading Through Unprecedented Challenges: Lessons from the COVID-19 Pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic presented leaders with unique and unprecedented challenges that tested faith, resilience, and decision making. Leaders faced uncertainty on multiple levels: rapidly changing public health guidelines, economic instability, disrupted operations, and the emotional toll on their teams and communities. Decisions often had to be made with limited information, and the consequences of those decisions could affect both physical well-being and organizational sustainability.

During this time, leaders had to balance the need for adaptability with the responsibility to protect and guide those under their care. Remote work, virtual gatherings, and social distancing required creative approaches to communication and community building. Many leaders struggled with feelings of isolation, heightened stress, and the pressure to provide stability in an unstable world.

Amid these difficulties, the pandemic highlighted the importance of trusting God in uncertainty. Leaders learned that spiritual discernment, prayer, and reliance on God’s wisdom were critical in navigating the unknown. Faith became a source of courage and clarity, reminding leaders that even in circumstances beyond their control, God’s guidance remains steady. The pandemic underscored that true leadership often requires trusting God before fully understanding the path ahead, serving others faithfully despite fear, and leaning on the support of a community of believers.

Practical Application

Reaffirm God’s promises regularly.

Make it a habit to anchor your mind and your team in God’s faithfulness. Begin meetings, planning sessions, or personal devotionals by reading Scripture that reinforces God’s presence and provision. For example, Isaiah 41:10 reminds us not to fear because God strengthens and upholds us; Proverbs 3:5–6 calls us to trust God fully rather than relying on our own understanding; and Romans 8:28 assures that God works all things for good for those who love Him.

Consider starting meetings with a brief moment of reflection or a Scripture reading that highlights God’s promises in past situations, helping your team cultivate confidence and perspective even when outcomes are uncertain.

Avoid hasty decisions fueled by anxiety.

Uncertainty can trigger fear-driven reactions. Leaders often feel pressure to “do something” immediately, but decisions made in haste or under stress frequently compound problems. Instead, implement intentional pauses—take a moment to pray, gather relevant information, and seek wise counsel.

Encourage your team to voice concerns and potential solutions in a calm environment, allowing space for God’s guidance to surface. Over time, this creates a culture where thoughtful, prayerful decision making becomes the norm rather than reactive choices dictated by fear.

Model calm and encouragement for your team.

Your behavior sets the emotional and spiritual tone for those around you. In times of uncertainty, actively demonstrate trust in God by remaining composed, speaking words of hope, and sharing concrete examples of His faithfulness from both your personal experience and the team’s history.

Encourage team members to identify and verbalize what they are trusting God for, creating a shared environment of faith. Even small gestures—acknowledging progress, celebrating perseverance, or reminding others of God’s promises—reinforce a culture of resilience and hope.

Build structured practices for ongoing reassurance.

Consider implementing routines that consistently reinforce trust in God. Begin weekly team meetings with a short devotional or testimony of God’s guidance. Keep a “God’s Faithfulness” journal for yourself and invite team members to contribute stories of answered prayers and victories over past challenges. Encourage reflective pauses before major decisions where the team collectively prays or seeks God’s insight.

Lead with perspective, not panic.

Remind yourself and others that uncertainty is temporary, but God’s sovereignty and promises are eternal. Teach your team to differentiate between problems requiring immediate action and situations that call for patience, discernment, and prayerful reliance on God. By modeling perspective over panic, you cultivate a resilient, faith-driven team capable of navigating ambiguity with confidence.

Trusting God in uncertainty also requires patience and humility. Leaders often feel pressure to have all the answers, but acknowledging our limitations and depending on God models vulnerability and authenticity. Over time, this builds a culture where the team also leans on God rather than defaulting to fear or self-reliance.

### Perseverance in Leadership

#### 8.4.1 — Describe the long-lasting impact of perseverance in leadership.

“Therefore, since we are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, let us throw off everything that hinders and the sin that so easily entangles, and let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us. Let us fix our eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy set before him endured the cross, scorning its shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.” (Hebrews 12:1–2)

Long-term leadership is less about occasional victories and more about steadfast faithfulness over extended periods of time. True leaders are not defined by a single moment of brilliance, a crisis resolved, or a goal achieved, but by their ability to remain committed and consistent, even when results are slow, circumstances are difficult, or recognition is absent.

Perseverance in leadership demonstrates dependence on God rather than self. Human abilities and strategies can only carry us so far; enduring leaders continually draw strength, wisdom, and guidance from God. This reliance is visible to those being led—when teams see their leaders remaining calm, prayerful, and faithful in the midst of trials, it builds trust and inspires them to persevere alongside their leaders.

Leadership endurance requires spiritual stamina, much like an athlete preparing for a long-distance race. Athletes cannot rely solely on natural talent; they must train, pace themselves, and endure fatigue to cross the finish line. Similarly, leaders must cultivate spiritual disciplines, such as prayer, Scripture meditation, accountability, and reflection to navigate pressure, uncertainty, and opposition without losing direction or momentum.

Moreover, endurance involves emotional and relational resilience. Leaders often face criticism, setbacks, or misunderstandings. Remaining steady requires processing these experiences in a healthy way, maintaining integrity, and continuing to invest in relationships with those they lead. It is the combination of spiritual reliance, disciplined practice, and relational wisdom that allows a leader to stay the course, inspire confidence, and leave a lasting impact.

Ultimately, endurance in leadership is a testimony of faith: it demonstrates that God’s purposes prevail over challenges, that His strength is sufficient, and that consistent faithfulness—rather than short-term success—is what truly shapes leaders and their legacies.

Practical Application

Keep your focus on Jesus, not immediate results

Success in leadership often takes time. Avoid becoming discouraged by short-term setbacks or delays. Daily devotion, prayer, and meditating on God’s Word help maintain perspective and align your vision with His purposes.

Develop resilience through setbacks

Treat failures and challenges as opportunities to learn rather than reasons to quit. Ask God for wisdom in navigating difficulties and adjust strategies without losing your vision. Overcoming obstacles strengthens character and builds credibility with your team.

Celebrate incremental progress

Recognize and affirm small wins—both in your personal growth and within your team. Celebrating progress helps sustain morale, reinforces commitment, and encourages everyone to persevere together.

Eliminate hindrances and distractions

Identify habits, commitments, or attitudes that drain energy or divert attention from your leadership mission. Simplifying and prioritizing allows you to focus on what truly advances God’s work through you.

Leadership endurance is not about doing more on your own—it is about training to rely more fully on God, maintaining spiritual stamina, and cultivating the habits, discipline, and perspective that allow you to run the long race faithfully.

### Leading in a World of Distraction

#### 8.5.1 — Identify five ways to lead through the noise of distractions.

“Let us fix our eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of our faith.” (Hebrews 12:2)

In today’s fast-paced culture, leaders face a constant battle against distraction. Technology, endless information, and competing priorities can easily pull attention away from what matters most. Effective leadership requires focus—keeping vision clear, priorities straight, and hearts centered on Christ.

In his bestselling book, How to Lead in a World of Distractions , author and speaker Clay Scroggins noted three primary problems with distractions (Scroggins 2019, 24).

First, there is the opportunity cost of the unknown—every time leaders give in to distraction, they sacrifice the chance to pursue something far more valuable. The real danger is not just wasted time; it is the unseen opportunities for growth, impact, and God’s calling that are missed.

Second, distractions create a lack of traction. Instead of moving forward with purpose, leaders spin their wheels, busy but unproductive, accomplishing activity without progress. Over time, this lack of traction can drain energy and lead to discouragement.

Finally, distractions result in the failure to live your best life—the life God intended. By scattering attention in a hundred directions, leaders risk settling for mediocrity instead of walking fully in the mission and joy of their calling. Together, these three consequences remind us that distractions are not harmless interruptions; they are spiritual and practical barriers that rob leaders of focus, fruitfulness, and fulfillment.

Recognize the Noise Around You

Before a leader can overcome distractions, they must first acknowledge them. Distractions often masquerade as good opportunities, urgent matters, or even “necessary tasks.” But unchecked, they divide a leader’s attention and drain energy from the mission God has entrusted to them.

Modern Leaders Face an Unprecedented Volume of Noise

Digital overload— constant notifications, emails, and social media updates that demand attention but rarely move vision forward.

Emotional noise— worries, insecurities, and the pressure to compare with others that cloud judgment.

Organizational noise— competing priorities, too many meetings, and initiatives that dilute focus.

Distraction is not always obvious. Sometimes it is subtle, like pouring energy into activities that feel productive but are not strategic. Leaders who fail to name the noise risk becoming busy but ineffective.

Nehemiah’s enemies tried to distract him with invitations to meetings that seemed harmless but were designed to halt the rebuilding of the wall (Nehemiah 6:2–3). His discernment allowed him to recognize distraction for what it was: an attempt to divert him from God’s assignment. His bold declaration—“I am carrying on a great work and cannot come down”—shows the power of naming the noise and refusing to bow to it.

Imagine a pilot mid-flight. If she listens to every snippet of chatter on the radio, she risks missing the critical instructions that keep the plane on course. Leaders face a similar challenge—tuning out the chatter to hear the voice that truly matters.

Make it a habit to regularly evaluate your inputs. Ask yourself: What voices or activities consistently drain my focus? Which ones propel me toward God’s vision? Writing down the three biggest sources of noise in your life or leadership and setting boundaries around them is the first step toward clarity.

Practical Application

Keep Vision at the Center

The best defense against distraction is a clear, God-given vision. When leaders are uncertain about where they are headed, every new idea or opportunity feels like it could be “the right thing.” But when vision is clear, distractions become easier to recognize because anything that fails to align with the mission can be set aside.

A leader’s focus is tested most when good things compete with the best things. Vision is what separates busyness from fruitfulness. Leaders who drift from their vision often burn out, not because they lack energy, but because their energy is scattered in too many directions.

Jesus faced constant pressure from the crowds. People wanted Him to heal more, feed more, even take political power. Yet He kept His mission central—proclaiming the kingdom of God and preparing the way of salvation. In Luke 4:42–44, when people tried to keep Him in one place, He replied, “I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns also, because that is why I was sent.” His vision clarified His decisions.

Consider a runner in a marathon. If she constantly stops to look at the scenery, wave to the crowd, or change her route, she will never finish the race. The finish line—the vision—keeps her moving forward despite the distractions around her. Similarly, leaders who keep vision front and center are able to endure distractions without losing momentum.

Practical Steps for Leaders

Filter Decisions Through Vision

Write the vision plainly (Habakkuk 2:2). If you cannot articulate it simply, your team cannot follow it.

Revisit and remind. Talk about the vision often—with yourself and with your team. Repetition cements direction.

Connect vision to identity. Remember that your leadership is not about random success; it is about stewarding God’s call.

When vision is kept at the center, leaders gain clarity and courage. The noise of distraction does not disappear, but it loses its power to derail because the leader knows what must be done—and what must be ignored.

Establish Rhythms of Focus

Focus is not accidental—it is cultivated. Leaders who do not build intentional rhythms into their lives end up living in constant reaction to the demands around them. Over time, this reactive posture erodes clarity, peace, and spiritual authority. Establishing healthy rhythms of focus allows leaders to stay grounded in God’s presence while leading effectively in a noisy world.

Why do rhythms matter? They create consistency in a world of chaos. They allow space for God’s voice to be heard above the noise. They prevent burnout by balancing work and rest. They sharpen discernment, enabling leaders to distinguish between urgent distractions and truly important matters.

Jesus is the perfect model of rhythm. Mark 1:35 tells us, “Very early in the morning, while it was still dark, Jesus got up, left the house and went off to a solitary place, where He prayed.” Even in seasons of overwhelming ministry demands, He withdrew regularly to refocus. His rhythm of retreat and prayer fueled His ability to lead with clarity and authority.

Think of a lighthouse keeper. If he neglects the daily rhythm of cleaning the glass and refueling the lamp, the light grows dim, and ships are put at risk. Leaders, too, must maintain spiritual and practical rhythms so their light shines clearly for those they guide.

Practical Rhythms for Leaders

Daily: Begin with Scripture and prayer before engaging the demands of the day. Even ten focused minutes can reset your perspective.

Weekly: Establish a Sabbath—a day of rest and renewal, free from constant productivity. God designed rest as part of effective leadership, not as a luxury.

Seasonally: Take time for retreats or extended reflection to step back, evaluate vision, and hear from God afresh.

Team Rhythms

Build rhythms of focus into your leadership environment—start meetings with prayer, revisit vision regularly, and protect time for creativity and problem solving without constant interruption.

Without rhythms of focus, distractions will always dominate. With them, leaders can lead with clarity, strength, and endurance, staying anchored in God’s mission no matter how noisy the world becomes. Finding space away from all the distractions can give you the chance to find your why.

Lead Others Through the Noise

Distraction is not just a personal battle—it is a leadership responsibility. The way leaders respond to noise shapes the culture of the team or organization they lead. A distracted leader produces a distracted team, but a focused leader creates clarity, stability, and confidence in those who follow.

People today live under a constant barrage of voices—news headlines, social media feeds, competing priorities, and cultural pressures. Many are exhausted not because they lack energy, but because they lack clarity. Leaders have the opportunity to guide others through the noise by embodying focus and pointing them toward God’s mission.

The apostle Paul consistently modeled this for the churches he led. Even while facing persecution, imprisonment, and hardship, he kept pointing others back to Christ. His words in Colossians 3:2—“Set your minds on things above, not on earthly things”—served as both instruction and personal testimony. His ability to remain focused on the eternal helped entire communities endure and thrive in the midst of cultural chaos.

Illustration

Imagine a symphony orchestra. If every musician focuses on their own tune without watching the conductor, the result is chaos. But when the conductor keeps steady focus and leads with clarity, the orchestra produces harmony. Similarly, leaders who cut through the noise provide their teams with alignment and unity.

Practical Ways to Lead Through the Noise

Model focus

Let your team see you choosing vision over distraction. Your example speaks louder than words.

Clarify priorities

Help your people know what matters most and give them permission to release lesser distractions.

Communicate consistently

In a noisy world, clarity requires repetition—say it, show it, and live it.

Protect the team’s attention

Structure meetings, projects, and goals around what advances the mission instead of chasing every new idea.

Encourage spiritual focus

Remind those you lead to anchor their identity and work in Christ, not in the shifting voices around them.

Leadership is not just about managing your own distractions—it is about guiding others through theirs. When leaders embody clarity, they give people the courage to tune out the noise and pursue what matters most.

Practical Application

Identify your top three distractions and create boundaries around them. Revisit your God-given vision weekly; write it down and pray over it. Schedule “distraction-free zones” for deep work and prayer. Teach your team how to filter noise by clarifying priorities.

Summary

Effective leaders do not ignore distractions—they overcome them by anchoring their focus on Christ and His mission. By keeping vision central and modeling focus, they inspire those they lead to pursue purpose in a noisy world.

## 9 - Principles of Leadership

### Foundations of Leadership

#### 9.1.1 — Identify and explain the seven core principles of leadership and how they relate to the work of the church.

We have studied Daniel and Moses; the third key biblical leader to consider is Nehemiah. You will remember that Nehemiah had a high position in the Persian court as a cupbearer to Artaxerxes, the emperor. A reading of the Book of Nehemiah reveals this wise official to be a man of compassion, loyalty, political shrewdness , and religious fervor. He had exceptional organizational and managerial skills and powerful leadership abilities. It is no wonder the discouraged Jewish remnant in Jerusalem were drawn to him; he successfully rallied the people to rebuild walls despite the hostility of neighboring enemies. From his Old Testament book we can glean the following seven principles that are time-tested, proven, and universal.

1. Leadership is influence

Influencing people in a positive way depends on building relationships, so influencing is a process that takes time. I learned this fairly rapidly after becoming a pastor in 1981. I had been with the new church for several weeks when I decided to move the piano from one side of the platform to the other. I thought that as the pastor I could do this if I wanted to, but when the two major influencers of the church showed up on Sunday morning, they were extremely agitated.

Someone said, “Pastor, who told you that you could move that piano? We do not want that piano there.” I learned a great deal about leadership that Sunday. I also learned something about change. My title did not carry the authority or the influence to lead change in that instance. I also discovered something about moving pianos that day. You do not move a piano in one Sunday. You move a piano one inch each Sunday. Change is a process, not an event. If you are going to be a change agent, you must first have influence.

A constant question that arises with pastors is this: “What do I do when I am the pastor and someone else has the influence?” The answer is simple. You influence the influencer. It is important to spend time with the people who have the influence in the church so you can influence them in the direction you want to see the church go.

2. Everything rises and falls on leadership

I learned that everything rises and falls on leadership. This was another one of those life-changing concepts about guiding a church that helped me in the ministry. Why does one church grow and another does not? Why does one church advance and another does not? It is a leadership issue. Again, no church has ever grown past its leadership. In other words, no church can go past the ability of a pastor to effectively lead it. For instance, if a person is only willing to lead a congregation of two hundred and they become the pastor of another church of four hundred, it will only be a matter of time until that individual will have their new flock down to two hundred.

This reality says to me that my church could not grow past me! So I must continue to grow if I want my ministry to grow. I spent sixteen years as a pastor on a growth curve. Every week I was pastoring a church larger than I had ever pastored before. I came to believe that if I did not continue to grow, the church would not grow either. I, in fact, was the lid on the church. I chose to stretch and still desire to. Everything rises and falls on leadership.

3. Leadership takes responsibility for the steps of growth

At some point you need to take responsibility for whatever you have. Jokingly it has been said that if you have been at your church three years, you cannot blame the former pastor for what you have. At some point you have to take responsibility for your ministry. One of the things the Lord taught me in my ministry was this: whatever you build the church on is what you are going to live with. If you build it on hype then you will have hype; if you build it on discipleship then you will have discipleship. The Lord holds each of us responsible for what we do with the ministry He has given us. As an under-shepherd you ultimately must accept responsibility for the ministry God has given you.

4. Leadership credibility holds a supreme value

In Unit 1 of this course we talked about the issues of integrity and credibility. Pastors so often lose credibility when they promise or promote things they do not or cannot fulfill. It is important that we not communicate publicly things we plan to do and then not fulfill them. This will result in teaching people to doubt whatever we say because our credibility has been lost. Credibility involves consistency, faithfulness, and being a person of our word. The greater the credibility, the more effective our leadership will be.

5. Leaders possess tremendous faith in people

If you do not believe in your people, you do not have to tell them; they will sense it. So you need to ask yourself, “Do I have faith in my people? Do I see their potential?” Jesus gathered twelve men close to Him and invested in them because He saw the potential in their lives. They were chosen for this close relationship, not because they had it all together, but because the Lord saw potential. People tend to rise to their leader’s expectations of them. If you do not like people, if you do not have faith in people, then you probably should not be in public ministry—especially not in the church. The ministry is simply about the people. When you look at a congregation of people, do you see the problems, or do you see the potential? Know that your view of people determines your effectiveness in ministry to them. Leaders believe in people.

6. Leadership can be taught

People often ask me if leaders are born as leaders. My answer is yes . People possess varying gifts and leadership. But one thing I know is that everyone can become a better leader than what they are at the present time. Leadership can be taught.

7. Great leaders are effective communicators of vision

Leaders see things that do not exist. Leaders have the ability to help people capture God’s plan and purpose for their lives. Unit 2 deals specifically with this critical area of leadership. Managers take things that exist and organize them. Leaders create things that do not exist. Although we need managers in ministry, if we are going to see churches built and growing, they must be pastored and led by visionary leaders.

Probably one of the most effective charts John Maxwell ever developed was the Five Levels of Leadership. Understanding this chart will enable you to more effectively see the process and progression of leadership in ministry.

### Five Levels of Leadership

#### 9.2.1 — Explain each of the five levels of leadership, and identify the level of your own leadership.

The following information is taken from John Maxwell’s book Developing the Leader Within You (1993, 11–13). Every leader should have knowledge of John Maxwell’s five levels of leadership. It is a valuable resource to consult and re-consult throughout your leadership years. Developing the Leader Within You is not a book that you will relegate to your bookshelf. Many people read it through several times each year; some keep dog-eared copies on their desks.

1. Position

Rights of the job are the basis for this leadership; people follow because they have to. This is the lowest level of influence for a leader. They must rely on their title to get people to follow. There is nothing wrong with titles, but if you must have a title for people to follow you, something is wrong—you are not really leading. Jesus never had a position or title, yet He had huge influence—through building relationships, meeting needs, and offering hope. His authority came from God and the life He lived, not from an assigned position or title. At level one, authority comes only from your title.

A biblical example is Rehoboam: “If today you will be a servant to these people, . . . they will always be your servants. But Rehoboam rejected the advice the elders gave him” (1 Kings 12:7–8).

Note: Influence on this level will not extend beyond the lines of the job description. The only authority possessed is what the title gives. The longer one stays at this level, the higher the turnover rate and the lower the morale of the people.

2. Permission

The bond of relationship is the basis for this leadership―people follow because they want to. Influence at this level extends beyond a leader’s stated authority because there is established connection with people relationally. This person has communicated trust and credibility, and others now choose to follow this leader out of devotion rather than duty. This level is an improvement from level one because you are influencing from your person, not your position. However, leaders must recognize there is a difference between being liked as a friend and being followed as a leader. Experiencing a mere relationship without producing any results for the church or organization will eventually fail to motivate people to make sacrifices, take risks, and follow.

A biblical example is Nehemiah: “You see the trouble we are in: Jerusalem lies in ruins, and its gates have been burned with fire. Come, let us rebuild the wall of Jerusalem, and we will no longer be in disgrace. . . . They replied, ‘Let us start rebuilding.’ So they began this good work. . . . I answered them by saying, The God of heaven will give us success” (Nehemiah 2:17–18, 20).

Nehemiah connected with the Jews on many relational levels: national pride, spiritual vision, renewed relationship with God, personal participation, common goal-setting, sense of ownership, and commitment to individual responsibilities.

Note: People will follow this leader beyond the stated authority. This leader allows work and ministry to be fun and joyful. If a leader stays too long on this level without rising, it will cause highly motivated people to become restless.

3. Production

Prior results are the basis for this level of leadership―people follow because of what has been accomplished for the organization or the church. People like the results they have seen. At this level, they not only enjoy a relationship with the leader but enjoy the results they have produced. There is fruit in the church and fulfillment in the people who participated in the journey. People love to follow a leader who gets things done. They love to be part of a team that wins. This describes leadership at this level.

A biblical example is David: “All the tribes of Israel came to David at Hebron and said, ‘We are your own flesh and blood. In the past, while Saul was king over us, you were the one who led Israel on their military campaigns. And the Lord said to you, ‘You will shepherd my people Israel, and you will become their ruler’’” (2 Samuel 5:1–2).

God defined David as “a man after His own heart” (1 Samuel 13:14). At each step of David’s journey with the Lord he gained conquests; though he suffered, he gained more influence and respect as he progressed. David offered a clear vision that united the people, sought to please the Lord first, solved problems, produced results, and shared responsibilities and the credit.

Note: This is the level of leadership that generates a sense of success. They like the leader and what they are doing. Problems are fixed with little effort because of momentum. People sense godly success for the church through this leader.

4. People Development

The ability to reproduce is the basis for this leadership—people follow because of what has been nurtured in them.

At this point, a leader gains a new level of authority. This individual personally impacts the lives of team members by pouring their life into them. He has not only been a minister but a mentor to others. She has developed the potential of key people. On this level, a leader reproduces him- or herself and brings about spiritual multiplication in the church.

A biblical example is Paul: “The things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable men who will also be qualified to teach others” (2 Timothy 2:2). Paul referred to Timothy as “my true son in the faith” (1 Timothy 1:2).

Paul mentored Timothy, Titus, Priscilla and Aquila, Luke, Onesimus, Philemon, and many others as he helped them move into their various works of ministry. He also instructed them to duplicate themselves in others. Paul’s strategies for explosive growth were to attract and select potential leaders, mentor and develop them as emerging leaders, give them assignments to prove their potential, and release them to serve and reproduce other leaders.

Note: This is the level where long-range growth occurs. A person’s commitment to developing leaders will ensure ongoing growth to the church and to individuals.

5. Personhood

Respect for life and service principles is the basis for this: people follow this leader because of who they are and what this one represents.

A biblical example is Samuel: “The Lord was with Samuel as he grew up, and he let none of his words fall to the ground. And all Israel from Dan to Beersheba recognized that Samuel was attested as a prophet of the Lord” (1 Samuel 3:19–20).

As a child, Samuel learned to recognize God’s voice. Hearing and walking with Him gave Samuel access to God Almighty, His word for His people, wisdom to teach and admonish those God would use, and eventually a record of truth and faithfulness as His witness. Samuel exhibited the heart of a servant leader, and all of Israel assembled to mourn at his death (1 Samuel 25:1). At this final step on the ladder to leadership, Samuel demonstrated faithful servanthood and a life of integrity; he was a consistent producer of leaders (Maxwell 2003c, 30).

Note: This step is reserved for leaders who have spent years growing people and organizations. Few make it to this level.

**Q:** What is the position level of leadership?

**A:** At this level people follow because your position requires them to follow. The influence of position leaders does not extend beyond the requirements of the job description. This leader relies on a title for people to follow him or her.

**Q:** What is the permission level of leadership?

**A:** At this level people follow because they have chosen to follow. People will follow the permission leader because they have given him or her permission to influence their direction.

**Q:** What is the production level of leadership?

**A:** At this level people follow the leader who has accomplished significant things for the organization. People choose to follow production leaders because they have achieved a history of success. This level will allow the leader to lead beyond the boundaries of his or her title. This level ultimately requires that a level of success be maintained or a higher level achieved.

**Q:** What is the people development level of leadership?

**A:** At this level people follow because of what the leader has done for them. This level provides a new level of authority that often extends into a long-term relationship. This level requires a commitment to delegate and mentor those the leader has selected.

**Q:** Define the personhood level of leadership.

**A:** At this highest level, people follow because of what the leader represents. Typically this level is reserved for those who have a long history of developing people. This level often focuses primarily on the leader’s character and the principles by which he or she leads.

### Climbing the Steps of Leadership

#### 9.3.1 — Describe the process of proceeding up the levels of leadership, and evaluate your current level of leadership.

Educators often use a diagram of a stairway when teaching the process of an endeavor. As you visualize the stairway to becoming an effective leader, remember these seven truths regarding your leadership climb (Maxwell 2003c, 31).

Seven Leadership Truths

1. The higher you go, the longer it takes.

2. The higher you go, the higher the level of commitment.

3. The higher you go, the easier it is to lead.

4. The higher you go, the greater the growth.

5. You never put aside the principles of the base level or the levels below your current level.

6. As a leader you will not be on the same level with all of those who work with you.

7. You must work to carry other leaders with you up the steps of leadership.

This is the point at which each leader and every potential and growing leader would logically ask, “How does one climb these leadership steps?” It takes asking God to grow you into an effective leader while you determine to develop confidence in your people skills for when it comes to people, every relationship is a chance to develop another person. (Note that the focus is outward toward others.) Teach yourself to be observant among your people and maintain a list of potential leaders you can invest in. Then prioritize your discipleship efforts, and you will find systematic ways to train people.

A point will come where you will need to select and develop key leaders. Then use the powerful impact of modeling—determine to live a life others want to imitate. Always remember that people are your most valuable asset.

How did Jesus do it? He did three things to develop His disciples.

1. He inspired them. The Lord Jesus was a visionary. He cast a vision for these men so they were willing to drop everything, leave their occupations, and follow Him. He inspired them with an incredible opportunity, a journey for their lives.

2. He instructed them. He taught them what to do.

3. He involved them. He let them do the task.

One of the great challenges is to let people work who cannot do it like we do it. God did not call you to accomplish all tasks. He called you to equip God’s people to lead and work. What you have to be willing to do is let other people do it even though they do not lead and work like you would or maybe not do it as well as you could, but that is not the point. The point is you are not supposed to be doing it. You are going to transfer everything you know about leadership; you are going to do everything to disciple those people, but you are going to have to let them do it.

Jesus was the greatest risk taker of all. He left this earth in the hands of a band of men who were imperfect and needed to grow more, but He had invested something in them and at some point He had to let them get their feet in the water. You can follow that example. Let them attempt it. If they fail, help them pick it back up. You are there to help them succeed. You are not there to take it back. John Maxwell wrote a book on this topic entitled, Failing Forward (2000a). No great thing has ever been done for God without failure at some point. It is what one does when it does not work that determines who succeeds and who does not. You learn from your mistakes, but you keep moving forward and growing.

Developing Leaders Versus Developing Followers

We end this chapter with the following pairs of insightful comparisons. Note these differences in developing leaders as opposed to developing followers. Establish now a priority to train leaders.

Leaders who develop followers need to be needed. Leaders who develop leaders want to be succeeded.

Leaders who develop followers want to be recognized. Leaders who develop leaders want to be replaced.

Leaders who develop followers focus on weakness. Leaders who develop leaders focus on strengths.

Leaders who develop followers hold on to position and power. Leaders who develop leaders give away position and power.

Leaders who develop followers grow their ministry by attracting more followers. Leaders who develop leaders grow their ministry by developing more leaders.

Leaders who develop followers grow through events. Leaders who develop leaders grow through process.

Leaders who develop followers grow outside in. Leaders who develop leaders grow inside out.

“Outside in” growth feeds off of charisma. “Inside out” growth feeds off of commitment.

If you develop a follower, you add. If you develop a leader, you multiply.

Leaders who develop followers impact only the people they touch. Leaders who develop leaders impact people beyond themselves.

## 10 - Cultivating People Skills in Your Leadership

### Leadership and People

#### 10.1.1 — Explain how a leader’s management of people will ultimately define the boundaries of their leadership success.

Here are four truths regarding leadership and people:

1. People are a church’s most appreciable asset .

2. A leader’s most important asset is people skills .

3. A good leader can lead various groups because effective leaders understand and relate to all people, regardless of their personalities.

4. You can have people skills and not be a good leader , but you cannot be a good leader without people skills .

Jesus told this story in response to a man who asked, “Who is my neighbor?” (Luke 10:29). He spoke of a man who was robbed and beaten alongside a road and left for dead. Soon, a couple of religious leaders walked by but never stopped. It is likely they were on their way to some religious activity. Then a Samaritan came by and helped the man, caring for him until he was well again. Jesus then asked the man to identify the neighbor in the story. He taught that relationships and ministry are not confined to your immediate circle of friends (Luke 10:36–37). He taught that relationships are more important than many spiritual activities we practice (Matthew 5:23–24).

Self-Perception and Serving Others

The Parable of the Good Samaritan illustrates how we treat others based on how we see ourselves. Notice the different ways the victimized man was treated in this story.

The robbers used people. They manipulated others. They saw the man as a victim to exploit .

The priest and Levite were law keepers. They were self-righteous. They saw the man as a problem to avoid.

The Samaritan was despised. He knew how it felt to be ignored. He saw the man as a person to be loved.

As a leader, you will have these three options too: to exploit people, to avoid people, and to love people. The goal is to look past their faults and see their needs (Maxwell 2003a, 30–31).

Leadership Is Relationships

Years ago, several Christian leaders met in a summit . Their goal was to summarize the Christian faith in a single sentence. They actually took the goal a step further. They summarized Christianity in a single word. The one word they chose was relationships .

What separates us from all the religions in the world is the centrality of relationships. Our faith is built around relationships, not creeds or disciplines. When Jesus was asked about the greatest commandment, He responded, “Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength” (Mark 12:30, a vertical relationship ), and to “love your neighbor as yourself” (Matthew 19:18, a horizontal relationship ). Jesus did not say, “By this will all men know that you are my disciples: that you have memorized fifty verses of Scripture.” Instead, He said that the way the world would know we are His disciples is how we handle our relationships. How well do we love people? John Maxwell reiterates a quote by Theodore Roosevelt: “People do not care how much you know, until they know how much you care” (1998, 107).

A Definition for Spiritual Leadership

A spiritual leader is one who assumes responsibility for the health and development of their relationships. Here are four word pictures that describe a spiritual leader:

1. Host . A spiritual leader is much like a host. Good hosts take initiative and make others feel comfortable. As a leader, you must host the relationships and conversations of your life. Leaders are not guests in relationships. Knowing what a good host does in their home, we ought to be able to do that same thing with people everywhere.

2. Doctor . A spiritual leader is much like a doctor. Good doctors ask questions. They probe until they see the need. As you attempt to discern people’s needs, ask questions until you discern their condition. Only then do you begin to address their needs. Do not give a prescription before a diagnosis.

3. Counselor . A spiritual leader is much like a counselor. Good counselors are active listeners and interpret what they hear. As a leader with solid people skills, you must become an active listener. You should nonverbally communicate that you understand the person and identify with them. We earn our right to speak by listening.

4. Guide . A spiritual leader is much like a guide. Guides do not merely fellowship with others; they get them to a destination. A leader’s people skills must result in their ability to take people to a destination. Our purpose is not to be liked by people, but to take them on a journey and to reach a goal they might not have reached alone (Maxwell 2003a, 31–32).

Leaders should take the appropriate role according to the needs of the person or persons they are leading. Our job is to connect with people so we can take them on the journey.

### Speaking with Truth and Grace

#### 11.1.1 — Explain why speaking with truth and grace is important as a leader.

“Instead, speaking the truth in love, we will in all things grow up into him who is the Head, that is, Christ.” (Ephesians 4:15)

Effective communication balances honesty with compassion. Jesus modeled this perfectly—He never compromised truth, yet His truth was always clothed in love. As leaders, our words must guide people toward growth and maturity without tearing them down. Truth without love wounds and alienates, while love without truth leaves people uncorrected and misled. Godly communication joins both—truth that corrects and love that heals.

Biblical Examples

Jesus and the Woman Caught in Adultery (John 8:1–11)

Jesus did not excuse sin, but neither did He condemn the woman. He first extended grace: “Neither do I condemn you.” Then He spoke truth: “Go now and leave your life of sin.” His words demonstrated that truth and love can coexist—grace restores and truth redirects.

Nathan Confronts David (2 Samuel 12:1–14)

Nathan spoke a hard truth to King David about his sin with Bathsheba, but he did so through a story that invited David to see his fault rather than shaming him publicly. His wise, Spirit-led approach brought conviction and repentance rather than rebellion or defensiveness.

Paul Confronts Peter (Galatians 2:11–14)

Paul boldly corrected Peter’s hypocrisy when he withdrew from Gentile believers out of fear. Though direct, Paul’s motive was to preserve the truth of the gospel, not to embarrass Peter. This example shows that truth spoken in love seeks restoration and alignment with God’s purposes.

Modern-Day Examples

A Pastor Confronting a Team Member

When a staff member consistently misses deadlines, a loving leader does not ignore the issue. Instead, they meet privately, affirm the person’s value to the ministry, and then clearly discuss the problem: “I believe in your potential, but we need to address these delays so the whole team can function well.” This blends affirmation (grace) with accountability (truth).

Parenting Through Conflict

A parent who corrects a child’s disrespectful behavior can model Ephesians 4:15 by calmly explaining why the behavior is wrong, expressing love, and guiding the child toward better choices. The goal is not punishment but restoration and growth.

Christian Leader in the Workplace

A supervisor discovers that an employee made an error that cost the company money. Instead of publicly shaming them, the leader meets privately, acknowledges the mistake, discusses lessons learned, and provides support to help the employee succeed. This approach strengthens both character and loyalty.

Peacemaking in the Church

During disagreements over ministry direction, a Christ-centered leader listens first, then speaks truthfully about concerns or vision differences without attacking character. The leader’s words model unity and humility, showing that grace does not mean avoiding truth—it means delivering it with love.

Practical Application

Be honest about expectations, mistakes, and feedback.

Transparency is the foundation of trust in any team. Leaders who communicate openly—about goals, challenges, and even personal weaknesses—create a culture where honesty is valued and accountability is natural. When people know what is expected of them, they can serve with clarity and confidence. When they receive constructive feedback, they grow rather than shrink.

Biblical Insight

Jesus consistently communicated clear expectations to His disciples. When Peter faltered, Jesus corrected him directly: “Get behind me, Satan! You are a stumbling block to me” (Matthew 16:23). Yet even in correction, Jesus reaffirmed Peter’s calling (John 21:15–17). Truth exposed the problem, but grace restored the person.

Leadership Application

Leaders must be clear, not vague.

Avoid softening truth to the point that your message is lost. If performance needs improvement, be specific about what must change—but balance that with affirmation of the individual’s value and potential. For example: “You have shown great creativity on this project, but the deadlines have been inconsistent. Let’s work together to create a system that helps you succeed.”

Transparency fosters loyalty, minimizes gossip, and strengthens the team’s moral fabric. People follow leaders they trust, and trust is built through consistent truth-telling.

Avoid harsh words; choose words that build up and instruct.

How something is said often determines how it is received. Even truthful words can wound if spoken harshly. Tone, timing, and body language all communicate as powerfully as the words themselves. The goal is not just to deliver truth but to deliver it in a way that the other person can receive it.

Biblical Insight

Proverbs 15:1 teaches, “A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger.” Jesus, though possessing all authority, often spoke with gentleness—inviting people into repentance rather than driving them away with condemnation. His interaction with the Samaritan woman (John 4) is a master class in this balance—He exposed her sin but did so in a way that drew her toward redemption.

Leadership Application

In moments of frustration, pause before responding.

Evaluate whether your tone reflects Christ’s heart. Practice active listening—repeat back what you heard before offering correction. For example: “I hear your concern about feeling overwhelmed. Let’s talk about what is causing that and how we can improve communication moving forward.”

Gentle communication disarms defensiveness and builds respect. Leaders who use their words to build rather than break become safe places for others to grow.

Pray before difficult conversations for wisdom and tone.

Before confronting issues, God’s wisdom must lead the conversation. Prayer softens our hearts, purifies motives, and reminds us that the goal is restoration, not vindication. Without prayer, truth can become a weapon; with prayer, it becomes a tool for healing.

Biblical Insight

Before choosing His disciples, Jesus spent the night in prayer (Luke 6:12–13). Prayer preceded every major decision and confrontation in His ministry. Likewise, James 1:5 instructs us, “If any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault.” Prayer allows the Holy Spirit to guide our words, ensuring they reflect God’s character rather than our emotions.

Leadership Application

Before a meeting or sensitive conversation, pause and pray:

“Lord, give me the right words, the right tone, and the right heart. Let my words bring light and not harm.”

Ask God to help you discern when to speak, how to speak, and what to speak.

Sometimes the Spirit will lead you to delay a conversation until your emotions have settled, ensuring your words build up rather than tear down.

Conversations led by prayer often result in reconciliation and renewed trust. They invite the Holy Spirit into the communication process, transforming ordinary dialogue into divine opportunity.

Speak for restoration, not for retaliation.

Many conversations fail, not because of what is said but because of why it is said. When our motive is to prove we are right, we speak from pride. When our motive is to heal and help, we speak from love. Leaders who communicate for restoration seek to bring people closer to Christ, not to win arguments.

Biblical Insight

Paul wrote, “Do not let any unwholesome talk come out of your mouth, but only what is helpful for building others up according to their needs, that it may benefit those who listen” (Ephesians 4:29). Even when he corrected the Corinthian church, his intent was to restore their fellowship with God, not to shame them (2 Corinthians 7:8–10).

Leadership Application

Before addressing a problem, ask:

“Is my goal to help this person grow or to vent my frustration?”

Reframe correction as an invitation to maturity:

“I see incredible potential in you, but this issue is standing in the way of your influence. Let’s fix it together.”

When truth is aimed at restoration, even difficult conversations become sacred opportunities for discipleship and growth.

Build a culture of grace-filled honesty.

When leaders model truth and grace consistently, it shapes the organizational culture. Team members begin to mirror that balance with one another—speaking truth courageously, yet compassionately.

Biblical Insight

The early church thrived in transparency: “All the believers were together and had everything in common. . . . They broke bread in their homes and ate together with glad and sincere hearts” (Acts 2:44, 46). Sincerity and grace built unity and trust among believers.

Leadership Application

Encourage open dialogue. Reward honesty. When mistakes happen, respond with understanding and solutions, not punishment. Celebrate when team members handle conflict biblically and maturely.

Result

A grace-filled culture does not avoid conflict—it redeems it. People feel safe to be honest because they trust they will be met with love and truth.

### Listening with Wisdom

#### 11.2.1 — Identify ten ways to listen with your ears and your heart.

“My dear brothers, take note of this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry.” (James 1:19)

Listening is as important as speaking—sometimes even more so. Wise leaders understand that true influence begins with understanding. By listening carefully, leaders discern needs, identify unspoken struggles, and make more informed, compassionate decisions. Listening communicates respect and value; it tells others that their voices matter. When leaders fail to listen, they miss opportunities for growth, problem solving, and deeper connection within their teams.

Jesus modeled this beautifully. Before responding to questions or making decisions, He often listened—to people’s stories, their pain, and even their silence. His attentiveness allowed Him to speak directly to the heart. In John 4, His conversation with the Samaritan woman demonstrates the power of listening. He heard not just her words but the longing beneath them, leading to her transformation and the evangelization of her entire village.

In modern leadership, listening remains a defining mark of humility and maturity. Effective leaders understand that every person on their team brings a perspective that can enhance the organization’s mission. Listening transforms monologues into meaningful dialogue and authority into collaboration.

Biblical Examples

Moses and Jethro (Exodus 18)

Moses listened to his father-in-law’s wise counsel about leadership structure. Because he was teachable, he implemented a system that allowed him to lead more effectively and sustainably.

Jesus and the Emmaus Disciples (Luke 24:13–35)

Jesus walked with the two disciples and listened to their confusion before revealing himself to them. His patient listening opened the door for revelation and renewed faith.

Modern Example

In today’s workplace, leaders like Satya Nadella, CEO of Microsoft, emphasize “empathy as the center of leadership.” Under his leadership, Microsoft shifted from a culture of “know-it-alls” to “learn-it-alls,” leading to significant growth and innovation. Listening—both to employees and customers—became a driving force for transformation.

Practical Application

Listening well does not happen by accident—it is a learned discipline that requires intentionality, humility, and practice. Great leaders do not just hear words; they discern meaning, emotion, and even what remains unspoken. The following practical applications will help you cultivate the skill of truly listening with both your ears and your heart:

Practice active listening in every setting.

Listening actively means giving others your full attention. Eliminate distractions—put away your phone, close your laptop, and make eye contact. Nod, affirm, and summarize what you have heard to show you are engaged. For example: “So what I’m hearing you say is . . .” This not only confirms understanding but also helps the speaker feel valued and heard.

Active listening communicates that people matter more than tasks, creating an atmosphere where honesty and collaboration thrive.

Resist the urge to interrupt or “fix” immediately.

Leaders often feel pressure to solve problems quickly, but sometimes the best leadership response is silence. Let others finish their thoughts without interruption. Resist mentally formulating your reply while they are still speaking.

Instead, focus on what they are feeling as much as what they are saying. When people feel truly heard, they often find their own clarity and solutions without the leader needing to intervene. This practice cultivates patience, empathy, and humility—key traits of Christlike leadership.

Listen for the heart behind the words.

Words are only part of the story. The tone, pace, and emotion behind them reveal much more. Pay attention to what is not being said—hesitations, sighs, or sudden changes in body language. These often indicate hidden concerns or deeper struggles.

Ask questions such as:

Can you tell me more about that?

What has been the hardest part of this for you?

How can I best support you in this?

By listening for the heart, you lead not only with skill but with compassion, bringing healing and strength to those you serve.

Create environments that encourage honest dialogue.

A listening leader cultivates safe spaces where people can speak openly without fear of judgment or retaliation. Encourage feedback regularly in team meetings, suggestion sessions, or informal one-on-ones. When feedback leads to real action, trust grows exponentially.

For example, if a team member raises a concern about communication gaps, do not dismiss it—address it and circle back with progress updates. Consistent follow-through proves that listening leads to change.

Reflect and pray before responding.

Listening is not only a relational act—it is a spiritual discipline. Before responding to a challenging conversation, pause to seek God’s wisdom. Pray silently, “Lord, help me hear not just their words, but their heart.”

Jesus often withdrew to quiet places to listen to the Father before taking major actions. Leaders who follow this model respond with discernment rather than impulse, ensuring their words align with God’s truth and grace.

Practice “listening leadership” beyond your team.

Extend your listening beyond your immediate circle. Spend time hearing from people at every level—volunteers, interns, or community members. Sometimes the most valuable insights come from those least likely to speak up.

When you intentionally invite diverse perspectives, you not only make wiser decisions but also strengthen unity and inclusiveness within the organization.

Use listening as a tool for conflict resolution.

When tension arises, most conflicts persist because neither side feels heard. As a leader, slow the conversation and restate each person’s point of view before offering solutions. For example, “Let me make sure I understand what you are saying . . .” This approach diffuses defensiveness and opens the door for understanding and reconciliation—reflecting the heart of Christ, the ultimate reconciler.

Evaluate your listening habits regularly.

Ask for honest feedback about your listening style. Invite trusted peers or team members to share if they feel heard and understood in conversations with you. Be open to their critique without defensiveness.

Consider journaling at the end of the week with prompts like:

Who did I truly listen to this week?

Where did I rush conversations or make assumptions?

How did my listening (or lack thereof) affect outcomes?

This self-assessment strengthens awareness and accountability in your leadership journey.

Listen to God with the same intentionality.

A leader who listens to God becomes better at listening to people. Set aside time daily for stillness—no agenda, no talking—just quiet awareness of His presence. As you grow sensitive to His voice through Scripture and prayer, you will develop the spiritual discernment to lead wisely and speak with authority that comes from intimacy with Him.

Model listening as a culture.

Make listening a defining part of your organization’s culture. Train your team to listen to one another, celebrate when people model it well, and integrate listening exercises into leadership development.

When listening becomes embedded in the DNA of a team, communication improves, morale strengthens, and unity deepens—creating a Christ-centered environment where every voice is valued.

3. What practical steps can I take this week to become a more attentive and patient listener—both with my team and in my relationship with God?

### Encouragement as a Leadership Practice

#### 11.3.1 — Identify a biblical example of encouragement.

“Therefore encourage one another and build each other up, just as in fact you are doing.” (1 Thessalonians 5:11)

Leaders must actively build people up. Encouragement is far more than a pleasant gesture—it is a strategic and spiritual act that strengthens the heart of a team. People thrive where they are appreciated. A leader’s role is not merely to direct but to develop; not just to assign tasks but to awaken potential. When leaders intentionally speak life into others, they become catalysts for growth and renewal.

Encouragement is not an optional kindness—it is a vital leadership tool that sustains those who serve alongside us. In seasons of pressure, uncertainty, or discouragement, a leader’s words can be the difference between someone giving up and pressing on. Encouragement breathes fresh courage into weary hearts—it reminds people that their work matters and that they matter. This practice reflects the nature of Christ himself, who constantly uplifted His disciples, affirmed their worth, and restored their confidence after failure.

A leader’s influence is multiplied when people feel seen, valued, and empowered. When individuals know their leader genuinely notices their effort, listens to their concerns, and celebrates their progress, they are far more likely to give their best. Feeling seen combats invisibility; feeling valued overcomes insecurity; feeling empowered releases initiative. Encouragement builds trust, and trust builds commitment.

Encouragement fosters loyalty, boosts morale, and often inspires people to achieve more than they thought possible. Teams led by encouraging leaders tend to innovate more, collaborate better, and recover faster from setbacks. A word of affirmation can turn doubt into determination, and a simple “I believe in you” can awaken confidence where fear once ruled. In many cases, encouragement is the spark that reignites purpose and passion in those who have grown weary in well-doing.

Encouragement communicates that you notice effort, understand struggles, and celebrate growth. Genuine encouragement sees beyond outcomes to the person behind the work. It acknowledges both progress and perseverance. When leaders encourage consistently, they cultivate a culture where people know that growth is recognized, grace is available, and their journey matters as much as their results.

Practical Application

Identify strengths and contributions.

Take time to observe your team members closely. Notice not only their successes but also their perseverance, creativity, and small acts of initiative. Affirm these strengths in ways that resonate with each person.

Celebrate achievements, both big and small.

Do not wait for milestones or big wins. Publicly acknowledge efforts in team meetings, and privately share a personal word of thanks. Even small accomplishments matter and reinforce a culture of positivity.

Use multiple forms of encouragement.

Written notes: A thoughtful email or handwritten note can leave a lasting impact.

Verbal affirmation: A simple, heartfelt compliment or expression of gratitude can shift someone’s entire day.

Public recognition: Highlighting a team member’s contribution in front of others not only encourages them but also sets a standard for the team.

### Leading with Storytelling and Testimony

#### 11.4.1 — Explain the importance of using storytelling and testimony in leadership.

“They overcame him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony; they did not love their lives so much as to shrink from death.” (Revelation 12:11)

Throughout Scripture, God uses stories to reveal truth and transform hearts. Jesus himself was a master storyteller—He used parables to illustrate spiritual truths in ways that connected with everyday life. Stories such as the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, and the Sower and the Seed continue to shape lives because they touch both the heart and the mind.

For Christian leaders, storytelling is not just a communication technique—it is a ministry tool. A well-told story builds bridges of understanding and trust. It helps people see themselves within God’s greater narrative. Personal testimony adds authenticity and authority to leadership, showing that God’s truth is not theoretical but lived and proven in real life.

When leaders share their own journeys—moments of faith, failure, perseverance, and redemption—they remind others that leadership is not about perfection but about dependence on God. Testimonies of God’s faithfulness can renew hope, spark courage, and draw teams into deeper unity around shared values and mission.

In modern contexts, great leaders—whether in ministry or the marketplace—understand that data informs, but stories inspire. Simon Sinek calls this “starting with why”—communicating purpose through story (Sinek 2009, 45–46, 183–185). When a leader communicates vision through testimony and narrative, it moves people from compliance to conviction.

Biblical Examples

Jesus and the Parables (Matthew 13)

Jesus used stories to reveal the kingdom of God in ways that people could grasp and remember.

Paul’s Testimony (Acts 26)

Paul frequently shared his conversion story to illustrate God’s grace and the power of redemption.

Modern Example

Corrie ten Boom, survivor of the Holocaust, used her testimony to inspire forgiveness and faith worldwide. Her stories carried more power than mere teaching because they flowed from deep personal experience.

Practical Application

Share your own experiences of God’s faithfulness and lessons learned.

Authenticity builds credibility. When leaders openly share their own experiences—especially the moments when God met them in weakness, confusion, or failure—they show that leadership is not about perfection, but about perseverance through God’s grace. A leader’s transparency reminds others that spiritual growth is a journey, not a destination. Sharing how you trusted God in a difficult decision, how He provided in an impossible situation, or how He turned pain into purpose gives others courage to do the same.

Example: A pastor sharing how God provided finances for a mission trip after a season of doubt may inspire the congregation to give or trust God with their own needs.

Use stories to illustrate vision, values, and principles for your team.

Stories breathe life into abstract ideas. Vision statements and core values can sound distant or corporate until they are anchored in real experiences. When leaders connect values—like integrity, compassion, or excellence—to personal or organizational stories, those values become memorable and meaningful.

For example, instead of simply stating, “We value serving others,” a leader might share about a volunteer who quietly went the extra mile to care for someone in need. That single story communicates the value more powerfully than a dozen reminders ever could.

Stories create emotional resonance. They help people not only understand the vision but feel it. When your team feels connected to the story behind the mission, they are more likely to embrace it with passion and commitment.

Encourage team members to share their testimonies to inspire one another.

A healthy leadership culture gives everyone a voice. Inviting team members to share testimonies of what God is doing in their lives fosters unity, mutual encouragement, and spiritual growth. It transforms meetings from routine updates into moments of ministry.

When people tell their stories, others see that God is working in diverse ways within the same community. This reminds everyone that they are part of a living, unfolding story of grace. Leaders who create opportunities for sharing testimonies cultivate an environment of faith, gratitude, and expectation.

Example: Begin staff or ministry meetings with a “God story” time, where one person shares a recent experience of God’s provision or answered prayer. Over time, this rhythm strengthens faith across the team.

Connect every story to a spiritual truth.

Storytelling becomes transformational when it is tied to Scripture and spiritual insight. After sharing a story, take time to draw out the biblical principle it illustrates. This ensures that storytelling remains Christ-centered and not self-centered.

Example: After sharing how God provided a way through a difficult situation, you might connect it to Philippians 4:19—“My God will meet all your needs according to his glorious riches in Christ Jesus.” This ties personal experience to eternal truth.

Doing so not only reinforces biblical principles but also helps your team interpret their own experiences through the lens of faith.

Make storytelling a rhythm of leadership communication.

Intentional leaders do not leave storytelling to chance—they plan it. Incorporate stories into sermons, team briefings, newsletters, or even casual conversations. A good rule of thumb: for every principle you teach, pair it with a story that brings it to life. You might share:

A personal story (something you have lived).

A ministry story (something the team or church experienced).

A biblical story (a truth illustrated from Scripture).

When this rhythm becomes part of your leadership style, you communicate not only information but transformation. People begin to see God’s faithfulness as an ongoing narrative in which they, too, have a role to play.

Celebrate transformation stories publicly.

Whenever possible, celebrate what God is doing in and through your people. Highlight testimonies of salvation, answered prayer, growth, or impact in ministry. Sharing these stories builds collective faith and gratitude. It also honors God by giving Him public glory for His work.

Example: At the end of a ministry season, invite participants to share how they saw God at work. Record short video clips or written stories and share them during services or on social media. These testimonies often reach far beyond the immediate audience—encouraging others to trust God in their own lives.

Storytelling and testimony are not simply communication tools—they are sacred acts of leadership. They turn ordinary moments into eternal messages, shaping hearts and strengthening faith. When leaders consistently weave stories of God’s faithfulness into their leadership, they remind everyone that they are part of something bigger than themselves—a living story authored by God.

Nehemiah 3–4

Nehemiah knew that a broken wall was a disgrace to God, but he also knew he could not rebuild it alone. He had to recruit a team of men to rebuild the wall around Jerusalem. If you read Nehemiah 3 – 4, you will see the number of people it took to accomplish such a task. Listed is name after name of families who undertook different tasks to rebuild various parts of the wall, and some men even acted as guards to protect the builders from their enemies who were trying to thwart their efforts.

From that day on, half of my men did the work, while the other half were equipped with spears, shields, bows and armor. The officers posted themselves behind all the people of Judah who were building the wall. Those who carried materials did their work with one hand and held a weapon in the other, and each of the builders wore his sword at his side as he worked. (Nehemiah 4:16–18)

Not only did they rebuild it, the team got the impossible task done in fifty-two days! Each person had specific tasks. Some played offense and laid bricks; others played defense and carried swords. Each person worked on a separate part of the wall, and everyone knew the role they played best.

Acts 27

Toward the end of Acts, Paul is taken prisoner and finds himself on a ship heading toward Rome to appear before Caesar. Along the journey they encounter a terrible storm and are shipwrecked. Despite being a prisoner, Paul steps forward as the coach who guides the passengers to safety.

After the men had gone a long time without food, Paul stood up before them and said: “Men, you should have taken my advice not to sail from Crete; then you would have spared yourselves this damage and loss. But now I urge you to keep up your courage, because not one of you will be lost; only the ship will be destroyed. Last night an angel of the God whose I am and whom I serve stood beside me and said, ‘Do not be afraid, Paul. You must stand trial before Caesar; and God has graciously given you the lives of all who sail with you.’ So keep up your courage, men, for I have faith in God that it will happen just as he told me. Nevertheless, we must run aground on some island.” (Acts 27:21 – 26)

When daylight came, they did not recognize the land, but they saw a bay with a sandy beach, where they decided to run the ship aground if they could. Cutting loose the anchors, they left them in the sea and at the same time untied the ropes that held the rudders. Then they hoisted the foresail to the wind and made for the beach. But the ship struck a sandbar and ran aground. The bow stuck fast and would not move, and the stern was broken to pieces by the pounding of the surf. The soldiers planned to kill the prisoners to prevent any of them from swimming away and escaping. But the centurion wanted to spare Paul’s life and kept them from carrying out their plan. He ordered those who could swim to jump overboard first and get to land. The rest were to get there on planks or on pieces of the ship. In this way everyone reached land in safety. (Acts 27:39 – 44)

Paul, as an inmate, became the chief influencer on the ship during the storm. He provided clear direction to everyone and gave them confidence that they would reach their goal.

### What Makes an Effective Team?

#### 12.2.1 — State and explain the internal characteristic necessary to be a part of an effective team.

We have summarized what is necessary to make up a good team, but it is time to dig deeper. What is required in a team in order to accomplish its goal? When you reduce it to the fundamentals, there are ten main components that make a team effective. As we work our way through these, evaluate the team you are currently on or a team you have been a part of in the past to determine how effective you really are.

1. An effective team cares for one another.

Think about it; what motivates you to be a team player? What pushes you to give 110 percent to your team? Is it because you feel a sense of duty or obligation? Is it a sense of have-to that provides the incentive? Usually not. Though such motivation may carry you so far, ultimately, the relationship you form with your team members motivates you to give your all for the team. Without feeling cared for, members will soon check out. Look at these three principles that help explain why relationship is so essential for the success of a team.

Three Principles of Relationships a Leader Needs to Know

1. The Second Mile Principle: People go the first mile because of a sense of duty. They go the second mile because of relationship.

2. The Connection Principle: Leaders always touch a heart before they ask for a hand. If you connect relationally with your followers, they will gladly join you in the work.

3. The Host Principle: Good leaders always host the conversations and relationships of their lives. They initiate as a host rather than wait for others to serve as a guest.

Jackie Robinson was the first African American to play in the major leagues. He played his first game for the Brooklyn Dodgers in April of 1947. He had the talent to play in the major leagues, but the first month was horrendous for him. Many of his own teammates did not want him on the team. There was a petition passed around the team declaring they did not want to play with a black man. Even in Brooklyn, he was jeered and booed by the hometown fans. When they traveled, he had to stay in separate hotels because during that time there were white men’s hotels and black men’s hotels. He could not eat meals at restaurants with the rest of the Dodgers either. He could not even drink from the same water fountain as the rest of the guys. Here he was a major league player, and he was alone. Reflecting on that time, Jackie said he did not think he would have made it if it had not been for Pee Wee Reese.

Pee Wee Reese was the captain of the team. He came up to Jackie a month after he was on the team, shook his hand, and apologized for the rest of the guys. From that time on, he took Jackie Robinson under his wing. It was not a moment too soon.

Jackie Robinson wrote later that the trip to Cincinnati to play the Reds was especially hard. Crosley Field was a bellowing frenzy of booing, hissing, and yelling. One spectator even threw a black cat onto the field. Then it happened. A ground ball was hit to him. He reached down to field, and he bobbled the ball. He lost his concentration. The fans went wild as they booed and screamed obscenities at him. Jackie just stood there with his head down waiting for the next pitch. It was at that moment that Pee Wee Reese decided he had to do something. He ran up to the pitcher’s mound to call a time-out. Then he walked over to Jackie. He put his arm around Jackie’s shoulder and stared at the crowd. Jackie said later that the noise subsided just like someone had turned down a loud radio. Jackie told Pee Wee Reese, “I think that arm around my shoulder saved my career.”

There are people near you right now who need an arm around their shoulders. Effective teams care for one another (Elmore 2001, 82).

2. An effective team knows and practices what is important.

An effective team is clear about its vision. It understands what it has been called together to do and knows its purpose. Everything the team does is in line with accomplishing that vision. No energy or time is wasted on things that do not contribute to the big picture. However, an ineffective team is unclear in its purpose and therefore in its priorities. The team may eventually accomplish its goal, but it may zigzag its way there, wasting time, energy, and resources along the way. Without clarity of vision, without knowing what is important and practicing it, the team’s actions are like the blind leading the way.

An elderly woman stood on a busy street corner, hesitant to cross because there was no traffic signal. As she waited, a gentleman came up beside her and asked, “May I cross over with you?” Relieved, she thanked him and took his arm.

The path they took was anything but safe. The man seemed to be confused as they dodged traffic and walked in a zigzag pattern across the street. “You almost got us killed!” the woman exclaimed in anger when they finally reached the curb. “You walk like you are blind!” “I am,” he replied. “That is why I asked if I could cross with you.”

3. An effective team grows together.

Effective teams never stay the same. Teams fall apart when they do not change and grow. As a leader you must minister, serve, lead, and live off of today’s growth, not yesterday’s truth. If you stop growing today, you will stop leading tomorrow. Great teams are never satisfied with where they are. They are always pushing for growth and development; they invite change.

There are several reasons growth is important for a team.

The leader’s growth determines the organization’s growth; you cannot lead beyond where you have grown yourself.

Life and society are constantly changing—if you are not addressing the changing needs of society, your work will be irrelevant.

The leadership team must be of one accord. You need to be moving forward in the same direction.

Leaders must improve to stay in front. If you are not improving, you will fall behind.

There are numerous, documented stories on the Internet regarding real-life stories of successful teamwork. These can be easily gathered and used to teach the benefits of teamwork to your staff and your congregation.

For instance, there was a community in California whose schools were in desperate need of repair. The schools had leaky roofs, asbestos was still present, and classroom space was extremely cramped. Parents and other concerned community residents met to list the needed improvements, and they drafted a bond issue . They set up a campaign headquarters where they organized phone work, sent out mass mailings, and educated the community on the schools’ specific needs. Their efforts resulted in the bond issue being passed with 88 percent of the voters casting ballots in favor of the improvements. Together the group accomplished its goal. One person could not have won the victory alone. Here is the truth about teamwork in a nutshell: Individuals win trophies, but teams win championships (Maxwell 2003b, 22).

4. An effective team has a chemistry that fits.

Have you ever just clicked with someone? You meet a person, start a conversation with them, and find you really connect. There is similar chemistry that occurs within a team when the right combination of people comes together. This is a match, a fit that enables the team members to work in harmony with one another. CBS radio newsman Charles Osgood told the story of two ladies who lived in a convalescent center. Each had suffered an incapacitating stroke. 5. An effective team places an individual’s rights below the team’s best interest.

Being part of a team means understanding that the whole (the team) is greater than its parts (the individuals). There is a cooperation that is necessary for a team to succeed, which requires individual sacrifice. There must be a transition from a me mindset to an us mindset (Elmore 2001, 85).

John Wooden was one of the greatest basketball coaches in NCAA history. As coach of the UCLA Bruins, he won more consecutive championships than anyone.

### Building a Strong Team

#### 12.3.1 — Identify the means of assembling an effective team, and evaluate the effectiveness of current and past team experiences.

6. An effective team knows everyone plays a special role.

It is easy to start playing the comparison game when you are on a team, to feel that your role is considerably less important or valuable compared to other team members.

Consider this: In March of 1981, President Ronald Reagan was shot by John Hinkley Jr. and was hospitalized for several weeks. Although Reagan was the nation’s chief executive, his hospitalization had little impact on the nation’s activity. Government and civil life continued.

On the other hand, suppose the garbage collectors in this country went on strike, as they did in Philadelphia some years ago. The city was not only in a literal mess, but also the pile of decaying trash quickly became a health hazard. A three-week, nationwide garbage strike could paralyze the United States.

The story leaves us to wonder: Who is more important, the President or a garbage collector? Scripture tells us to stop asking that question. We are all necessary, and often the most insignificant parts of the body of Christ are the most indispensable! (Read 1 Corinthians 12.)

God has arranged the parts in the body, every one of them, just as he wanted them to be. If they were all one part, where would the body be? As it is, there are many parts, but one body. The eye cannot say to the hand, “I don’t need you!” And the head cannot say to the feet, “I don’t need you!” On the contrary, those parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable, and the parts that we think are less honorable we treat with special honor. And the parts that are unpresentable are treated with special modesty, while our presentable parts need no special treatment. But God has combined the members of the body and has given greater honor to the parts that lacked it, so that there should be no division in the body, but that its parts should have equal concern for each other. If one part suffers, every part suffers with it; if one part is honored, every part rejoices with it. (1 Corinthians 12:18–26)

7. An effective team has a strong bench.

Think about the purpose of the bench in sports. It is not just a place to sit for supporting players who are not on the floor, but it serves a variety of other purposes as well. It is a place for strategy. During a time out, the team huddles at the bench and figures out the strategy for the next play. It is a place for rest. When a player on the floor gets tired, they sit on the bench to catch their breath while another player goes in for the exhausted teammate. It is a place for assistance. Whether getting signals from the coach to the players on the floor or receiving first aid, it is the place where team members look for help. The bench is a place that represents both unity and depth.

If these attitudes are present on a team, it has moved from a we mentality to an us against them mentality. A team with a good bench does not use it as a distinguishing point between team members. There is no better and best on a team, whether you are on the floor or on the bench. The bench is not a point of division, but it is a necessary place of support and strategy.

8. An effective team knows exactly where the team stands.

A team knows where it stands because there is a scoreboard everyone can see. Players glance at the scoreboard continually during a game to measure their progress. When the game is over, they know if they have won or lost.

Imagine you are at a basketball game. The players are running up and down the court, shooting the ball, blocking shots; occasionally one side steals the ball from the other. Everything seems normal in this game except for one thing: there is no rim on the backboard. The players shoot the ball, but we can only guess whether the ball would have gone in. At halftime no one knows for sure what the score is—it is all speculation.

Sounds absurd, right? What team would continue to play in a game if the team members did not know where they stood?

9. An effective team pays the price.

There is no success without sacrifice. If I succeed without sacrifice, then it is because someone who went before me made the sacrifice. If I sacrifice and do not see success, then someone who follows me will reap success from my sacrifice (Maxwell 2003b, 23).

Here is an account from Stu Webber that illustrates the need for sacrifice on a team:

We’d been running every day, but this was something else. We’d been sweating from the time we rolled out of the sack before daybreak, but now moisture drained from every pore in our bodies. Sure, this was the physical training stage of US Army Ranger School, and we expected exertion, even exhaustion. But this was no morning PT rah-rah in T-shirts.

We ran in full field uniform. As usual, the word was, “You go out together, you stick together, you work as a unit, and you come in together. If you don’t come in together, don’t bother to come in!”

Somewhere along the way, through a fog of pain, thirst, and fatigue, my brain registered something strange about our formation. Two rows ahead of me, I noticed one of the guys out of sync—a big, rawboned redhead named Sanderson. His legs were pumping, but he was out of step with the rest of us. Then his head began to loll from side to side. This guy was struggling, close to losing it.

Without missing a step, the ranger on Sanderson’s right reached over and took the distressed man’s rifle. Now one of the rangers was packing two weapons: his own and Sanderson’s. The big redhead did better for time. But then, while the platoon kept moving, his jaw became slack, his eyes glazed and his legs pushed like pistons. Soon his head began to sway again.

This time, the ranger on the left reached over, removed Sanderson’s helmet, tucked it under his own arm, and continued to run. All systems go. Our boots thudded along the dirt trail in heavy unison. Tromp-tromp-tromp-tromp.

Sanderson was hurting, really hurting. He was buckling, going down. But no. Two soldiers behind him lifted the pack off his back, each taking a shoulder strap in his free hand. Sanderson gathered his remaining strength and squared his shoulders. And the platoon continued to run. All the way to the finish line.

We left together. We returned together. And all of us were the stronger for it. Together is better (retold in Elmore 2001, 89).

10. An effective team says yes to the right questions.

The final component to discovering whether your team is effective or not is in being able to answer yes to the right questions. There are ten questions every team should ask and hopefully answer with a yes . Ask these ten questions about your team. If you respond with a no , reflect on ways you can change the no to a yes .

1. Do we trust each other?

When the concept of work teams and quality circles was new to the United States in the 1970s, critics said work teams could not succeed because individuals are conditioned to compete with each other, not to work together. Is your team proving the critics wrong by trusting each other and working together?

2. Do we have concern for each other?

3. Do team members feel free to communicate openly?

If a team were to have only one ground rule, a good one would be that members encourage each other to communicate openly and freely. In such an environment, a team can achieve anything.

4. Do we understand our team goals?

Without a clear focus on team goals, even the best teams will drift.

5. Do we have a commitment to those goals?

Teams need to believe in their goals and not simply see them as something abstract or demanded by outside forces.

6. Do we make good use of each member’s abilities?

Do members feel they are making a worthwhile contribution? Do we look for the best talents of each member and put them to work in the group?

7. Do we handle conflict successfully?

Conflict is natural in any group situation. Success is judged by how it is handled within the team as a whole. Is it redemptive? Is it constructive?

8. Does everyone participate?

One symptom of a team on the decline is when one or two members begin to dominate. The others allow it to happen because they have grown indifferent.

9. Do we respect our individual differences?

There are team members with whom you personally may never find yourself in agreement. But do you respect those team members nonetheless? It is the individual differences that make your team unique and special.

10. Do we like being members of this team?

True success depends on enjoying what you do, and that includes enjoying your team and liking the people with whom you work.

A few years ago, the Special Olympics were held in the Pacific Northwest. Hundreds of families traveled to participate with their disabled children. One race became an object lesson in teamwork for everyone. It was the 440-yard race.

Nine runners lined up at the starting blocks, each of them hoping to win the gold medal. When the gun fired, they took off. They were in a dead heat when one of the runners fell. He was shaken up, but he brushed himself off and attempted to catch up to the rest of the pack. In his haste, however, he stumbled and fell again. This time, he recognized there was no way to win the race. He could not even catch up to the pack. So he just sat there and began to cry.

One by one, the rest of the runners heard him crying. Within a moment, each of them stopped running and turned their attention toward their comrade. Suddenly, without any encouragement, they all sprinted across the field to their fellow runner and helped him up. Each of them hugged him, and then they did something unforgettable. They all joined hands and started toward the finish line. They decided to finish the race together.

The crowd was stunned. Everyone was quiet as they pondered who was going to get the medals. Suddenly, they broke into applause. They realized it did not matter. These physically and mentally disabled kids had taught the onlookers a huge lesson in teamwork.
