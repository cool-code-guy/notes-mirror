## 1 - Unchristian Conduct and Attitudes (1 Corinthians 1–4)

### Introduction and Background to 1 Corinthians (1:1–9)

#### 1.1.1 — Summarize facts related to the authorship of 1 Corinthians.

Summarize facts related to the authorship of 1 Corinthians.

Paul’s Contact and Correspondence with Corinth

According to Acts, Paul first visited Corinth during his second missionary journey, probably sometime in AD 50 or 51. This date is fairly certain because during his eighteen-month stay in Corinth, Paul appeared before Gallio, the newly appointed Roman proconsul, whose time in office has been established by archaeological finds as July 1, 1951, to July 1, 1952 (Murphy-O’Conner 1983, 141–152; Carson, Moo, and Morris 1992, 223–231).

Paul arrived in Corinth after a string of difficult and trying circumstances in the provinces of Macedonia and Achaia. At Philippi (Acts 16:12), he was miraculously delivered after being beaten and imprisoned. He narrowly escaped similar treatment in Thessalonica and Berea (Acts 17), having been driven out of the city by the Jews. At Athens, his spirit was deeply troubled by the widespread idolatry he encountered; his preaching of the gospel there was met with scorn and a meager response. It is little wonder he approached Corinth shortly thereafter in “weakness and fear, and with much trembling” (1 Corinthians 2:3).

Encouraged by Christ in a night vision (Acts 18:9–10), Paul labored in Corinth for about a year and a half (18:11) along with Priscilla and Aquila, who had recently come to Corinth from Rome due to persecution by the emperor Claudius (18:2). From there, he traveled to Ephesus, where he left Priscilla and Aquila and then departed for Palestine by ship. He arrived at Caesarea and went to Jerusalem to greet the church. Afterward, he traveled to Antioch from where he began his third missionary journey.

After crossing Asia Minor and strengthening the churches (18:23), Paul came again to Ephesus (19:1). Luke indicates that Paul’s stay in Ephesus was at least two and a half years (19:8–10; 20:31). Considering the time of his initial visit to Ephesus and allowing for travel time to and from Jerusalem, Paul’s stay at Ephesus ended late in the year AD 55. He wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus probably in the spring (before Pentecost, according to 1 Corinthians 16:8) of AD 55. While in Ephesus, he heard disturbing news of affairs in Corinth, prompting the following most likely historical sequence:

Paul writes a letter known as the “previous letter” (1 Corinthians 5:9) in which he warns the Corinthians not to associate with immoral individuals. The letter was apparently misunderstood and led to some confusion (5:10–11).

Perhaps as he hears of their misunderstanding, Paul also hears of other disorders in the church from members of “Chloe’s household” (1:11). In addition, a delegation from Corinth arrives consisting of Stephanus, Fortunatus, and Achaicus (16:17). The men bring a letter (7:1) outlining several questions the church wishes Paul to answer.

In response to the letter and report, Paul writes 1 Corinthians and sends it via Timothy (16:10–11; Acts 19:22).

Identify the problematic questions and issues Paul addresses in this letter.

Corinth: A Problem Church

A review of 1 Corinthians quickly reveals that Paul is responding to problems which had been reported by others (ch. 1–6) as well as questions about which the church had written him (ch. 7–16). In 7:1, he introduces his instruction on marriage with the words, “Now for the matters you wrote about.” Similar words appear in 7:25, 8:1, 12:1, and 16:1; all four of these passages reflect the Greek phrase peri de , which means “now concerning.” Paul’s words in 1:11 and 5:1 also indicate that these are his responses to questions and issues presented to him.

On closer examination, the questions above fall primarily into three categories: (1) moral/ethical; (2) liturgical ; and (3) doctrinal/theological. These questions for Paul were not trivial but represented problems that posed a serious threat to the congregation’s faith and spiritual well-being. As their “father” in the faith (1 Corinthians 4:15), Paul felt obligated to protect them by providing proper instruction and correction when necessary. Thus, 1 Corinthians presents a series of Paul’s instructions on matters of vital importance for the church.

While time and culture shape the church’s life and practice, this epistle contains principles that are as relevant today as they were in Paul’s time. A good student of 1 Corinthians will ask not only what Paul’s words meant to the original hearers but also what they teach us about God’s will for the church today.

Describe how the Corinthian church could be called a blessed church.

Corinth: A Blessed Church

It is odd that Paul describes a church with so many problems as greatly blessed. The source of that blessing is the grace of God given to the Corinthians in Christ Jesus (1 Corinthians 1:4). The grace of God refers to the entire work of salvation and the spiritual blessings that result from it. Therefore, these blessings have come to the Corinthians through the redemptive work of Jesus Christ—His crucifixion, resurrection, and ascension into heaven. Undoubtedly, this is why Jesus said it was to our advantage that He go away, referring to His ascension following the cross and resurrection. His departure allowed Him to send the Paraclete, or Holy Spirit, to us (John 16:7). Later, Paul explains that the Holy Spirit gives and distributes His gifts within the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12:4–13). Curiously, the Greek word for “spiritual gifts” ( charismata ) is derived from the word grace ( charis ). 1 Thus, spiritual gifts are “grace-gifts” or spiritual blessings of God’s salvation made possible by Christ’s redemption and the Holy Spirit’s ministry. Paul remarks that concerning spiritual gifts, the Corinthians lack none of these gracious endowments of the Holy Spirit (1:7).

In 1:5, Paul highlights two areas in which the Corinthians are richly blessed: (1) speech (Greek logos ) and (2) knowledge ( gnosis ). Greek culture and its fascination with philosophy and religion had taught them to esteem the ability to speak with eloquence and to acquire special or revealed knowledge (Fee 1987, 39–40). In his choice of terms here, Paul intends to include the gifts of utterance and revelation—tongues, interpretation, prophecy, the word (message) of knowledge, and the word of wisdom (1 Corinthians 12:8–10). The Corinthians had received Paul’s testimony concerning Christ, including the promise that Jesus would baptize them with the Holy Spirit (Matthew 3:11; John 1:33). Paul now indicates that this testimony was confirmed by the Holy Spirit, who has empowered the Corinthians with a full array of spiritual gifts in their collective life, worship, and service to Christ.

**Q:** Why do you think the Bible does not include Paul’s previous letter to the Corinthian church?

**A:** God has providentially worked to preserve the writings that He wanted in the New Testament. Just as John tells us that his Gospel could not contain all the works of Jesus (John 21:25), we should not be surprised to learn that the apostles wrote epistles other than those found in our New Testament canon.

**Q:** How does doctrinal instruction relate to a congregation’s health and life?

**A:** The two share a vital link. Behind the Corinthians’ errant practices were beliefs, values, and attitudes that had to be corrected with doctrinal instruction.

**Q:** How can a church with so many problems receive such a wealth of spiritual gifts?

**A:** God gives charismata or grace-gifts to edify the church and empower Christian life, service, and worship within the body of Christ. The gifts are not earned or achieved; the level of spiritual maturity or godliness has no bearing.

### Divisions in the Church: Causes and Results (1:10–3:23)

#### 1.2.1 — Explain how and why exalting certain persons caused divisions at Corinth.

Explain how and why exalting certain persons caused divisions at Corinth.

Exalting Personalities

After his greeting and introduction, Paul begins with a formal appeal for unity. This is the first stated concern among the many he might have mentioned. He strongly urges them to “agree with one another” and “be united in mind and thought” so that there would be no divisions (Greek schismata ) among them (1 Corinthians 1:10). Paul wanted the Corinthians to be complete in their faith, and the lack of unity was hindering their spiritual growth. From Chloe’s people, he learned that their disunity had resulted in quarrels.

Pentecostal scholar Gordon Fee challenges the traditional view that Paul is writing to a church divided into factions or “parties.” He prefers to see the divisions as representing a conflict between Paul, the founder, and the entire community, although he admits that texts like 1:10–12, 3:4–5, and 11:18–19 clearly present internal divisions. Later, Fee acknowledges that the divisions resulted from the opinions of various leaders and that Chloe’s people would have declared themselves as “those who followed Paul” (Fee 1987, 4–8, 47–66). In my view, this seems to define a schism or party of divided opinion. How defined such groups were may be debatable, but it appears certain that they existed. Stanley Horton suggests that such divisions were not real but rather in the making (1999, 23). Yet the texts from 1 Corinthians cited above seem to indicate otherwise, especially 11:18, where Paul says the divisions ( schismata ) are “among them.” Moreover, such factions ( heireseis ) must be among them to identify those who are approved of God (11:19). The term heireseis can refer to either a group or sect (Acts 5:17) or the false teaching of such a group (1 Peter 2:1). The context in 1 Corinthians seems to favor the former sense and makes divisions and factions parallel if not equivalent terms. In any case, even if Paul meant these terms to refer to differing teachings, it is impossible to separate the teachings from those he identifies as holding to them.

Although the passage does not specify the exact focus of the dissension, the whole epistle seems to indicate that it involved conflicting teachings and practices within the church. The source of differing opinions revolved around competing loyalties to particular personalities. Rival claims to be of Paul, of Apollos, or of Cephas reveal that camps within the church were forming around the teachings of various leaders. These preferences fostered spiritual pride, as indicated by Paul’s rebuke of their pride over who had administered their baptism (1 Corinthians 1:14–15). Paul reminds them that Christ is not divided. Christ alone—in whose name they were baptized—is the source of their redemption (vv. 13–16).

Paul knew these divisions not only fostered pride but also undermined the preaching of the gospel (v. 17). By exalting human personality, the Corinthians placed dangerous emphasis on the manner or form of preaching rather than on the cross-centered content of the gospel. Paul’s warning echoes down the corridors of time to those in our day who prefer preaching style over substance.

Clarify how the Corinthians misunderstood the wisdom of God.

Misunderstanding God’s Wisdom

Along with exalting certain individuals, the divisions in the Corinthian church came from a wrong view about the gospel itself. In 1 Corinthians 1:17, Paul states that Christ did not send him to preach the gospel “with words of human wisdom.” Here, Paul uses the Greek phrase en sophia logou (literally, “by wisdom of word or speech”), which can refer to either the form or content of speech. While Paul can and does have the form of his words in mind (2:1, 4), he does not separate the form from the content of his message (2:2).

Ancient Greeks thought most highly of those who sought wisdom and truth through the mind and reason. In fact, the term philosopher means “lover of wisdom.” In 1 Corinthians 1, Paul draws a sharp contrast between two kinds of wisdom: the wisdom of the world (or “this age,” v. 20) and the wisdom of God (v. 21). Framing the contrast in terms of eschatology, Paul presents no common ground between the two, since through the world’s wisdom the world did not come to know God (v. 22). God’s wisdom belongs not to this age but to a new age that began with the coming of Christ and, specifically for Paul, with Jesus’ death and resurrection. 2 Now, through the preaching of the gospel, the wisdom of God can be heard in the message of the cross (vv. 17–18). The contrast is such that those who belong to this age see only foolishness in this message.

Paul explains why the message of the cross hides God’s wisdom from both Jew and Greek. Jews, who seek signs of God’s wisdom and power, find the cross intolerable. How can God’s chosen Messiah (Greek Christos , “the anointed one”) be crucified? Such a thought scandalized them, as their own Scriptures taught that “anyone who is hung on a tree is under God’s curse” (Deuteronomy 21:23; Galatians 3:13). To the Greeks, death belonged in the realm of mortals and the material world. As such, it was inferior to the realm of the spirit to which the immortal gods belonged. How could God’s wisdom and power (1 Corinthians 1:24) be revealed through something as weak and human as death?

But Paul reminds the Corinthians that the world’s wisdom is at odds with God’s; it will be set aside and ultimately destroyed (v. 1:19; Isaiah 29:14). In fact, in Christ’s crucifixion lies God’s design for the Church. God has chosen to reveal His wisdom and power through human weakness and what is regarded as folly to this world. In short, He has chosen the “nobodies” of this world to preach the message of the cross of Christ, in whom reside not only God’s wisdom but also our righteousness, holiness, and redemption (v. 30).

The Corinthians had likely misunderstood both the nature of God’s wisdom and its source. Given their fondness for philosophy, they may have believed that such wisdom was acquired by intellectual pursuit and inquiry. In 1 Corinthians 2, Paul corrects this notion by teaching that such wisdom is spiritual in two senses of the word:

1. God’s wisdom does not come from intellectual inquiry but through revelation from God’s Spirit. Only the Spirit, who “searches all things, even the deep things of God” (2:10), knows the thoughts of God (v. 11). While God’s wisdom is hidden to this world (v. 8), God has revealed it to us “by his Spirit” (v. 10). Christians have not received the spirit of this world but the Holy Spirit, who is from God (v. 12). He alone can impart God’s wisdom to the human heart.

2. God’s wisdom is spiritual in nature. That is, it has a spiritual dimension that demands faith (1 Corinthians 2:5) and a new nature to receive and understand. Jesus taught Nicodemus, “Flesh gives birth to flesh, but the Spirit gives birth to spirit” (John 3:6). Flesh, as John uses it here, speaks of natural human life outside the eternal life of God, which is supernatural. In the same passage, Jesus explained that only those who are born again (or “from above”) can enter the kingdom of God (v. 5). Those who have experienced this rebirth are said to be “born of the Spirit” (v. 8). Without mentioning a spiritual rebirth, Paul nonetheless builds on Jesus’ teaching. Only those who are “spiritual” by nature can understand the truths the Spirit reveals and teaches. The natural human, relying only on human intellect and wisdom, cannot accept and understand these Spirit-taught truths because they are “spiritually discerned” (1 Corinthians 2:14).

In 1 Corinthians 3, Paul turns his attention to the consequences that await the Corinthians if their divisions are not addressed and resolved. #### 1.2.2 — Clarify how the Corinthians misunderstood the wisdom of God.

Explain how and why exalting certain persons caused divisions at Corinth.

Exalting Personalities

After his greeting and introduction, Paul begins with a formal appeal for unity. This is the first stated concern among the many he might have mentioned. He strongly urges them to “agree with one another” and “be united in mind and thought” so that there would be no divisions (Greek schismata ) among them (1 Corinthians 1:10). Paul wanted the Corinthians to be complete in their faith, and the lack of unity was hindering their spiritual growth. From Chloe’s people, he learned that their disunity had resulted in quarrels.

Pentecostal scholar Gordon Fee challenges the traditional view that Paul is writing to a church divided into factions or “parties.” He prefers to see the divisions as representing a conflict between Paul, the founder, and the entire community, although he admits that texts like 1:10–12, 3:4–5, and 11:18–19 clearly present internal divisions. Later, Fee acknowledges that the divisions resulted from the opinions of various leaders and that Chloe’s people would have declared themselves as “those who followed Paul” (Fee 1987, 4–8, 47–66). In my view, this seems to define a schism or party of divided opinion. How defined such groups were may be debatable, but it appears certain that they existed. Stanley Horton suggests that such divisions were not real but rather in the making (1999, 23). Yet the texts from 1 Corinthians cited above seem to indicate otherwise, especially 11:18, where Paul says the divisions ( schismata ) are “among them.” Moreover, such factions ( heireseis ) must be among them to identify those who are approved of God (11:19). The term heireseis can refer to either a group or sect (Acts 5:17) or the false teaching of such a group (1 Peter 2:1). The context in 1 Corinthians seems to favor the former sense and makes divisions and factions parallel if not equivalent terms. In any case, even if Paul meant these terms to refer to differing teachings, it is impossible to separate the teachings from those he identifies as holding to them.

Although the passage does not specify the exact focus of the dissension, the whole epistle seems to indicate that it involved conflicting teachings and practices within the church. The source of differing opinions revolved around competing loyalties to particular personalities. Rival claims to be of Paul, of Apollos, or of Cephas reveal that camps within the church were forming around the teachings of various leaders. These preferences fostered spiritual pride, as indicated by Paul’s rebuke of their pride over who had administered their baptism (1 Corinthians 1:14–15). Paul reminds them that Christ is not divided. Christ alone—in whose name they were baptized—is the source of their redemption (vv. 13–16).

Paul knew these divisions not only fostered pride but also undermined the preaching of the gospel (v. 17). By exalting human personality, the Corinthians placed dangerous emphasis on the manner or form of preaching rather than on the cross-centered content of the gospel. Paul’s warning echoes down the corridors of time to those in our day who prefer preaching style over substance.

Misunderstanding God’s Wisdom

Along with exalting certain individuals, the divisions in the Corinthian church came from a wrong view about the gospel itself. ### Divisions in the Church: The Cure (4:1–21)

#### 1.3.1 — Describe Paul’s view of ministry as stewardship before God.

As Gordon Fee observes, the streams of internal divisions at Corinth seem to flow into a river of opposition against Paul (1987, 5–6, 8). Worldly wisdom and pride moved the Corinthians to exalt one leader over another (1 Corinthians 3:22), promoting Paul’s admonishment to stop boasting in people (v. 21). This worldly comparison of leaders seems to have resulted in criticisms of Paul. Thus, in 1 Corinthians 4, Paul responds to this negative criticism, providing insight and instruction on how to prevent the divisions so troublesome in Corinth.

Describe Paul’s view of ministry as stewardship before God.

Steward of Ministry

Paul reminds the Corinthians that he and those with whom he is being compared (Apollos and Cephas) are mere men and servants of Christ. Their ministry of preaching the gospel is a stewardship that God gave them. As caretakers of this trust, they must be faithful, but whether they are faithful or not is determined or judged by God alone. It is He who has entrusted them with the gospel, and it is He who will judge them at the appointed time.

Consequently, Paul does not let criticism overwhelm him and even avoids self-criticism (1 Corinthians 4:3), which can be equally condemning. While his conscience is clear before God, Paul knows the only real proof of innocence is the judgment of the Lord reserved for the time of Christ’s coming. At that time, God will bring the hidden things to light, even the unseen motives of individuals’ hearts. Paul then exhorts the believers not to get ahead of the Lord and pass judgment now. Obviously, such a premature judgment is not even theirs to make. Paul’s statements here remind us of his rebuke of the Romans for judging another man’s (that is, the Lord’s) servant in matters of conscience (Romans 14:1–12).

Summarize Paul’s instructions to the Corinthians to remedy their divisions.

Lessons from Paul

To avoid the worldly boasting that fosters divisions, Paul declares that the Corinthians must heed his teaching, which conforms not to the world’s wisdom but to “what is written” (the Old Testament Scriptures). 3 Worldly wisdom produces the kind of pride that sets one person’s ministry against another (1 Corinthians 4:6).

Thus, the distinctions and comparisons the Corinthians have drawn reveal both pride and ignorance. Paul asks, “What do you have that you did not receive? And if you did receive it, why do you boast as though you did not?” (v. 7). To emphasize his point, Paul draws some comparisons of his own to disclose the foolish pride of the Corinthian leaders. With irony and sarcasm, he contrasts their exalted claims and arrogant attitudes with the humility, humiliation, and sufferings of himself and other apostles. He writes not to shame them but to show that, as their father in the faith, he loves them and wants to warn them away from a destructive path. He seeks to correct their course to promote their spiritual maturity and well-being.

As a spiritual parent, Paul realizes that the most effective instruction requires more than words; it needs modeling. Therefore, he calls the Corinthians to imitate him in his service to Christ (1 Corinthians 4:16). To help them, Paul sends one of his beloved and faithful children in the faith to Corinth. Having been reared at Paul’s side, Timothy is well acquainted with Paul’s life and teachings. He can testify to the integrity of Paul’s life and example and its consistency with what he teaches everywhere in the churches. The combination of word and deed is by far the most powerful form of instruction. Today, we would do well to learn that our good words can fall on deaf ears when they are drowned out by a life of contradiction and compromise.

Some in Corinth arrogantly declared Paul’s warnings to be mere words. Perhaps they interpreted his delay in coming to them as reluctance to back up his words with authoritative action. But Paul sets the record straight: he is coming soon. When he comes, he will do some judging of his own to see if the words of the arrogant leaders are matched by deeds of power. If they are truly preaching the truth of the gospel, the power of the kingdom of God that accompanies its preaching (v. 20) should be present.

Paul’s preaching and ministry were consistent. He did not teach of a power he had not experienced. As he stated earlier, his “message and preaching were not with wise and persuasive words, but with a demonstration of the Spirit’s power” (2:4).

Steward of Ministry

Paul reminds the Corinthians that he and those with whom he is being compared (Apollos and Cephas) are mere men and servants of Christ. Their ministry of preaching the gospel is a stewardship that God gave them. As caretakers of this trust, they must be faithful, but whether they are faithful or not is determined or judged by God alone. It is He who has entrusted them with the gospel, and it is He who will judge them at the appointed time.

Consequently, Paul does not let criticism overwhelm him and even avoids self-criticism (1 Corinthians 4:3), which can be equally condemning. While his conscience is clear before God, Paul knows the only real proof of innocence is the judgment of the Lord reserved for the time of Christ’s coming. At that time, God will bring the hidden things to light, even the unseen motives of individuals’ hearts. Paul then exhorts the believers not to get ahead of the Lord and pass judgment now. Obviously, such a premature judgment is not even theirs to make. Paul’s statements here remind us of his rebuke of the Romans for judging another man’s (that is, the Lord’s) servant in matters of conscience (Romans 14:1–12).

Summarize Paul’s instructions to the Corinthians to remedy their divisions.

Lessons from Paul

To avoid the worldly boasting that fosters divisions, Paul declares that the Corinthians must heed his teaching, which conforms not to the world’s wisdom but to “what is written” (the Old Testament Scriptures). 3 Worldly wisdom produces the kind of pride that sets one person’s ministry against another (1 Corinthians 4:6).

Thus, the distinctions and comparisons the Corinthians have drawn reveal both pride and ignorance. Paul asks, “What do you have that you did not receive? And if you did receive it, why do you boast as though you did not?” (v. 7). To emphasize his point, Paul draws some comparisons of his own to disclose the foolish pride of the Corinthian leaders. With irony and sarcasm, he contrasts their exalted claims and arrogant attitudes with the humility, humiliation, and sufferings of himself and other apostles. He writes not to shame them but to show that, as their father in the faith, he loves them and wants to warn them away from a destructive path. He seeks to correct their course to promote their spiritual maturity and well-being.

As a spiritual parent, Paul realizes that the most effective instruction requires more than words; it needs modeling. Therefore, he calls the Corinthians to imitate him in his service to Christ (1 Corinthians 4:16). To help them, Paul sends one of his beloved and faithful children in the faith to Corinth. Having been reared at Paul’s side, Timothy is well acquainted with Paul’s life and teachings. He can testify to the integrity of Paul’s life and example and its consistency with what he teaches everywhere in the churches. The combination of word and deed is by far the most powerful form of instruction. Today, we would do well to learn that our good words can fall on deaf ears when they are drowned out by a life of contradiction and compromise.

Some in Corinth arrogantly declared Paul’s warnings to be mere words. Perhaps they interpreted his delay in coming to them as reluctance to back up his words with authoritative action. But Paul sets the record straight: he is coming soon. When he comes, he will do some judging of his own to see if the words of the arrogant leaders are matched by deeds of power. If they are truly preaching the truth of the gospel, the power of the kingdom of God that accompanies its preaching (v. 20) should be present.

Paul’s preaching and ministry were consistent. He did not teach of a power he had not experienced. As he stated earlier, his “message and preaching were not with wise and persuasive words, but with a demonstration of the Spirit’s power” (2:4).

## 2 - Christian Conduct (1 Corinthians 5–7)

### Church Discipline (5:1–13)

#### 2.1.1 — Describe the moral situation that called for immediate discipline.

Describe the moral situation that called for immediate discipline.

The Demand for Discipline

Among the problems reported to Paul was a shocking case of immorality—a man was living with his father’s wife! We assume from Paul’s description that this was not the man’s mother but his stepmother. Still, incest was forbidden by Jewish law (Leviticus 18:6–18; Deuteronomy 22:30; 27:20–23).

We can almost hear Paul gasp as he remarks that this kind of immorality is not even practiced among pagans. In the Greco-Roman world, incest between parent and child was universally condemned (Keener 1993, 462). Hence, Paul’s statement was significant because the Greeks were notorious for their sexual sins, and no Greek city had a worse reputation than Corinth. One Greek writer even coined the term korinthiazein , meaning “to act like a Corinthian” or to commit fornication (Fee 1987, 2). No doubt the presence of pagan temples and their accompanying temple prostitution contributed to the city’s reputation. Although scholars generally consider the ancient historian Strabo’s account of a thousand prostitutes at the temple of Aphrodite to be an exaggeration, few question that Strabo’s description of a city filled with sexual excess is essentially correct (Fee 1987, 3).

It should not be surprising that Corinthian Christians raised in this immoral climate would struggle with sexual purity. However, what shocks Paul is not only that this kind of sin existed but that it was tolerated by the community of believers. Clearly, here is a situation where the church should have exercised discipline.

Identify four reasons church discipline is needed today.

Benefits of Church Discipline

Paul pointed out that the Corinthians’ pride kept them from mourning or being grieved over such sin in their midst. They arrogantly tolerated this sin instead of disciplining the man by putting him out of the church and forbidding fellowship. Had they acted with moral outrage and discipline, they would have called a halt to this sin and prideful rebellion.

In their failure to discipline, the Corinthians not only tolerated this sin but encouraged it. As any parent knows, proper and consistent discipline discourages misbehavior and establishes a standard of acceptable behavior. In the church, discipline curtails sin by showing observers what will and will not be accepted. It also teaches the moral standards of God’s Word and allows His people to hide that Word in their hearts so they might not sin against Him (Psalm 119:11).

Paul suggests that discipline can also be preventative. In Jewish literature, yeast (leaven) commonly depicted the corrupting influence of sin (Barclay 1975, 45). In using this symbol, Paul expressed his concern that failure to discipline threatens the moral purity of the entire community. Sin left unchallenged and undisciplined is like yeast that spreads its effects throughout the whole batch of dough (1 Corinthians 5:6). Paul calls for the discipline of putting the offending brother out of the church (vv. 2, 13). To do so would preserve the congregation’s moral purity by preventing the spiritual contamination from spreading.

Finally, Paul gives the most important reason why he advises discipline for the offending brother: His motive is not to punish but to restore. His purpose is redemptive in that the discipline is to be carried out “so that the sinful nature may be destroyed and his spirit saved on the day of the Lord” (v. 5). How the discipline relates to the destruction of the sinful nature (Greek sarx , “flesh”) is not spelled out. However, it is clear that Paul identifies putting the man out of the church with turning him over to Satan. Paul probably sees such discipline as subjecting this man to the destructive work of Satan, who will take advantage of his rebellion and bring some sort of sickness or affliction on him. But Paul sees a redemptive side to this development. The saving of his spirit can occur only if the man repents. Consequently, Paul most likely sees the ensuing affliction as bringing this man to repentance.

**Q:** Explain why even unbelievers have a moral conscience (see 1 Corinthians 5:1).

**A:** Although marred by sin, a moral conscience is still part of the image of God given to unbelievers. As Paul notes in Romans, although they do not possess the Law (as their moral code), they show through their conscience the work of the Law “written on their hearts” (Romans 2:14–15).

**Q:** From where do Christians receive instruction on sexual and other morality?

**A:** Christians have not only the Scriptures to enlighten them but also the indwelling presence of the Holy Spirit to guide them.

**Q:** Did Jesus’ doctrinal instruction to the early believers go beyond matters of faith? Explain.

**A:** Yes. Jesus’ teaching included what constituted “righteousness” in God’s kingdom (Matthew 5–6). The Law already defined righteousness for the Jew, but Jesus taught a righteousness that went beyond the legal code practiced by the Pharisees and scribes (Matthew 5:20).

**Q:** How could such a gross sin as incest be tolerated in the Corinthian church?

**A:** Although Paul claims that pride was the root of their tolerance, he does not say what they were proud of. From historical and cultural studies, we learn that the Greeks tended to view the body as separate and somewhat irrelevant to the human soul or spirit. Thus, they may have viewed themselves as “spiritual” people for whom sins of the flesh were irrelevant.

**Q:** How does failure to discipline in the church help spread sin?

**A:** Believers (especially the young and immature) who are aware of sin but see no discipline by the church’s leadership can mistakenly conclude that the behavior is not sinful or is irrelevant to their Christian walk of faith.

### Legal Disputes and Immorality (6:1–20)

#### 2.2.1 — Explain how and why Paul uses eschatological grounds to rebuke the Corinthians.

Explain how and why Paul uses eschatological grounds to rebuke the Corinthians.

Exercising Discipline and Judgment

In chapter 6, Paul continues to exhort the church to practice discipline. Whereas in the previous chapter the issue was very specific, involving adultery and incest, now the apostle exhorts them to settle internal disputes by judging matters of everyday life (6:4). Curiously, the reason Paul gives for practicing such judgment is eschatology. That is, the Corinthians should not hesitate to judge earthly matters because they are destined to participate in God’s end-time and final judgment of the world.

Roman civilization had a high regard for law and, in fact, saw all of life as falling in some category of law (Ferguson 1993, 21, 61–62). Furthermore, Romans were fond of going to court and settling civil disputes. Corinthian Christians who came from this background would have at first seen nothing wrong with taking a brother to court. But to Paul, such a practice was unthinkable. In effect, he asks, “How dare you do such a thing?” He is both shocked and indignant at their actions. His reaction seems to stem from the thought of asking unbelievers to judge Christians (1 Corinthians 6:1, 6).

Paul argues that if the Corinthians are destined to judge the world (v. 2), why do they ask unbelievers to judge the saints of God? If they will one day preside in a heavenly court that judges angels, 1 how is it that they cannot see clearly enough to judge earthly matters among fellow believers? Certainly it makes no sense to turn such matters over to those who have no standing in the church, who have no part in the end-time judgments but rather will be judged in them. 2

Paul’s counsel is that it is better to suffer injustice or wrong than to drag a brother into court and ask unbelievers to settle your dispute. Paul’s words leave us with an uncomfortable question: what kind of witness does the world receive when a Christian goes to court against a fellow believer?

Identify and illustrate ways Christians’ end-time destiny should affect their present conduct.

Not Forfeiting the Eternal Inheritance

The mention of unbelievers raises another issue for Paul. Earlier (6:1), he referred to them as “ungodly” (Greek adikoi ). Now he warns the Corinthians that no unrighteous person will inherit the kingdom of God. Paul lists ten types of sinners who fit that category. It is not surprising, given the moral climate of Corinth, that four of the ten have to do with sexual sins.

By issuing this warning both at the beginning of 6:9 and the end of 6:10, Paul uses a well-known literary device called inclusio to emphasize the warning’s importance and urgency. His reasons for including it twice are clear: the Corinthians are not to engage in sinful lifestyles that characterize the unrighteous who will not receive an eternal inheritance in God’s kingdom. To be sure, some had engaged in such sins, but God had redeemed them.

They now stand cleansed from sin, consecrated to God, and made holy and righteous before Him (v. 11). To go back and live such sinful lifestyles would be to deny the work of God’s grace and salvation in their lives, to live as if they still belonged to this world which is passing away and destined for judgment (7:31; 1 John 2:8). Ultimately, to live in sin is to risk forfeiting one’s inheritance in the kingdom of God. If the church will discipline sin, it will maintain spiritual purity within its ranks by upholding and reinforcing the moral standards of God’s kingdom.

Glorifying God in Our Bodies

At the beginning of verse 12 and again in verse 13, Paul is probably quoting a popular saying of Corinthian Christians: “Everything is permissible for me.” In arrogance, some Corinthians thought that because of their new “spiritual” status, they were beyond moral restraints or the need to curtail bodily appetites. Paul agrees that there is freedom in Christ, but it is not an unbridled freedom to do whatever we want to do with our bodies. He taught elsewhere (Galatians 5:13) that Christians should not use their freedom to gratify their sinful nature. In fact, such a view of freedom would bring them right back into bondage to that nature, giving it mastery of them.

However, the real tragedy of adopting a libertine lifestyle is that it ignores a profound truth of the Christian life: the physical body is an integral part of a person and is important to God. It is so important that God will resurrect it by His power (1 Corinthians 6:14). The body is “for the Lord, and the Lord for the body” (v. 13). Thus, a person cannot commit immorality without consequence to his or her relationship to Christ. Such sin not only offends God but also constitutes a sin against one’s own body that is intended to be used in the service and worship of the Lord (v. 19).

One final truth eludes the libertine Corinthian: a spiritual unity exists between the believer and the Lord. This unity encompasses not just the believer’s spiritual or non-material self but the totality of his or her person, including the physical body. Given this mystifying truth, when a believer engages in fornication, he or she brings Christ into the unholy union with a prostitute. Paul’s words are indeed shocking, if not nearly incomprehensible. Christians who grasp this truth realize that because our bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit (v. 19), our supreme goal is to use our bodies to glorify God.

**Q:** What perspective should motivate and shape Christians’ present life and service to God?

**A:** Paul teaches that Christians should keep in mind the goal of their redemption and the destiny that God has for His church.

**Q:** Does Paul actually conceive of a situation where a true believer might forfeit his or her salvation?

**A:** Paul is speaking to believers. The warning is so urgent that he mentions it twice. It is not reasonable that Paul would motivate them to godly living by issuing such an urgent warning against a situation that could not actually occur.

### Marriage (7:1–40)

#### 2.3.1 — Explain why the Corinthians failed to understand God’s plan for sexual fulfillment in marriage.

Explain why the Corinthians failed to understand God’s plan for sexual fulfillment in marriage.

Marriage: God’s Plan for Sexual Fulfillment

Greek philosophy tended to associate the material world with evil and so looked down on the physical body. This often led to two opposite responses. The first viewed the body and its desires as of little value. Consequently, people could do whatever they pleased without affecting their spirits. This view was called libertinism because it promoted total freedom to satisfy the appetites of the body. The other response, called asceticism , saw the body and its desires as something to be mastered and brought under subjection. This required a strict program of discipline and self-denial that often included fasting or abstaining from sexual relations. Some in Corinth had taken this second view.

Paul now responds to those who had written, asking him about the counsel they had received “not to touch a woman.” The expression translated in 7:1 as “not to marry” is used throughout Greek literature as a polite way to refer to not having sexual intercourse (Horton 1999, 67–68). Some may have thought that celibacy was more spiritual than marriage, perhaps misunderstanding Luke 20:34–35 and supposing that their new status in Christ called them to live a sexless existence like the angels (Horton 1999, 68). However, Paul instructed them, in light of their society’s rampant immorality, to go ahead and marry.

Yet Paul is not just saying that marriage is a practical way to gratify a person’s sexual desires and avoid immorality. He is stating God’s plan for humanity. God has made men and women sexual beings and created marriage as the context in which they are to experience sexual fulfillment.

1 Corinthians 7:39 specifies that a widow who chooses to remarry must choose a spouse only if he loves the Lord. This logically should also be true for any believer who wants to marry: their spouse must be a Christ-follower.

Summarize Paul’s advice to those who consider remaining unmarried.

Celibacy: Paul’s Preferred and Gifted Option

Even though Paul understood the purpose of marriage in God’s plan, he himself preferred to remain unmarried for practical reasons. Later, in verses 25–35, he explains that the unmarried man or woman is undivided in devotion and service to the Lord. Single individuals are free to wholly serve the Lord without competing duties or distractions. With that advantage in mind, Paul wishes everyone would remain unmarried. However, he realizes that not everyone can do so because each person has his or her own “gift.” Since Paul uses the same Greek word here as he does for the gifts ( charismata ) of the Spirit in chapter 12, we can conclude that he sees the ability to serve God without marrying as the enabling power of the Spirit.

Paul adds some practical advice regarding the decision to marry. Although virgins, young men, and widows were free to marry (7:28, 36–39), Paul felt that the difficult times and circumstances of his day made marrying unwise (v. 26). Those who chose to marry would face many troubles (v. 28). Consequently, Paul counsels people at that time not to seek to change their marital status. In offering this advice, he cannot give a direct command from the Lord but is confident it is trustworthy from someone who has received both God’s mercy (v. 25) and God’s Spirit (v. 40). While Paul is not against marriage, he clearly believes that the unmarried person has advantages over the married.

Explain why Paul counsels against divorce.

Divorce

The discussion of divorce in 1 Corinthians 7 seems to reflect questions the Corinthians themselves raised concerning situations in the church. Some may have thought that becoming a Christian meant separating oneself from an unholy union with an unbeliever. However, regarding the practice of divorce, Paul does not give his own opinion but echoes the teaching of Jesus (Mark 10:1–12): a Christian should not seek to be divorced. Rather, a Christian wife or husband should remain married as long as the unbelieving spouse agrees to live with him or her. Paul reasons that the unbelieving spouse is sanctified by the believer. By this, Paul likely means that the family is set apart by God and comes under the influence of His grace through the believer’s life. At stake is nothing less than the salvation of the unbelieving spouse.

The only exception to staying in the relationship is if the unbelieving partner abandons the marriage. In that case, the believer cannot force his or her spouse to remain. Thus, the believer is not bound before God to that marriage. According to Craig Keener, the phrase “not bound” (1 Corinthians 7:15) comes from the language of divorce contracts of the day, which always stipulated the freedom to remarry (2005, 65).

#### 2.3.2 — Summarize Paul’s advice to those who consider remaining unmarried.

Explain why the Corinthians failed to understand God’s plan for sexual fulfillment in marriage.

Marriage: God’s Plan for Sexual Fulfillment

Greek philosophy tended to associate the material world with evil and so looked down on the physical body. This often led to two opposite responses. The first viewed the body and its desires as of little value. Consequently, people could do whatever they pleased without affecting their spirits. This view was called libertinism because it promoted total freedom to satisfy the appetites of the body. The other response, called asceticism , saw the body and its desires as something to be mastered and brought under subjection. This required a strict program of discipline and self-denial that often included fasting or abstaining from sexual relations. Some in Corinth had taken this second view.

Paul now responds to those who had written, asking him about the counsel they had received “not to touch a woman.” The expression translated in 7:1 as “not to marry” is used throughout Greek literature as a polite way to refer to not having sexual intercourse (Horton 1999, 67–68). Some may have thought that celibacy was more spiritual than marriage, perhaps misunderstanding Luke 20:34–35 and supposing that their new status in Christ called them to live a sexless existence like the angels (Horton 1999, 68). However, Paul instructed them, in light of their society’s rampant immorality, to go ahead and marry.

Yet Paul is not just saying that marriage is a practical way to gratify a person’s sexual desires and avoid immorality. He is stating God’s plan for humanity. God has made men and women sexual beings and created marriage as the context in which they are to experience sexual fulfillment.

1 Corinthians 7:39 specifies that a widow who chooses to remarry must choose a spouse only if he loves the Lord. This logically should also be true for any believer who wants to marry: their spouse must be a Christ-follower.

Summarize Paul’s advice to those who consider remaining unmarried.

Celibacy: Paul’s Preferred and Gifted Option

Even though Paul understood the purpose of marriage in God’s plan, he himself preferred to remain unmarried for practical reasons. Later, in verses 25–35, he explains that the unmarried man or woman is undivided in devotion and service to the Lord. Single individuals are free to wholly serve the Lord without competing duties or distractions. With that advantage in mind, Paul wishes everyone would remain unmarried. However, he realizes that not everyone can do so because each person has his or her own “gift.” Since Paul uses the same Greek word here as he does for the gifts ( charismata ) of the Spirit in chapter 12, we can conclude that he sees the ability to serve God without marrying as the enabling power of the Spirit.

Paul adds some practical advice regarding the decision to marry. Although virgins, young men, and widows were free to marry (7:28, 36–39), Paul felt that the difficult times and circumstances of his day made marrying unwise (v. 26). Those who chose to marry would face many troubles (v. 28). Consequently, Paul counsels people at that time not to seek to change their marital status. In offering this advice, he cannot give a direct command from the Lord but is confident it is trustworthy from someone who has received both God’s mercy (v. 25) and God’s Spirit (v. 40). While Paul is not against marriage, he clearly believes that the unmarried person has advantages over the married.

Explain why Paul counsels against divorce.

Divorce

The discussion of divorce in 1 Corinthians 7 seems to reflect questions the Corinthians themselves raised concerning situations in the church. Some may have thought that becoming a Christian meant separating oneself from an unholy union with an unbeliever. However, regarding the practice of divorce, Paul does not give his own opinion but echoes the teaching of Jesus (Mark 10:1–12): a Christian should not seek to be divorced. Rather, a Christian wife or husband should remain married as long as the unbelieving spouse agrees to live with him or her. Paul reasons that the unbelieving spouse is sanctified by the believer. By this, Paul likely means that the family is set apart by God and comes under the influence of His grace through the believer’s life. At stake is nothing less than the salvation of the unbelieving spouse.

The only exception to staying in the relationship is if the unbelieving partner abandons the marriage. In that case, the believer cannot force his or her spouse to remain. Thus, the believer is not bound before God to that marriage. ## 3 - Christian Choices (1 Corinthians 8–10)

### Christian Freedom and Meat Sacrificed to Idols (8–9)

#### 3.1.1 — Describe the problem regarding meat offered to idols.

Describe the problem regarding meat offered to idols.

Sacrifices to idols were a common but critical part of Greco-Roman religious life in Paul’s day. For the pagan, these sacrifices symbolized devotion to a particular deity and were considered a means of entering into spiritual fellowship with that deity. Only a small portion of each sacrifice was dedicated to the god; the rest was given to the officiating priest(s) and worshipper. It often found its way to either a banquet table for some celebration or, in the case of public sacrifices, to the meat shops in the market (Barclay 1975, 72).

Explain how knowledge should govern Christian freedom.

Freedom in Christ

Paul now turns to a question that undoubtedly troubled the Corinthians: is it proper for Christians to eat meat that has come from pagan sacrifices offered to idols? Paul will eventually answer that believers are free to do so, but first he offers a mild rebuke to those who exercise their freedom out of knowledge but not with love. The numerous uses of the words knowledge and know suggest that some in Corinth were boasting of superior knowledge on the subject. Paul informs them that knowledge without love can lead only to destructive pride. Love, on the other hand, not only builds up fellow believers but also invites and promotes an intimate knowledge of God.

Still, Paul does not deny that knowledge is important. He wants believers to know several things about pagan idolatry, including that there is no reality behind idols. Perhaps echoing Isaiah’s thoughts on the foolishness of idolatry (Isaiah 2:8; 40:18–20; 41:7; 44:9–20; 46:1–2), Paul wants followers of Christ to realize that idols are lifeless human creations without the power to speak or act (Horton 1999, 79). Despite the pagan worship of many “so-called gods” and “lords,” Christians should know that “there is no God but one” (1 Corinthians 8:4), who is God, the Father, the Creator. Furthermore, there is only one Lord, the Lord Jesus Christ, who together with the Father creates all things and gives life to all (v. 6). 1

Jesus said that what goes into a person’s stomach is not what defiles him or her (Mark 7:15–19). Paul later notes that not every Christian knows that what we eat or do not eat carries no particular importance to God (1 Corinthians 8:8). Because of such ignorance, some Corinthian believers were stricken in their consciences when faced with eating meat from the market or on the table of an unbeliever.

Thus, Paul gives this instruction to those whose knowledge permits them to eat such food: do not use your liberty in a way that causes your weaker brother or sister to stumble into sin. Such sin occurs if weaker believers violate their consciences, or their personal convictions before God, by eating food they believe is forbidden by their faith in Christ (v. 12). In this case, the weaker believers’ eating “is not from faith; and everything that does not come from faith is sin” (Romans 14:23). Paul’s counsel not to destroy the brother or sister for whom Christ died (1 Corinthians 8:11) therefore echoes Romans 14:20: “Do not destroy the work of God for the sake of food.”

Relation to Personal Rights

Continuing the theme of Christian freedom, Paul uses himself as an illustration: surely his status as an apostle grants him certain rights. However, at Corinth, as in Galatia, 2 Paul faced opposition from those who did not accept his apostleship. Nevertheless, Paul is confident of his apostolic calling, which resulted from divine revelation. Because the resurrected Christ personally appeared to him (1 Corinthians 9:1), his apostleship is not inferior to that of the original apostles (vv. 2–6). Furthermore, he has become a spiritual father to the Corinthians by bringing them the gospel (4:14–15). They themselves are the identifying seal or mark of his apostleship (9:2).

As an apostle, Paul has the right to make a living by preaching the gospel (vv. 4–11)—a point he makes clear through several common sense illustrations and an Old Testament quotation: “Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain” (Deuteronomy 25:4). Yet true Christian freedom means being able to refuse the full use of our rights and freedom. This is exactly what Paul does in regard for the Corinthians. He has every right to expect their financial support for his gospel work. In fact, Paul says such support was commanded (Greek diatassō , “to charge”) by the Lord himself (1 Corinthians 9:14).

However, although he does not explain his reasons for doing so, Paul chooses not to take such a living for the sake of the gospel. Paul states only that to do so would “hinder the gospel of Christ” (v. 12). Furthermore, his decision is rooted in his divine call to preach the gospel and his desire to win the lost to Christ. For this reason, he has determined to do nothing that would hinder his goal and everything to accomplish it.

Paul does not preach the gospel for monetary gain. On the contrary, he regards it as a privilege and honor to preach without remuneration. As in 2 Corinthians 11:11–12, he refers to this practice as his “boast.” Using irony, Paul states that his “reward” is to preach the gospel “free of charge” (1 Corinthians 9:18). He is not only willing but also under divine compulsion—“Woe to me,” he says, “if I do not preach the gospel” (v. 16).

In preaching the gospel, Paul is compelled to “become all things to all men” so that by all means he might save some (vv. 20–22). Making the message and truth of Scripture relevant and intelligible to the receptor audience or culture in this way is called contextualization (Hernando 2005, 17, 81).

To further explain his motivation, Paul compares his work to an athletic contest (vv. 24–27). He is driven to make the demands, sacrifice, and discipline necessary to achieve the victor’s crown. Unlike the fading garland wreath given at Greek games, the crown that awaits Paul is imperishable, promising eternal life and divine glory (2 Corinthians 4:17). Thus, he realizes that while laboring to bring salvation to the Gentiles, through preaching the gospel, he has become a “fellow partaker” with them.

**Q:** How did Old Testament sacrifices differ from those practiced by the pagans of Paul’s day?

**A:** Although many parallels exist between Old Testament sacrifices and those of Greco-Roman religions, the Old Testament sin offerings were offered to preserve and maintain fellowship with God by dealing with the problem of sin.

**Q:** Why is the weak brother “destroyed” (v. 11) for something Paul indicates is not sin and matters little to God (v. 8)?

**A:** The weaker brother commits sin by doing something that violates his conscience before God. It is sin because he does not do what his conscience tells him is right (James 4:17). In addition, if he does what he cannot do in faith, it is sin (Romans 14:23).

**Q:** How does contextualization serve to spread the gospel?

**A:** Contextualization helps to remove cultural barriers from the truth of Jesus Christ. It relates the gospel message to the receptor culture or audience without dimming the truth.

### The Example of the Israelites (10:1–11:1)

#### 3.2.1 — Identify factors that led to Israel’s idolatry and that exemplify what believers are not to do.

Identify factors that led to Israel’s idolatry and that exemplify what believers are not to do.

An Example Not to Follow

At the end of chapter 9, Paul gave a negative motivation for his self-discipline and sacrifice in pursuit of eternal reward. He did not want to be “disqualified” from receiving the prize (9:27). Paul was not talking about losing the rewards of Christian service. Rather, he used the Greek word adokimos , which describes those who are depraved sinners (Romans 1:28) and false teachers (Titus 1:15–16), clearly not destined for eternal life. In other words, Paul’s warning is clear: through moral neglect, the Corinthians may fail to obtain the eschatological prize of eternal salvation. The text is clear here, and those who reject such an interpretation of this passage do so because of their prior theological bias (Fee 1987, 440).

In chapter 10, Paul offers historical Israel as an example of those who misused their freedom and became “disqualified.” Paul highlights three factors that led to Israel’s idolatry and failure to enter the Promised Land: their ingratitude, sinful indulgence, and perversion of their knowledge of God. Despite God’s gracious protection and provision in the wilderness, Israel repeatedly put God to the test (Numbers 14:22).

In particular, Paul mentions that God sustained them by miraculously giving them water from a rock. By calling Christ the rock (1 Corinthians 10:4), Paul draws an analogy between what God did for Israel physically as their Rock (Deuteronomy 32:4, 31) and what Christ has done for the Corinthians spiritually. In both cases, a supernatural provision saved them. Because of the Israelites’ ingratitude and rebellion, they lost God’s favor, and He was forced to discipline an entire generation who eventually died in the wilderness.

From the three examples Paul seems to have in mind (Exodus 32; Numbers 11; 25), then, it is clear that ungrateful complaints and grumbling preceded Israel’s idolatry. This was accompanied by giving in to fleshly appetites. Their lust for food and drink led to craving evil things and sexual immorality (1 Corinthians 10:6–8). Such carnal indulgences (common in pagan worship) led Israel to compromise and pervert the worship of the one true God.

Explain why Paul issues a strong warning against participating in pagan worship.

Paul’s Counsel and Example

Twice (10:6, 11), Paul states that the Scriptures recount Hebrew history as instruction and a warning to God’s people not to follow the path of the Israelites. Paul desires to preserve the Corinthians’ purity in their devotion to Christ. He warns them to flee idolatry (v. 14), knowing that participation in pagan idolatry is nothing short of demon worship and totally incompatible with their communion and fellowship with Christ (vv. 16–21).

The present subject again leads to the issue of eating food offered to idols (1 Corinthians 8). Paul wants to make sure the Corinthians know he is not placing a restriction on eating such food. However, even if they know they possess such freedom, they must not selfishly use it (10:24). Instead, they should have regard for and sensitivity toward the conscience of all believers, Jewish and Greek.

Our goal should be to build, not bring condemnation to, another’s conscience (vv. 23–29). Such selfless freedom in the service of others brings glory to God. In seeking to please “all men in all things,” Paul uses his freedom for one purpose—that all individuals might be saved (v. 33). It is an example that he desires them to follow, an example left to us by the Lord himself (11:1).

**Q:** Why is idolatry so offensive to God?

**A:** At the heart of idolatry is the denial of God, our Creator, and the denial and perversion of the truth He has revealed about himself in creation (Romans 1:20). Consequently, humans wind up worshipping something created rather than the Creator of all things (1:25).

**A:** No. God’s condemnation of Israel’s idolatry involved not only their worship of idols or false gods but also worship of Yahweh in a form of an idol that perverted His true image (Exodus 20:4–6; 32:4–8). #### 3.2.2 — Explain why Paul issues a strong warning against participating in pagan worship.

Identify factors that led to Israel’s idolatry and that exemplify what believers are not to do.

An Example Not to Follow

At the end of chapter 9, Paul gave a negative motivation for his self-discipline and sacrifice in pursuit of eternal reward. He did not want to be “disqualified” from receiving the prize (9:27). Paul was not talking about losing the rewards of Christian service. Rather, he used the Greek word adokimos , which describes those who are depraved sinners (Romans 1:28) and false teachers (Titus 1:15–16), clearly not destined for eternal life. In other words, Paul’s warning is clear: through moral neglect, the Corinthians may fail to obtain the eschatological prize of eternal salvation. The text is clear here, and those who reject such an interpretation of this passage do so because of their prior theological bias (Fee 1987, 440).

In chapter 10, Paul offers historical Israel as an example of those who misused their freedom and became “disqualified.” Paul highlights three factors that led to Israel’s idolatry and failure to enter the Promised Land: their ingratitude, sinful indulgence, and perversion of their knowledge of God. Despite God’s gracious protection and provision in the wilderness, Israel repeatedly put God to the test (Numbers 14:22).

In particular, Paul mentions that God sustained them by miraculously giving them water from a rock. By calling Christ the rock (1 Corinthians 10:4), Paul draws an analogy between what God did for Israel physically as their Rock (Deuteronomy 32:4, 31) and what Christ has done for the Corinthians spiritually. In both cases, a supernatural provision saved them. Because of the Israelites’ ingratitude and rebellion, they lost God’s favor, and He was forced to discipline an entire generation who eventually died in the wilderness.

From the three examples Paul seems to have in mind (Exodus 32; Numbers 11; 25), then, it is clear that ungrateful complaints and grumbling preceded Israel’s idolatry. This was accompanied by giving in to fleshly appetites. Their lust for food and drink led to craving evil things and sexual immorality (1 Corinthians 10:6–8). Such carnal indulgences (common in pagan worship) led Israel to compromise and pervert the worship of the one true God.

Explain why Paul issues a strong warning against participating in pagan worship.

Paul’s Counsel and Example

Twice (10:6, 11), Paul states that the Scriptures recount Hebrew history as instruction and a warning to God’s people not to follow the path of the Israelites. Paul desires to preserve the Corinthians’ purity in their devotion to Christ. He warns them to flee idolatry (v. 14), knowing that participation in pagan idolatry is nothing short of demon worship and totally incompatible with their communion and fellowship with Christ (vv. 16–21).

The present subject again leads to the issue of eating food offered to idols (1 Corinthians 8). Paul wants to make sure the Corinthians know he is not placing a restriction on eating such food. However, even if they know they possess such freedom, they must not selfishly use it (10:24). Instead, they should have regard for and sensitivity toward the conscience of all believers, Jewish and Greek.

Our goal should be to build, not bring condemnation to, another’s conscience (vv. 23–29). Such selfless freedom in the service of others brings glory to God. In seeking to please “all men in all things,” Paul uses his freedom for one purpose—that all individuals might be saved (v. 33). It is an example that he desires them to follow, an example left to us by the Lord himself (11:1).

### Instructions on Worship (11:2–16)

#### 4.1.1 — Describe the problem regarding worship that prompted Paul’s teaching on headship.

Describe the problem regarding worship that prompted Paul’s teaching on headship.

With 1 Corinthians 11:2, Paul begins to address various topics and questions related to worship. He commends the Corinthians for remembering the traditions he taught and exhorts them to continue to practice them. In the New Testament church, the word tradition (Greek paradosis ) referred to what the apostles taught and passed on concerning Christian conduct and worship. The term focused less on belief and more on practice.

However, a problem has arisen, and Paul typically deals with problems by teaching foundational truth. The issue concerns proper conduct during worship, in particular whether women are to wear head coverings while praying or prophesying. To answer this question, Paul teaches on a threefold “headship” of God, of Christ, and of humankind.

The passage hinges on verse 3 and the meaning of head (Greek kephalē ). In this passage, the term is used both literally of the physical head (vv. 4–7) and figuratively (v. 3). This later sense is debated. That is, controversy has arisen over how the word head was understood in the ancient Greek world. The two competing but not exclusive options are “source,” speaking of origin (Gill and Caveness 2004, 87–92; Kroeger 1993, 375–377), and “head” or “authority,” connoting authority over (Grudem 1985, 38–59; 1990, 3–72; 2001, 25–65). The camps are divided over the issue of whether women or wives bear any subordinate role to men or husbands. Since Bible-believing Christians want to be faithful to Scripture, what Paul meant by this term is extremely important. One thing is fairly certain: the meaning of head in verse 3 must remain the same in all three uses.

Regarding the issue of the head covering, this much is clear: certain women in Corinth were choosing not to cover their heads during public worship. In the eastern culture, to see a woman without a veil or head covering was disgraceful and immodest (Barclay 1975, 97–98; Keener 1993, 475). Paul is sensitive to the culture and likewise emphasizes that such conduct is shameful. More significant for the Greco-Roman context, a Christian wife who removed her head covering for prayer was rebelling against the authority of the state, against Roman law itself (Winter 2003, 96).

However, Paul’s reasons for insisting on the head covering have more to do with the order of creation and the relationship of husband and wife within that order. A man should pray or prophesy with his head uncovered because he “is the image and glory of God; but the woman is the glory of man” (1 Corinthians 11:7), having been created from man (v. 8) and for his sake (v. 9). Paul sees the absence of a wife’s head covering as showing disrespect for the husband in his role as “head.”

Identify the three sets of relationships Paul uses to illustrate headship.

That head does not mean “source” in this context seems clear. The historic orthodox understanding of the Trinity has always rejected any notion that the Son was created or that His existence somehow depended on the Father. The church in the third and fourth centuries hotly debated the deity of Christ and how the Persons of the Trinity relate to one another.

In affirming Christ’s full deity and humanity, the orthodox Early Church rejected any view that (1) saw the Son of God as created or (2) considered His essential being to have been derived or generated in a temporal sense (Gonzalez 1970, 344–362). Therefore, the Son’s “generation” from the Father (John 16:28) should be understood solely in terms of Christ’s incarnation and the Father’s sending Him to accomplish the plan of redemption. Consequently, within the Trinitarian “household,” the term head is best understood as referring to authority, reflecting the Father’s role in sending the Son for the purpose of accomplishing redemption, rather than implying any difference in essence or status.

Because Christ is God’s agent in creation (John 1:1, 3), the declaration that He is the head of every man (1 Corinthians 11:3) could refer to the fact that Jesus is the source of every person. But Christ’s status as Lord and His role as Creator are inseparable. Paul elsewhere links Christ’s authoritative lordship to His authorship of creation (Colossians 1:16–18; 3:1–10; Ephesians 2:10) and to His exaltation and honor as “head over everything for the church” (Ephesians 1:22). In this exalted role as head, Christ possesses supreme and universal authority. God has “placed all things under his feet” in subjection (1:20–22).

Since Paul clearly has the context of creation in mind (1 Corinthians 11:7–9), it seems correct to interpret man and woman in verse 3 as a reference to “husband” and “wife,” not the male and female genders. (Consider 1 Corinthians 14:35, where Paul advises women to “ask their own husbands” about church teachings.) This is an important difference. Namely, any subordination that head might convey must be understood as a wife’s functional and voluntary submission to her husband (Ephesians 5:24; Colossians 3:18; Titus 2:5; 1 Peter 3:1) (Horton 1999, 99).

It is also important to note that submission is not equated with inferiority. Jesus honored His Father and freely submitted to His will. Yet this in no way meant that He held lesser dignity or was inferior. His submission was demanded by His role in redemption. Likewise, a wife’s submission to her husband must be viewed as voluntary and among equals. Still, her submission honors her husband as her head and recognizes his responsibility and role to not only lead and guide but also sacrificially love, nurture, and protect her according to Christ’s example (Ephesians 5:24–29).

### Instructions on the Lord’s Supper (11:17–34)

#### 4.2.1 — Identify principles that should govern how believers partake of the Lord’s Supper.

Identify principles that shoulvd govern how believers partake of the Lord’s Supper.

In the latter portion of 1 Corinthians 11, Paul turns to another problem related to worship—the manner in which believers practiced the Lord’s Supper. Apparently, some in Corinth regarded the Lord’s Supper as a banquet much like those during Greek religious festivals.

The Early Church had begun the custom of a fellowship meal called the agape (love) feast (see Figure 4.1), which they seem to have conducted along with the breaking of bread at the Lord’s Supper (Acts 2:46), also called the Eucharist or Holy Communion in some churches. Perhaps describing the feast’s origin, in Acts 2:42–47, Luke emphasizes the unity and devotion among the believers. By contrast, the Corinthians conducted themselves selfishly, revealing their divisions and factions (1 Corinthians 11:18–19). They came to the feast with their own food and ate without sharing or waiting for others.

Great irony was involved here. Contrast Paul’s effusive praise in 1 Corinthians 11:2 with his sharp rebuke in 11:17. In the former section, Paul praises them for the prayer and prophecy of men and women together in the gathering. Now, he states that they are doing damage with how they observe the Lord’s Supper to the extent that they are doing “more harm than good.”

The Lord’s Supper was intended as a celebration symbolizing the unity and communion of all believers in the redemptive benefits of Christ’s death. Instead, it had become a testimony to their disunity and lack of true fellowship. Paul does not withhold his obvious displeasure and issues a sharp rebuke (v. 22). Their selfish conduct displayed shameful disregard for the poor and amounted to despising the church of God.

To correct the situation, Paul reminds them of the teaching he had received from the Lord and delivered to them on other occasions. He emphasizes the solemn truth behind the Lord’s Supper. It is a celebration, yes, but it is also a memorial and testimonial to Jesus’ death. His death instituted a new covenant whereby all believers enter into living communion with Him. Each time we partake of the Lord’s Supper, we proclaim His death for us.

Paul concludes by cautioning the Corinthians not to conduct this feast in an “unworthy manner” (11:27). By this, Paul probably has in mind the selfish and inconsiderate conduct he describes (vv. 20–22, 33–34). To enter into the Lord’s Supper in a way that mocks its meaning is risky business. In no uncertain terms, Paul states that such an offense invites God’s discipline and judgment (vv. 28–32). To avoid this, believers should approach the Lord’s Table with sober self-examination to discern whether they need to correct or repent for anything.

Describe how Christology serves as a criterion for judging prophecy.

Spiritual Gifts and Jesus’ Teaching

Chapter 12 begins a discussion about spiritual gifts, an area of practice with which the Corinthian church experienced problems. In verse 1, Paul uses the broader term pneumatika (“spiritual things”) to introduce the subject of spiritual phenomena, or things that pertain to life in the Spirit, but he quickly changes terminology. In 12:4, he uses the term charismata to refer to God’s gracious gifts through the Holy Spirit to the church. In chapter 1 of this course, we noted that charismata is derived from charis , meaning “grace.” Supernatural in origin and nature, these gifts enable believers to carry out vital ministry within the church and the world.

The Corinthians were not ignorant of spiritual phenomena. In particular, Greco-Roman religions included pagan oracle sites, where people sought supernatural guidance from spirit-inspired prophets.

Recalling their idolatrous background (v. 2), Paul then teaches them how to judge prophecy. Because the Spirit has come to bear witness to Christ (John 15:26), we must judge prophecy by what the prophet says concerning the person of Jesus. After all, as John writes, “The testimony of (concerning) Jesus is the spirit of prophecy” (Revelation 19:10). True prophecy does not diminish Christ’s redemptive work or deny His lordship (1 John 2:18–23; 4:1–6).

Moreover, from Paul’s perspective on salvation, the doctrine of Christ and the doctrine of the Holy Spirit are inseparable; in fact, at times Pauline Christology (the study of Christ) and pneumatology (the study of the Holy Spirit) run together. For example, Paul tells the Roman believers that they are “in the Spirit ( en pneumatic )” if the Spirit of God indwells them. He then refers to the Spirit as the Spirit of Christ (Romans 8:9) and adds, “If Christ is in you” (v. 10). Other passages link the work of the Spirit either to the church’s preaching of Christ (Romans 9:1; 15:19) or to Jesus’ and the believers’ resurrection (8:11), deliverance from the law of sin and death (8:2), the work of sanctification and justification (1 Corinthians 6:11), and so on. All of this underscores that the Spirit mediates and brings about everything that a Christian’s life accrues from the redemptive work of Christ.

Summarize principles Paul taught about spiritual gifts.

Spiritual Gifts and the Spirit

The use of spiritual gifts had been both problematic and divisive in Corinth. To remedy the situation, Paul emphasizes the unity of the gifts’ divine source, despite their number and diversity. They are the work of the one Triune God. That is, they are gifts of the Holy Spirit and make possible the ministries of the Lord Jesus through the supernatural workings of God the Father.

However, in the economy of the Trinity, distribution of the gifts in the body of Christ is primarily the Spirit’s role. Thus, each gift is called a “manifestation of the Spirit” (1 Corinthians 12:7). Paul repeatedly points out that despite the many and diverse gifts, they are the work of the “same Spirit,” who distributes them throughout the body “just as he determines” (v. 11). Moreover, Paul stresses unity in the purpose for the gifts. They are given for the “common good” (v. 7)—that is, to bless and benefit the whole body. As Suurmond states, “It is not so much a matter of having a gift as of being a gift” (Suurmond 1998, 105).

## 5 - Chapter 5

### Spiritual Gifts and the Body (12:12–13:13)

#### 5.1.1 — Explain why Paul chose the metaphor of the body to teach about spiritual gifts.

Explain why Paul chose the metaphor of the body to teach about spiritual gifts.

Our Makeup

Paul now presents spiritual gifts as fundamental to God’s plan for the Church, using the example of a body to get his point across. Why Paul chose this metaphor is not explained here, but elsewhere he uses it to emphasize the organic unity between the Church and Christ, its head (Ephesians 1:22–23; Colossians 1:18; 2:19).

The figure is also well suited for a discussion of the composition of Christ’s body. Just as a physical body has many separate parts with different functions, so the body of Christ has many members, each possessing an important function. Those functions represent ministries necessary to the overall working of Christ’s body.

The ability to minister in these capacities comes from various spiritual gifts (Greek charismata ), yet the different members and gifts come from one and the same Spirit. The Spirit is responsible for placing (“baptizing”) us into the one body of Christ and, in doing so, enables us to partake (“drink”) of the one Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:13). Paul wants us to understand that unity can exist alongside diversity. In fact, diversity within unity is an integral part of God’s plan for the Church.

Paul wants the Corinthians to recognize that each member of Christ’s body has been gifted to carry out a necessary function in the body (vv. 15–26) and that this arrangement is God’s. God has sovereignly placed them in the body “just as he wanted them to be” (v. 18). 1 Therefore, no one has grounds to feel inferior, jealous (v. 15), proud, or superior (v. 21). Furthermore, within the body of Christ, all members complement and are interdependent of one another. Each member needs all the others for the body’s proper functioning. This profound harmony unites them in common experience. Whatever joy or suffering comes to one is borne by all (v. 26).

Such an arrangement is God’s handiwork; it is not only His design but also His right. He has set (“appointed”) each gifted member in the Church for His own purpose: ministry within the body. The Greek verb for arranged (v. 18) or appointed (v. 28) is in the middle voice, indicating that the subject (God) is acting in regard to himself or in His own interest.

Paul clearly implies that spiritual gifts create the capacity for ministry within the body of Christ and that all members of the body are gifted for service. Nevertheless, they do not possess the same gifts. For that reason, the answers to the questions in 12:29–30 (for example, “Are all apostles? Do all have gifts of healing?”) are understood to be negative. No gift is owned or possessed by all because diversity is part of God’s design for His Church. The “greater gifts” to be desired are not greater in importance, for that would contrast with Paul’s teaching in verses 15–26. Rather, the term greater gifts most likely refers to those gifts with a greater capacity to edify the body by intelligible speech (Godet 1977, 657–658; Fee 1987, 623–625), a topic Paul will deal with at length in 1 Corinthians 14.

Clarify how love is the “most excellent way” in regard to spiritual gifts.

Love and Spiritual Gifts

In chapter 13, Paul expounds on the “most excellent way”—love (12:31). He is not saying that love surpasses or replaces the spiritual gifts, making them unnecessary. Rather, he claims that love is the most excellent way to express or practice gifts for three reasons:

1. Only gifts administered in love deliver a blessing or benefit to the minister (13:1–3). And, perhaps, only gifts administered in love have true value before God. 2

2. As a fruit of the Spirit finding expression through spiritual gifts, love in action promotes Christian character formation. God gave the gifts of the Spirit for service within the body for the profit and benefit of all (12:7). However, as Paul admonished the Galatians, believers are to “serve one another in love” (Galatians 5:13). To do this, they must “live by the Spirit” (5:16) so their lives will be characterized by the fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23), of which love is the first to be mentioned. Thus, Spirit-empowered service, motivated and rendered in love, is the most excellent way.

3. Love never fails (1 Corinthians 13:8). That is, love, unlike spiritual gifts, has permanent worth and relevance. The gifts belong to the temporary and imperfect setting of earthly existence. 3 Love, however, along with faith and hope, is foundational in our unending relationship with God. Thus, love gives eternal value to our service to God and ministry in spiritual gifts. Among the triad of virtues mentioned, love is greatest because it expresses the very redemptive character of God, who is love (1 John 4:8), and is the divine motive out of which we were redeemed (John 3:16) (Horton 1999, 130).

**Q:** Is the baptism by the Spirit in 1 Corinthians 12:13 the same as the Pentecostal baptism in Acts 1:5?

**A:** No. The baptism in 1 Corinthians 12:13 is performed by the Holy Spirit to place us in the body of Christ. The baptism in the Holy Spirit in Acts 1:5 is performed by Jesus to spiritually empower believers for Christian service and witness (Acts 1:8).

**Q:** What does God’s distribution of gifts communicate about His passions?

**A:** God loves diversity and expects us to embrace a similar passion.

**Q:** According to Paul, which is superior: love or spiritual gifts?

**A:** Neither. Paul is not saying that love surpasses or replaces the gifts, making them unnecessary. Rather, he presents love as the most excellent way to express or practice the gifts.

Our Makeup

Paul now presents spiritual gifts as fundamental to God’s plan for the Church, using the example of a body to get his point across. Why Paul chose this metaphor is not explained here, but elsewhere he uses it to emphasize the organic unity between the Church and Christ, its head (Ephesians 1:22–23; Colossians 1:18; 2:19).

The figure is also well suited for a discussion of the composition of Christ’s body. Just as a physical body has many separate parts with different functions, so the body of Christ has many members, each possessing an important function. Those functions represent ministries necessary to the overall working of Christ’s body.

The ability to minister in these capacities comes from various spiritual gifts (Greek charismata ), yet the different members and gifts come from one and the same Spirit. The Spirit is responsible for placing (“baptizing”) us into the one body of Christ and, in doing so, enables us to partake (“drink”) of the one Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:13). Paul wants us to understand that unity can exist alongside diversity. In fact, diversity within unity is an integral part of God’s plan for the Church.

Paul wants the Corinthians to recognize that each member of Christ’s body has been gifted to carry out a necessary function in the body (vv. 15–26) and that this arrangement is God’s. God has sovereignly placed them in the body “just as he wanted them to be” (v. 18). 1 Therefore, no one has grounds to feel inferior, jealous (v. 15), proud, or superior (v. 21). Furthermore, within the body of Christ, all members complement and are interdependent of one another. Each member needs all the others for the body’s proper functioning. This profound harmony unites them in common experience. Whatever joy or suffering comes to one is borne by all (v. 26).

Such an arrangement is God’s handiwork; it is not only His design but also His right. He has set (“appointed”) each gifted member in the Church for His own purpose: ministry within the body. The Greek verb for arranged (v. 18) or appointed (v. 28) is in the middle voice, indicating that the subject (God) is acting in regard to himself or in His own interest.

Paul clearly implies that spiritual gifts create the capacity for ministry within the body of Christ and that all members of the body are gifted for service. Nevertheless, they do not possess the same gifts. For that reason, the answers to the questions in 12:29–30 (for example, “Are all apostles? Do all have gifts of healing?”) are understood to be negative. No gift is owned or possessed by all because diversity is part of God’s design for His Church. The “greater gifts” to be desired are not greater in importance, for that would contrast with Paul’s teaching in verses 15–26. Rather, the term greater gifts most likely refers to those gifts with a greater capacity to edify the body by intelligible speech (Godet 1977, 657–658; Fee 1987, 623–625), a topic Paul will deal with at length in 1 Corinthians 14.

Clarify how love is the “most excellent way” in regard to spiritual gifts.

Love and Spiritual Gifts

In chapter 13, Paul expounds on the “most excellent way”—love (12:31). He is not saying that love surpasses or replaces the spiritual gifts, making them unnecessary. Rather, he claims that love is the most excellent way to express or practice gifts for three reasons:

1. Only gifts administered in love deliver a blessing or benefit to the minister (13:1–3). And, perhaps, only gifts administered in love have true value before God. 2

2. As a fruit of the Spirit finding expression through spiritual gifts, love in action promotes Christian character formation. God gave the gifts of the Spirit for service within the body for the profit and benefit of all (12:7). However, as Paul admonished the Galatians, believers are to “serve one another in love” (Galatians 5:13). To do this, they must “live by the Spirit” (5:16) so their lives will be characterized by the fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23), of which love is the first to be mentioned. Thus, Spirit-empowered service, motivated and rendered in love, is the most excellent way.

3. Love never fails (1 Corinthians 13:8). That is, love, unlike spiritual gifts, has permanent worth and relevance. The gifts belong to the temporary and imperfect setting of earthly existence. 3 Love, however, along with faith and hope, is foundational in our unending relationship with God. Thus, love gives eternal value to our service to God and ministry in spiritual gifts. Among the triad of virtues mentioned, love is greatest because it expresses the very redemptive character of God, who is love (1 John 4:8), and is the divine motive out of which we were redeemed (John 3:16) (Horton 1999, 130).

**Q:** Is the baptism by the Spirit in 1 Corinthians 12:13 the same as the Pentecostal baptism in Acts 1:5?

**A:** No. The baptism in 1 Corinthians 12:13 is performed by the Holy Spirit to place us in the body of Christ. The baptism in the Holy Spirit in Acts 1:5 is performed by Jesus to spiritually empower believers for Christian service and witness (Acts 1:8).

**Q:** What does God’s distribution of gifts communicate about His passions?

**A:** God loves diversity and expects us to embrace a similar passion.

**Q:** According to Paul, which is superior: love or spiritual gifts?

**A:** Neither. Paul is not saying that love surpasses or replaces the gifts, making them unnecessary. Rather, he presents love as the most excellent way to express or practice the gifts.

### Gifts of Prophecy and Tongues (14:1–40)

#### 5.2.1 — Explain why spiritual gifts are given to the Church.

Explain why spiritual gifts are given to the Church.

The Purpose of Spiritual Gifts

Paul next exhorts the believers to “follow the way of love” (14:1). The verb translated here as follow (Greek diōkō ) also means “to pursue” or often “to persecute.” Perhaps Paul is suggesting that we vigorously and relentlessly “go after” love.

This exhortation links chapter 13 with the whole issue of spiritual gifts and is a fitting conclusion to his instruction on love as the “most excellent way.” From what follows, it appears that the Corinthians needed special instruction concerning two particular gifts: tongues and prophecy. The value that Greek culture placed on rhetorical eloquence made Spirit-inspired speech greatly esteemed. But from what Paul writes, it is clear that the Corinthian believers’ use of tongues (Greek glossolalia ) was not characterized by love. As a result, it did not build up or edify the church (14:4–5, 12), which is the purpose for all spiritual gifts (12:7).

To make this latter point, Paul compares the gifts of tongues and prophecy in their edification value. He prefers prophecy to tongues, not because of its superiority, but because of its relative and functional value. Whereas prophecy edifies the church, the gift of tongues edifies only the speaker unless it is also interpreted. Tongues coupled with interpretation is equal in value to prophecy because they then share an equal degree or scope of edification (14:4–5). Paul gives three illustrations of the fact that tongues without interpretation cannot be understood and therefore is unprofitable in public worship (vv. 7–11). Since love “is not self-seeking” (13:5) but edifying, in a church setting Paul would rather prophesy than speak in tongues.

At the same time, Paul endorses tongues in 14:18. He makes it clear that he is not trying to diminish the value of tongues or limit its use in church. In fact, he later gives a straightforward command: “Do not forbid speaking in tongues” (v. 39). The problem was not with the gift of tongues itself but with its overemphasis and abuse.

Identify the sign value of tongues compared to prophecy.

Comparison of Sign Values

If tongues and interpretation have the same scope of edification as prophecy, why have three gifts when only one would do? Paul answers that tongues and prophecy each have their own distinct purpose.

First, tongues serve as a sign to unbelievers, even as the strange language of the Assyrians served as a sign to rebellious Israel (14:21; Isaiah 28:11). By this, Paul refers to the instance when, after being captured and uprooted from the Promised Land, the Northern Kingdom of Israel received a message from God: the strange, unintelligible words of their Assyrian captors would signify the judgment that the Israelites were no longer in covenant fellowship with Him. Likewise, the gift of tongues signals to unbelievers that they are alienated and lack fellowship with God.

On the other hand, prophecy is a sign to believers; even historically, Israel understood that prophets declared God’s will to His people. Through the gift of prophecy, believers receive a sign that God dwells with His people and reveals His will to them.

Paul then addresses the problem of uninterpreted tongues. The gift of tongues by itself is not understood. Thus, if everyone speaks in tongues at the same time in the public worship setting, confusion reigns, and criticism arises from unbelievers and those unfamiliar 4 with spiritual gifts (1 Corinthians 14:23). However, prophecy is spoken in the language of the people and therefore is understood. Through it, God can speak to all, unbeliever and believer alike. Prophecy becomes the means by which God can convict unbelievers and reveal their true spiritual condition. Thus, prophecy is both a powerful tool and a witness of the Holy Spirit to lead people to accountability before God.

Summarize key principles to ensure that spiritual gifts edify the local church.

Principles for Edification

Paul now gives some rules for the proper and edifying use of spiritual gifts. He opens with the ideal for public worship, for all present to participate by using their spiritual gifts to edify the church (14:26). His words indicate that he is promoting a broad and diverse use of the charismata . This does not mean that their use is without regulation. In fact, the stated restrictions seem to indicate that the Corinthians, in addition to exercising the gifts in an unloving manner, were overusing certain gifts (namely tongues and prophecy) to the neglect of others.

In the case of tongues, Paul suggests that they be limited in use (two or at the most three per service), conducted in an orderly manner (one at a time), and accompanied by the gift of interpretation. With respect to prophecy, Paul gives a similar restriction but emphasizes the need to judge the prophecy’s validity. Undoubtedly, Paul issued these restrictions on prophecy because anyone can claim to speak for God. In the Old Testament, Moses outlined the criteria for judging true prophets and their prophecies (see Deuteronomy 13:1–18; 18:20–22). Similarly, the New Testament gives criteria for detecting false prophets (see Matthew 7:15–23; 1 Corinthians 12:3; 2 Peter 2; and 1 John 2:18–23; 4:1–6). Whereas in the Old Testament false prophets were judged by the revealed will of God in the Law, in the New Testament false prophets were detected by their departure from the apostles’ teaching in regard to the person and work of Christ.

Note that alongside his guidelines for proper order and conduct, Paul states that “you can all prophesy in turn” (1 Corinthians 14:31). This confirms Peter’s perspective in Acts 2:17–18 that in the “last days,” beginning with Pentecost, God has created within His church a community of prophets. Again, as with tongues, Paul does not try to limit those who are used in the gift of prophecy; he merely wants to ensure that its use does not create confusion but builds up the entire church (1 Corinthians 14:31, 33).

Finally, Paul teaches that spiritual gifts must be practiced in submission to spiritual authority. The lack of such submission creates problems, as indicated by his next words. First, some kind of disturbance had occurred involving women talking in the church in an improper way. Paul’s admonition for women to “remain silent” and his statement that women are not permitted to speak cannot be absolute, since earlier (11:5) he instructs women to prophesy with their heads covered.

Scholars have resolved this apparent reversal in a variety of ways, including suggesting that 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 do not belong in the text. 5

A careful reading of the text reveals that this is not a change of focus: Paul continues his instructions related to use of charismatic gifts. Three groups are commanded to refrain from speech under specific conditions using the same term: sigatō . Speakers in tongues are to “ sigatō ” (be silent) if there is no interpreter (1 Corinthians 14:28). Prophets are to “ sigatō ” (be silent) if a judgment or correction is given (1 Corinthians 14:30). Women (wives) are to “ sigatō ” (be silent) in the assembly (1 Corinthians 14:34)—but under what conditions?

Paul has already instructed “the others” to judge prophecy (1 Corinthians 14:29). They could be the entire congregation, but certainly must be at least the other prophets, which includes women (1 Corinthians 11:2–16, esp. v. 5). The restriction here is that wives are to judge (actually, interrogate— eperōtaō ) their husband’s prophecy at home, not in the congregational setting. Although many English translations render eperōtaō as “ask,” this word is found mostly in the Gospels (52 of 56 NT occurrences) and virtually all of these are antagonistic: Jesus is being challenged, questioned, or interrogated by His religious opponents and even Pilate (Mark 15:2, 4) (Kowalski 2017, 171–181).

This problem was symptomatic of a larger problem in Corinth. In their zeal for spiritual gifts (1 Corinthians 14:12), the Corinthians were exercising an unrestrained and independent spiritual authority. They were not properly submissive to the practice of the Church at large (v. 33), not even to its apostolic teaching. Thus, Paul asks them if they thought the Word of God had come to them alone (v. 36). Against this attitude, he gives a challenging rebuke and warning. If they claim to be a “prophet or spiritual,” they should be able to discern that Paul’s instruction carries the authority of the Lord’s commands. If they cannot make this judgment, they themselves will not be recognized by God (Fee 1987, 712).

Paul concludes chapter 14 by repeating the thought with which he began. His stated goal was to help the Corinthians recognize that rather than an excessive and unrestricted use of tongues, they should earnestly desire to edify the church through the use of prophecy (1 Corinthians 14:1). However, he also does not want to leave the impression that this means a cessation of tongues in the church. The command in verse 39 suggests that some might have tried to remedy the problem by forbidding tongues, a course he does not endorse. He gave his instruction and restrictions on tongues and prophecy to promote order and maximize the church’s edification.

Notes for Chapter 5

1. The distribution of the gifts is credited to the Holy Spirit in verse 11, but here (v. 18) it is credited to God the Father. The unity of divine action by the members of the Trinity makes this kind of interchange possible without contradiction.

2. What Paul means by “I gain nothing” (1 Corinthians 13:3) is unclear. He may refer to the lack or loss of reward he mentioned earlier (3:10–15). If so, Paul is stating that only gospel work motivated by love carries the promise of heavenly reward (3:14).

3. The coming of the perfect (13:10) is identified with coming to full knowledge and being fully known. This can refer only to the parousia or second coming of Christ (1 John 3:2–3). Whereas the revelatory gifts of knowledge and prophecy outlive their usefulness at Christ’s second coming, love continues forever as the foundation of our relationship with God.

4. The term idiotes is paired with unbelievers here, but is it elaborating or naming another category of those present? Given the word’s use in Acts 4:13 for the apostles who lacked formal education and in 2 Corinthians 11:6 for unskilled individuals, it seems best to understand this term as referring to those who had not yet experienced spiritual gifts.

5. Verse 35 suggests that Paul has in mind a general teaching or principle of the Law that places a wife in a subordinate status to her husband. The intent of verses 34–35 is so clear that Fee regards them as incompatible with Paul’s teaching elsewhere and argues that these verses are not authentic but later additions to the text (1987, 699–705). However, Fee also admits that textual support for this view is not substantial (1987, 699; see also Carson 1987, 124–125).

Again, as with tongues, Paul does not try to limit those who are used in the gift of prophecy; he merely wants to ensure that its use does not create confusion but builds up the entire church (1 Corinthians 14:31, 33).

Finally, Paul teaches that spiritual gifts must be practiced in submission to spiritual authority. The lack of such submission creates problems, as indicated by his next words. First, some kind of disturbance had occurred involving women talking in the church in an improper way. Paul’s admonition for women to “remain silent” and his statement that women are not permitted to speak cannot be absolute, since earlier (11:5) he instructs women to prophesy with their heads covered.

Scholars have resolved this apparent reversal in a variety of ways, including suggesting that 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 do not belong in the text. 5

A careful reading of the text reveals that this is not a change of focus: Paul continues his instructions related to use of charismatic gifts. Three groups are commanded to refrain from speech under specific conditions using the same term: sigatō . Speakers in tongues are to “ sigatō ” (be silent) if there is no interpreter (1 Corinthians 14:28). Prophets are to “ sigatō ” (be silent) if a judgment or correction is given (1 Corinthians 14:30). Women (wives) are to “ sigatō ” (be silent) in the assembly (1 Corinthians 14:34)—but under what conditions?

Paul has already instructed “the others” to judge prophecy (1 Corinthians 14:29). They could be the entire congregation, but certainly must be at least the other prophets, which includes women (1 Corinthians 11:2–16, esp. v. 5). The restriction here is that wives are to judge (actually, interrogate— eperōtaō ) their husband’s prophecy at home, not in the congregational setting. Although many English translations render eperōtaō as “ask,” this word is found mostly in the Gospels (52 of 56 NT occurrences) and virtually all of these are antagonistic: Jesus is being challenged, questioned, or interrogated by His religious opponents and even Pilate (Mark 15:2, 4) (Kowalski 2017, 171–181).

This problem was symptomatic of a larger problem in Corinth. In their zeal for spiritual gifts (1 Corinthians 14:12), the Corinthians were exercising an unrestrained and independent spiritual authority. They were not properly submissive to the practice of the Church at large (v. 33), not even to its apostolic teaching. Thus, Paul asks them if they thought the Word of God had come to them alone (v. 36). Against this attitude, he gives a challenging rebuke and warning. If they claim to be a “prophet or spiritual,” they should be able to discern that Paul’s instruction carries the authority of the Lord’s commands. If they cannot make this judgment, they themselves will not be recognized by God (Fee 1987, 712).

Paul concludes chapter 14 by repeating the thought with which he began. His stated goal was to help the Corinthians recognize that rather than an excessive and unrestricted use of tongues, they should earnestly desire to edify the church through the use of prophecy (1 Corinthians 14:1). However, he also does not want to leave the impression that this means a cessation of tongues in the church. The command in verse 39 suggests that some might have tried to remedy the problem by forbidding tongues, a course he does not endorse. He gave his instruction and restrictions on tongues and prophecy to promote order and maximize the church’s edification.

## 6 - Christian Doctrine (1 Corinthians 15–16)

### Truths About the Resurrection (15:1–58)

#### 6.1.1 — Describe the doctrinal error that caused Paul to teach on the resurrection.

It is clear from 1 Corinthians 15:12 that some in Corinth were denying the bodily resurrection of believers. Although the exact reason for their denial is uncertain, it likely stems from Greek thought that tended to view the physical body as unimportant. A possible clue lies in 14:37, where Paul sarcastically alludes to his opponents as those who think they are “spiritually gifted.” Some may have regarded the receiving of the Spirit and spiritual gifts (especially tongues) as proof of their “spiritual” status (Fee 1987, 715). In turn, this may have led to the notion that believers now participate in a spiritual realm of existence, making bodily resurrection unnecessary and irrelevant, if not outright unthinkable.

Christ’s Bodily Resurrection: A Historical Fact

Describe the doctrinal error that caused Paul to teach on the resurrection.

Paul begins to answer this doctrinal error by first establishing common ground. He reminds the Corinthian believers of what they already know, believe, and preach: the resurrection of Christ (15:1). They know this doctrine well because Paul had lived among them and taught them the Word of God for eighteen months (Acts 18:11).

In his defense of the resurrection of Christ, Paul establishes several clear truths. First, Jesus’ resurrection is a fact of history, made clear from Paul’s recitation of a long list of eyewitnesses, including Paul himself (1 Corinthians 15:5–8). These same witnesses preached the death, burial, and resurrection of Christ according to the Scriptures (vv. 3–4, 12). That this resurrection was from physical death is established by the details Paul includes: Christ “died” for our sins and “was buried” and then “raised” from death on the third day. Certainly this is the same interpretation that the authors of the Gospels give us.

Christ’s Resurrection: Essential to the Gospel

Identify what is at stake if Christ was not bodily raised from the dead.

Second, the resurrection of Christ is indispensable and foundational to the gospel message (15:1–4). In fact, there is no gospel without it. Paul underscores its importance by reminding the Corinthians that their salvation resulted from believing this message and will stand so long as they continue to believe it (v. 2).

In verses 12–19, Paul outlines the logical consequences of denying the resurrection. It makes no sense to preach the resurrection of Christ and deny the future resurrection of believers, he says. The two are inseparable (v. 12). To deny the resurrection of believers is to deny the resurrection of Christ himself. Such a denial makes the preaching of the gospel not just an empty promise but a lie. Christians who have believed such a gospel are in a pitiful state indeed. They have believed in vain, are still in their sins, and are without the hope of eternal life after death.

Moreover, if Christ was not raised from the dead, Paul and the other apostles are false witnesses who preach a false hope in the resurrection. The hope would be false because it would claim that God has done something He has not—that is, raise Christ from the dead. Yet the redemption purchased through Christ’s death on the cross is ratified and validated by His resurrection. In essence, God declares in Christ’s resurrection that Jesus’ work was accepted and effective. Therefore, since Jesus’ resurrection testifies that God has accepted His death as the atonement for our sins (Romans 4:25), to deny the resurrection can mean only one thing: we are still in our sins, our faith in the gospel is worthless, and our hope in eternal life is a pitiful illusion (1 Corinthians 15:18–19). However, the gospel gives Christians the hope of conquering death along with Christ (vv. 20–25). His resurrection is but the “firstfruits,” the pledge of a future harvest that is sure to come.

Christ’s Victory over Death

Explain the event that will signal Christ’s ultimate conquest of death and the realization of the Christian’s hope.

The hope of a future resurrection naturally raises the issue of life after death. In 15:35–50, Paul argues that the resurrection is necessary to prepare believers for their eternal home in heaven. He uses the creation of animal species to illustrate that bodies are adapted to different habitats. As the human body is adapted for life on earth, so life in heaven will demand a different kind of body, suited for a new and different environment. Theologian Stan Grenz refers to humans as “embodied beings,” arguing that the body is indispensable to being human (1998, 29–43). Thus, our natural and perishable bodies must be outfitted for a heavenly existence. This will require a body that is spiritual, imperishable, and eternal.

Paul now tells us that the transformation accompanying our future resurrection will occur in a split second. The language Paul uses is apocalyptic and depicts the parousia or triumphant second coming of Christ. The imagery, including God’s trumpet signaling His triumphal deliverance and ultimate victory over death for believers (1 Corinthians 15:51–52), strongly suggests that Paul has in mind the Rapture and resurrection depicted in 1 Thessalonians 4:13–17. For Paul, the resurrection of believers declares and completes Christ’s victory over death, purchased by His death and resurrection. Using Old Testament passages that depict the humiliation of a conquered foe (Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:10–14), Paul taunts and mocks death in light of Christ’s conquest of it (1 Corinthians 15:54–55).

Implicit in this dramatic portrayal is a stunning truth: sin was not part of God’s eternal plan for humans, nor death as its consequence. Death is God’s enemy (vv. 25–26), and metaphorically speaking, God in Christ went to war to defeat it. Believers can gratefully rejoice in Christ’s victory over death. Its sting has been removed, its power broken. Therefore, believers can stand firm in their faith and be fruitful in the work of the Lord, knowing that their eternal future is secure.

**Q:** How does Paul counteract the idea that Jesus’ resurrection was an illusion?

**A:** There would be no reason to refer to the resurrection as “from the dead” (15:12), mention that Jesus was “buried” (v. 4), or identify the numerous eyewitnesses unless Paul believed the event to be an actual physical, bodily resurrection.

**Q:** Besides Christ’s resurrection, what is fundamental to the gospel message?

**A:** Answers might include Jesus’ death and God’s grace.

**Q:** Biblically speaking, is it correct to refer to the “souls” that will be in heaven?

**A:** No. Although we sometimes speak that way in reference to a person who dies and goes to be with the Lord, we need to remember that the body is integral to our nature as humans and will not be discarded in our eternal heavenly existence. This is precisely why there must be a resurrection.

## 7 - Paul’s Recent Past Revisited (2 Corinthians 1:1–2:11)

### Introduction to 2 Corinthians (1:1–2)

#### 7.1.1 — Summarize the historical circumstances that led Paul to write 2 Corinthians.

Summarize the historical circumstances that led Paul to write 2 Corinthians.

Paul’s interaction with Corinth was long and complex (Hernando 1999, 915–917); he knew the congregation well. Acts tells us that Paul founded the Corinthian church during his second missionary journey and labored there for eighteen months (Acts 18:1–11). From his perspective, and probably theirs as well, Paul was their spiritual father (1 Corinthians 4:15). Given his long-standing history with the Corinthian church, it is not surprising that Paul frequently reminds them of his conduct among them when answering the accusations and charges of his opponents.

According to the information in 1 and 2 Corinthians and Acts, Paul had written at least four letters to the Corinthians and visited them twice (Acts 18:1; 20:1–2; 2 Corinthians 2:1–4). They had written at least one letter to him (1 Corinthians 7:1) and sent a delegation to deliver it (16:17), after which Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus (see chapter 1 to review a detailed history behind Paul’s writing of 1 Corinthians). Following is a summary of the events between Paul’s writing of 1 Corinthians and the writing of 2 Corinthians:

### Comfort in Affliction (1:3–11)

#### 7.2.1 — Identify truths Paul teaches that can help a Christian survive trials.

Identify truths Paul teaches that can help a Christian survive trials.

God of Comfort

Following the introductory greeting in his epistles, Paul usually commends the specific church by thanking God for some demonstration of their faith or new life in Christ. But this time, the memory of a recent deliverance seems so intense that Paul instead begins by praising God for His grace in the midst of trials (2 Corinthians 1:8–11). God’s character of compassion or mercy (Exodus 33:19; Psalm 111:4) and comfort (Isaiah 40:1; 66:13) was well known to His people in the Old Testament. Mercy ( oiktirmos ) is the quality of God that moves Him to respond with kindness and compassion toward those who are suffering or in distress. Comfort ( paraklēsis , 2 Corinthians 1:3) refers to His help and carries the idea of consolation or encouragement. From personal experience, Paul testifies that God is a source of boundless mercy and comfort for Christians who are suffering.

Clarify why God allows trials in believers’ lives.

Divine Purpose in Trials

Part of Paul’s comfort comes from taking God’s perspective on trials. According to 2 Corinthians 1:4, enduring trials through divine comfort is part of God’s plan to equip and qualify us as His comforting agents. This principle of identification lies at the heart of the incarnation of Christ, who, as our high priest, is able to “sympathize with our weaknesses” because He was subjected to the same human temptations yet was without sin (Hebrews 2:10; 4:14–16).

Paul’s own afflictions and the comfort he received from God prepare him to offer divine encouragement to the Corinthians when they encounter trials (2 Corinthians 1:5–6). Note that the Greek word soteria , translated “salvation” in verse 6, does not always designate eternal salvation but can mean deliverance or rescue from peril (Luke 17:1; Acts 7:25). Paul may refer to the fact that his afflictions resulted in not only comfort but also the message of salvation. Whatever the case, Paul’s encouragement enables the believers to persevere in faith through the “same sufferings” he endured, thus ensuring their deliverance.

As Paul’s experience demonstrates, the Corinthians can be reassured of God’s help. Paul faced a deadly peril in Asia. In fact, the burden of the trial was so severe that he “despaired even of life” (v. 8) and felt as if a “sentence of death” had already been passed on him (v. 9). But the God who raises the dead had repeatedly delivered Paul. Paul sets his hope on Him for all future deliverance (vv. 8–10).

**Q:** How was God’s comfort or mercy ministered to you personally?

**Q:** How does your enduring a similar trial provide comfort and encouragement to a suffering person?

**A:** The experience of a similar trial enables you to have sympathy and compassion and increases the likelihood that the person suffering will detect and receive your comfort and concern. A person undergoing a trial needs to know that God is faithful to deliver us and provide grace to endure trials. The individual who can testify of God’s faithfulness encourages suffering people to both trust God for deliverance and persevere in faith.

**Q:** Did Paul see the resurrection as only a future event?

**A:** No. The resurrection is eschatological, but it also had a present reality for Paul. He saw the power of the resurrection at work in his own life, bringing deliverance from deadly peril (2 Corinthians 4:8–14).

### Paul Defends His Change in Plans (1:12–2:11)

#### 7.3.1 — Analyze Paul’s response to unfair and false accusations, and identify principles to help believers deal with such situations today.

Analyze Paul’s response to unfair and false accusations, and identify principles to help believers deal with such situations today.

Integrity in Ministry

Throughout 2 Corinthians, we see evidence that Paul’s opponents are falsely accusing him. In fact, he mentions his “deadly peril” primarily to silence accusations that his delay and change of plans prove that he cannot be trusted to keep his promises (1:15–2:4). Paul wants the Corinthians to know that nothing less than the severest of trials could have prevented him from coming as planned.

But Paul is unshaken by these accusations, not only because they are unfounded, but also because his conscience is clear. He has walked before them and “in the world” with integrity and transparent openness. The word sincerity (Greek eilikrineia ) in verse 12 carries the idea of purity in motive (see also 2:17). Furthermore, Paul’s conduct has depended not on worldly (or “fleshly,” Greek sarkikos ) wisdom but on the grace of God. Christian workers need to take heart and instruction from Paul’s example. There is no better antidote for unjust criticism than a clear conscience before God.

As a result of Paul’s sincerity, his epistles have the character of integrity. He writes what he means, and he means what he writes. There is no secret message or hidden meaning behind his words. Paul preaches the message of God. Since God is faithful, Paul’s words are likewise as sure and trustworthy as the promises of God in Christ (1:17–20).

This section portrays Paul’s pastoral heart and wisdom. Even while recognizing difficulty in his relationship with the Corinthians, his words to them are filled with encouragement. He expresses hope that they will come to fully understand and appreciate his true motives and ministry among them (vv. 13–14). At the same time, he affirms that they have already begun to do so. His hope looks ahead to the “day of the Lord Jesus” (v. 14) when they can be as proud of him as he is of them.

Paul understands the importance of being open and honest in his relationships. Rather than trying to defend his delay in coming to them and conceal his true motives, he tells them the truth. His recent visit was so painful for him that he did not want to repeat it (1:23–2:4). Discipline may have been necessary, but as a true spiritual father, Paul did not enjoy making them sorrowful.

Identify truths or principles Paul teaches about church discipline.

Discipline and Detachment

It seems clear from 2 Corinthians 2:5–11 that Paul’s discipline focused on a certain individual who challenged Paul’s authority. Following Paul’s tearful letter (2:4), the church repented and disciplined this erring brother.

In their zeal to make things right (7:11), however, they may have been somewhat harsh. Rather than gloat over his personal vindication, Paul counsels them to cease punishing the man for fear of crushing his spirit with excessive grief (2:7). In doing so, Paul shows a remarkable ability to detach himself from his personal feelings and see the larger picture. At stake is the spiritual well-being of a fallen brother and, beyond that, of the church at large (v. 10).

Paul also realizes that true discipline seeks not to punish but to correct and restore a broken relationship. Thus, he urges the Corinthian believers to extend forgiveness to the brother, even as Paul himself has done. Forgiveness is foundational to wholesome relationships in the body of Christ and mirrors the grace of God shown to us in Christ (Colossians 3:13).

Again, the purpose of church discipline is to bring reconciliation and complete restoration to spiritual health and unity. To accomplish this, Paul urges the believers to comfort the brother and reaffirm their love for him (2 Corinthians 2:7–8). Paul warns them that excessive discipline can overwhelm a person with sorrow, giving Satan an opportunity to turn the grief into resentment and bitterness, and the zeal for discipline into prideful vengeance.

**Q:** Does having integrity safeguard ministers from false accusations?

**A:** No. Even ministers of sterling character are often attacked by those who oppose the work of the gospel or by worldly Christians who misunderstand the ministers’ true motives.

**Q:** How did Paul respond to his opponents’ accusations?

**A:** Not with defensiveness or indignation but with calm honesty

**Q:** What is the best way for a church to discipline a pastoral staff member caught in moral failure?

**A:** Answers will vary but should reflect the principles of (a) restoring the spiritual health and unity of the person and the congregation and (b) correcting and restoring the broken relationship (forgiveness). Note, however, that reconciliation and forgiveness are not enabling; that is, they do not require us to ignore the offense or place the person back in his or her former position without any consequence. 8 CHAPTER

## 8 - Paul’s Apostolic Ministry (2 Corinthians 2:12–7:16)

### The Greatness of Paul’s Ministry (2:12–4:6)

#### 8.1.1 — Discuss the relationship between uncertainty and faith.

Discuss the relationship between uncertainty and faith.

Paul now briefly returns to the story that explains his recent conduct and change of plans. Having sent his third Corinthian letter with Titus, Paul was left to wait for news. He moved northward to Troas, where he planned to meet Titus if things went well at Corinth. When he did not find Titus in Troas, he went on to Macedonia. Paul must have waited for news from Titus with a good deal of prayerful anxiety. No doubt when Titus failed to meet him at Troas, Paul’s fears seemed to materialize. The situation left him with “no peace of mind” (2:13; literally, “no rest for my spirit”).

Fear often paralyzes, apart from the grace of God. Paul drew upon God’s grace (v. 14) and remained engaged in the work of the Lord. Moreover, in spite of his anxieties, inner turmoil, and the uncertain future, Paul was confident of a victorious outcome. In verse 14, he demonstrates that confidence by expressing grateful praise to God. He is undoubtedly remembering how his anxious fears were put to rest when Titus came with encouraging news (7:6, 13).

Explain how Paul drew confidence from God.

Confidence in Christ’s Triumph

In thanking God (2:14), Paul draws his readers’ minds to a rare but memorable scene: the Roman triumphal procession. Marking a monumental victory by a Roman general, the triumphal procession included a parade of the captives, slaves, and spoils of war from the conquered enemy territory, followed by the honored general and some of his troops (“Roman Triumph” 2006). In 1 Corinthians 4:9, Paul is one of the captives at the very end of the procession, “condemned to die in the arena.” Here, Paul portrays Christ as the victor who leads Paul as one of his captives in public display and testimony to the triumph of His redemption. 1 The paradox is that, while Christ has taken us captive (Psalm 68:18; Ephesians 4:7–8, 10), He has given us ultimate freedom from our slavery to sin. Paul draws confidence in knowing that his life is in Christ who has already conquered sin and death (1 Corinthians 15:54–57).

Through preaching the gospel, Paul declares God’s victory in Christ over sin and death. At the same time, he realizes that his preaching helps determine the eternal destiny of individuals. It is a matter of spiritual life and death. Paul is humbled by the thought and admits his inadequacy for such an awesome responsibility. However, Paul’s confidence is not in himself but in his integrity before God. He proclaims the gospel with a pure and sincere heart before God. He is not like so many of his day who “peddled the word of God” (Martin 1986, 344).

The thought of his opponents (2 Corinthians 2:17) causes Paul to further distance himself from them. Neither the character of his ministry nor the content of his preaching leaves much to be compared with his opponents. Unlike them, the affirmation of his ministry does not rest on human approval. He does not need the external testimony of letters recommending his apostleship. Rather, the validation of his ministry comes through the inward work of the Spirit seen in the Corinthians themselves (3:1–3).

Contrast the old covenant of law with the new covenant.

Paul draws his confidence from the fact that God in Christ has established a new covenant, a covenant that stands in stark contrast to the old. Unlike the covenant of law, the new covenant provides the dynamic and transforming power of the Holy Spirit (3:4–6).

To make his point, Paul refers to the old covenant as “the ministry that brought death” (v. 7) and states that “the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life” (v. 6). In Romans, Paul says that knowledge of sin comes through the Law (Romans 3:20) and that the Law brings God’s wrath (4:15). The Law provokes and increases the incidence of sin by working through the fallen human nature’s sinful passions (7:5, 8). In this way, it becomes an instrument of death. Ironically, although the Law itself is “holy, righteous and good” (7:12) and was intended “to bring life,” it “actually brought death” and condemnation (7:10) in exposing our sin.

By contrast, the new covenant brings to all God’s people the life-giving power of the Spirit through the new birth (John 3:3–8) and regeneration (Titus 3:5). Paul celebrates this freedom brought by the Spirit in Romans 8:1–4.

When Paul contemplates the magnitude and supernatural character of his ministry, he is humbled. He realizes that nothing within him is adequate for the demands and responsibilities of his apostolic calling. He acknowledges that the work and power of God make him adequate for the task (2 Corinthians 3:5). Moreover, it is through the Spirit that Paul is empowered to fulfill this calling (v. 6).

Glory in the New Covenant

In 2 Corinthians 3, Paul presents the new covenant’s superiority by comparing and contrasting its revealed glory of God to that of the old covenant. He affirms that the former covenant came with glory (v. 7) and reminds the Corinthians of its inauguration on Mount Sinai. At that time, the revelation of God was so resplendent that Moses carried the afterglow of his divine encounter down the mountain with him. But Paul quickly points out that the glory was fading and that the “ministry of the Spirit” comes with “even more” glory (v. 8). God made Paul a minister of this new covenant of the Spirit, which, in contrast to the Law, gives life and comes with the greater glory of the life-giving Spirit (vv. 6–11). This abiding glory is available to all believers in Christ.

The contrast between old and new is clear from the gospel Paul preaches, but those who tie their salvation to keeping the Law resist his message. Elsewhere (Galatians 4:3; 5:1), Paul describes this approach to salvation as spiritual bondage. Here Paul likens the condition to spiritual blindness, which is fostered by their adherence to (“reading” of) the Law. Their eyes remain unseeing until they turn in faith to Christ and receive the life-giving liberty of the Spirit (2 Corinthians 3:12–17).

The Spirit provides freedom and empowers believers to serve the Lord. This provision also comes with a revelation of God’s glory (vv. 16, 18) and the power of spiritual transformation. By beholding the revelation of God’s glory now presented in Christ, believers are progressively changed into the same glorious image. As Paul describes it, this transformation occurs “with ever-increasing glory” (v. 18).

Paul recognizes the difficulty and challenges of bringing such a revelation to people who have been spiritually blinded by the god of this world (4:4). The opposition is great, and he acknowledges the false charges against him, even that of preaching to conceal rather than reveal the truth (v. 3). Nevertheless, he is determined to illuminate the world of spiritual darkness by preaching Jesus Christ, who is the “image of God.” The light of the gospel is the revelation of Christ himself and His divine glory. Thus, the gospel is God’s instrument of illumination, bringing the revelation of His glory seen “in the face of Christ” (vv. 1–6).

**Q:** Do feelings of uncertainty and anxiety indicate an absence of faith?

**A:** No. Uncertainty results from our lack of knowledge. Anxiety is the normal result of our concern and inability to control future events and outcomes. Paul had both, but he continued in the work of the Lord and trusted God to bring about His will and purpose. That perseverance testifies to the presence, not absence, of faith.

**A:** Answers will vary.

**Q:** How is the glory of the new covenant different from that of the old?

**A:** Under the old covenant, the glory was temporary; it faded. The glory of the new covenant is lasting and increasing and comes with the life-giving Spirit.

Explain how Paul drew confidence from God.

Confidence in Christ’s Triumph

In thanking God (2:14), Paul draws his readers’ minds to a rare but memorable scene: the Roman triumphal procession. Marking a monumental victory by a Roman general, the triumphal procession included a parade of the captives, slaves, and spoils of war from the conquered enemy territory, followed by the honored general and some of his troops (“Roman Triumph” 2006). In 1 Corinthians 4:9, Paul is one of the captives at the very end of the procession, “condemned to die in the arena.” Here, Paul portrays Christ as the victor who leads Paul as one of his captives in public display and testimony to the triumph of His redemption. 1 The paradox is that, while Christ has taken us captive (Psalm 68:18; Ephesians 4:7–8, 10), He has given us ultimate freedom from our slavery to sin. Paul draws confidence in knowing that his life is in Christ who has already conquered sin and death (1 Corinthians 15:54–57).

Through preaching the gospel, Paul declares God’s victory in Christ over sin and death. At the same time, he realizes that his preaching helps determine the eternal destiny of individuals. It is a matter of spiritual life and death. Paul is humbled by the thought and admits his inadequacy for such an awesome responsibility. However, Paul’s confidence is not in himself but in his integrity before God. He proclaims the gospel with a pure and sincere heart before God. He is not like so many of his day who “peddled the word of God” (Martin 1986, 344).

The thought of his opponents (2 Corinthians 2:17) causes Paul to further distance himself from them. Neither the character of his ministry nor the content of his preaching leaves much to be compared with his opponents. Unlike them, the affirmation of his ministry does not rest on human approval. He does not need the external testimony of letters recommending his apostleship. Rather, the validation of his ministry comes through the inward work of the Spirit seen in the Corinthians themselves (3:1–3).

Contrast the old covenant of law with the new covenant.

Paul draws his confidence from the fact that God in Christ has established a new covenant, a covenant that stands in stark contrast to the old. Unlike the covenant of law, the new covenant provides the dynamic and transforming power of the Holy Spirit (3:4–6).

To make his point, Paul refers to the old covenant as “the ministry that brought death” (v. 7) and states that “the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life” (v. 6). In Romans, Paul says that knowledge of sin comes through the Law (Romans 3:20) and that the Law brings God’s wrath (4:15). The Law provokes and increases the incidence of sin by working through the fallen human nature’s sinful passions (7:5, 8). In this way, it becomes an instrument of death. Ironically, although the Law itself is “holy, righteous and good” (7:12) and was intended “to bring life,” it “actually brought death” and condemnation (7:10) in exposing our sin.

By contrast, the new covenant brings to all God’s people the life-giving power of the Spirit through the new birth (John 3:3–8) and regeneration (Titus 3:5). Paul celebrates this freedom brought by the Spirit in Romans 8:1–4.

When Paul contemplates the magnitude and supernatural character of his ministry, he is humbled. He realizes that nothing within him is adequate for the demands and responsibilities of his apostolic calling. He acknowledges that the work and power of God make him adequate for the task (2 Corinthians 3:5). Moreover, it is through the Spirit that Paul is empowered to fulfill this calling (v. 6).

Glory in the New Covenant

In 2 Corinthians 3, Paul presents the new covenant’s superiority by comparing and contrasting its revealed glory of God to that of the old covenant. He affirms that the former covenant came with glory (v. 7) and reminds the Corinthians of its inauguration on Mount Sinai. At that time, the revelation of God was so resplendent that Moses carried the afterglow of his divine encounter down the mountain with him. But Paul quickly points out that the glory was fading and that the “ministry of the Spirit” comes with “even more” glory (v. 8). God made Paul a minister of this new covenant of the Spirit, which, in contrast to the Law, gives life and comes with the greater glory of the life-giving Spirit (vv. 6–11). This abiding glory is available to all believers in Christ.

The contrast between old and new is clear from the gospel Paul preaches, but those who tie their salvation to keeping the Law resist his message. Elsewhere (Galatians 4:3; 5:1), Paul describes this approach to salvation as spiritual bondage. Here Paul likens the condition to spiritual blindness, which is fostered by their adherence to (“reading” of) the Law. Their eyes remain unseeing until they turn in faith to Christ and receive the life-giving liberty of the Spirit (2 Corinthians 3:12–17).

The Spirit provides freedom and empowers believers to serve the Lord. This provision also comes with a revelation of God’s glory (vv. 16, 18) and the power of spiritual transformation. By beholding the revelation of God’s glory now presented in Christ, believers are progressively changed into the same glorious image. As Paul describes it, this transformation occurs “with ever-increasing glory” (v. 18).

Paul recognizes the difficulty and challenges of bringing such a revelation to people who have been spiritually blinded by the god of this world (4:4). The opposition is great, and he acknowledges the false charges against him, even that of preaching to conceal rather than reveal the truth (v. 3). Nevertheless, he is determined to illuminate the world of spiritual darkness by preaching Jesus Christ, who is the “image of God.” The light of the gospel is the revelation of Christ himself and His divine glory. Thus, the gospel is God’s instrument of illumination, bringing the revelation of His glory seen “in the face of Christ” (vv. 1–6).

### Ministry’s Trials and Triumph (4:7–5:9)

#### 8.2.1 — Examine the paradoxes of value in earthen vessels and of life through death.

In the previous section, Paul spoke in glowing terms of his ministry under the new covenant with the great and glorious hope that awaits believers. The gospel he preaches is indeed a treasure. It is the revelation of God’s glory in the face of Christ (4:4, 6). Through it, God has brought His inextinguishable light into the world (John 1:4–10). Paul understands not only his role as a chosen vessel to bear that light to both Jew and Gentile, but also the suffering his calling demands (Acts 9:15–16).

Examine the paradoxes of value in earthen vessels and of life through death.

Now Paul presents us with a paradox: a “treasure in jars of clay” (2 Corinthians 4:7). He is awed by the contrast between the untold wealth and enduring value of this “gospel-treasure” and the unworthiness and human weakness (“clay”) of those who now carry it to the world. God has chosen to display the greatness of His power through the weakness of humans, so there can be no mistake about who is at work.

The paradox continues as the gospel declares that God has brought life through death. Jesus died and was resurrected that believers might receive His life. Paul and his associates model this truth by repeatedly risking their lives to bring the life-giving message of the gospel (vv. 10–11). Moreover, God’s resurrection life is at work in sustaining Paul and his fellow workers and delivering them from physical death (vv. 8–9). Paul describes these experiences as identifying with and even participating in the death of Jesus.

In mentioning Christ’s death and his own participation in it, Paul completes the paradox. Jesus’ shame-filled death on the cross has become the occasion and instrument to further the glory of God (v. 15). In those who suffer for the sake of the gospel, it produces an incomparable and eternal weight of glory (v. 17). In other words, any suffering that might be endured for the cause of Christ is insignificant (“light”) and fleeting (“momentary”) and not worthy to be compared to the eternal glory.

It now becomes clear that Paul is sustained in the midst of his trials by his hope and faith in the promise of a resurrection. 2 Even the prospect of death cannot shake his confidence because he is assured that when his body, like a tent, becomes worn out, torn down, and destroyed, God will provide a new one (5:1). But unlike his earthly tent-dwelling, this “building from God” belongs to the heavenly realm and is eternal.

With such statements, Paul shows that, unlike segments of the Greek world, he views the body not as inherently evil but as an essential part of humanity (Grenz, Guretzki, and Nordling 1999, 16). Without the physical body, a human being is incomplete. Therefore, we long to be clothed with our heavenly bodies after death, bodies that are suited for eternal life (1 Corinthians 15:35–50; 2 Corinthians 5:2–4; Hernando 1999, 938–939).

Furthermore, Paul knows that death does not end a believer’s relationship with the Lord; rather, it is the beginning of greater intimacy. He describes it as being “at home with the Lord” (2 Corinthians 5:8). Death does not terminate our communion with Christ but brings us into His presence. Resurrection creates a continuity between this life and the life to come. The common denominator is the Holy Spirit, whom we have as a down payment now but will know in fullness in heaven (v. 5). Therefore, while the exchange of our earthly body for an eternal one is essential, the most important element of our coming resurrection is the continuity of our relationship with Christ. Then, as now, we will be able to love and serve our Lord Jesus Christ. We will still seek to please Him, even as we make that our supreme ambition in this life.

**Q:** Does death bring an end to our service to Christ?

**A:** No. Although our earthly service ends with death, both our relationship with Christ and our service to Him continue.

### Ministry’s Nature and Function (5:10–6:10)

#### 8.3.1 — Indicate how both fear and love motivated Paul in apostolic ministry.

Indicate how both fear and love motivated Paul in apostolic ministry.

Motivations and Message of Paul’s Ministry

The thought of our eternal service to God raises a sober truth for Paul: all believers will one day stand before the judgment seat of Christ and give an account of their lives. Our deeds, whether good or bad, will be judged and recompensed (5:10).

Paul applies this truth to his own calling as an apostle, outlining two major motivations that guide his ministry. First, the realization that he will one day give an account of his apostleship inserts the fear of the Lord (v. 11) into the equation. This fear is not a paralyzing terror but an awe-filled reverence for the sacredness and eternal consequences of his service. In the Old Testament, the “fear of the Lord” was to characterize God’s people and enable them to walk in wisdom (Proverbs 1:7) and avoid evil (Job 28:28; Proverbs 16:6). The fear of the Lord was required if Israel was to walk in God’s ways, love Him, and serve Him with complete devotion (Deuteronomy 10:12).

In the Early Church, living or walking in the fear of the Lord was associated with the work of the Spirit (Acts 9:31). In other passages, after exhorting believers to be filled with the Spirit, Paul tells them to submit to one another “out of reverence for (fear of) Christ” (Ephesians 5:21). In 2 Corinthians 7:1, he presents reverence or fear of God as the attitude believers should have as they perfect holiness in their lives. Apparently in Paul’s mind, then, such an attitude of reverence should characterize the life of the Spirit-filled church. On a personal level, such an attitude motivates him to persuade individuals to accept the gospel of Christ.

Second, the love of Christ compels Paul to serve (2 Corinthians 5:14–15), just as it should be the chief motivator behind every believer’s service and obedience to Christ (Matthew 22:37; Mark 12:30; Luke 10:27; John 14:15, 21, 23). The context of Paul’s statement clarifies that it is Christ’s love for him, not Paul’s for Christ, that compels him. 3 When Paul evaluates the magnitude of Jesus’ boundless and universal love, seen in His selfless death for all, Paul must serve; he can do nothing less. What Jesus did compels Paul to serve Him out of gratitude and love.

The cross and resurrection have brought an end to Paul’s former life. His union with Christ ushers him into a totally new sphere of existence. He is a new man with a new identity (Galatians 2:20) and belongs to another world. Paul speaks of the believer’s preconversion life as being “in Adam” and contrasts it with being “in Christ” (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22). The contrast is the difference between death and life. The change is so dramatic that it can be described only as a “new creation” (2 Corinthians 5:17). All the old things of his former life—its relations, conditions, and situations—have passed away and have been replaced by a new life “in Christ.”

Such great changes can come only from God (v. 18). To bring them about, God needed to do something about the sin problem. Paul gives us the solution in a word: reconciliation. Reconciliation is the most relational aspect of God’s redemption. Paul sees himself as Christ’s ambassador and God’s voice of reconciliation (v. 20), delivering the good news: God and humankind are no longer enemies. Through Christ’s death, God has removed the enmity that stood between humanity and himself and replaced it with peace (Romans 5:10–12; Ephesians 2:13–15).

Explain why Paul is not ashamed at his sufferings endured for the sake of Christ.

The Commendation of Paul’s Ministry

In 6:3–10, Paul describes the manner of his life and ministry. Verse 3 continues the sentence that began in 6:1, interrupted by the Old Testament quotation in 6:2. Connected, it would read, “[As] we urge you not to receive God’s grace in vain . . . , we put no stumbling block in anyone’s path.” That is, while exhorting others to receive God’s saving grace, Paul’s utmost concern is to never hinder the work of that grace in anyone. Always and in everything, he desires to live in a way that speaks well of him as a servant of God. Despite unimaginable sufferings and hardships, Paul conducts himself in a commendable manner (v. 4).

Note that Paul is not ashamed of the hardships he suffers for the sake of Christ. In fact, in this epistle, he intentionally showcases them (4:8–9; 11:23–29; 12:10). As noted in 4:7–11, Paul realizes that his sufferings draw attention not only to God’s power operating through human weakness but also to the common experience Paul shares with his Savior.

**Q:** Who will face Christ’s judgment in the end-time?

**A:** Not only those who reject Christ but also believers, whose works of service will be judged (1 Corinthians 3:12–15). However, this judgment is not of believers’ sins, which were judged at the cross when Jesus died.

**Q:** Do you view your hardships as blessings or curses?

**A:** Like Paul’s, our troubles can showcase God’s power and draw us nearer to Christ, if we let them.

### Ministry’s Intimacy and Joy (6:11–7:16)

#### 8.4.1 — Describe Paul’s joy in his relationship with the Corinthians.

True Affection

Describe Paul’s joy in his relationship with the Corinthians.

Paul breaks off his account of apostolic sufferings with an emotional appeal to the Corinthians for mutual openness and affection (6:11–13). This type of direct address is rare in Paul’s letters; when it occurs, it reveals his intense passion, affection, and concern. Here, as the Corinthians’ “father” in the faith (1 Corinthians 4:15), Paul longs for intimacy with his spiritual children. Knowing that true affection is freely given, however, he coaxes but does not command. He urges them to return his affection, to “open wide” their hearts toward him as he has done toward them.

It seems strange that amid a plea for mutual affection and openness, Paul would turn so sharply to an unresolved problem he had already warned the Corinthians against: idolatry (1 Corinthians 10:1–22). Perhaps, given the prominence of idolatry in the Corinthian culture (Murphy-O’Conner 1984, 147–159), Paul’s earlier outright condemnation and threat of judgment fell on deaf ears. His words of warning are harsh, but his severity comes precisely because he has such love for them. He knows that idolatry would destroy their relationship with God.

Note that in his command not to be bound together with unbelievers (2 Corinthians 6:14), Paul is not forbidding all associations with them. That would be impossible and would not advance the kingdom of God. Rather, he appears to refer to forming relationships that would harness a believer and unbeliever, leading to some form of spiritual compromise with paganism, particularly idolatry. Exactly what kind of relationships he had in mind is uncertain; Paul was not specific. Yet he leaves us a clear principle to apply under the guidance of the Holy Spirit: Christians would be wise to avoid entering any relationship—personal, business, or otherwise—that forces them into situations of compromise and threatens the purity of their devotion to Christ (see 2 Corinthians 11:3).

Identify how Paul safeguarded and restored his relationship with the Corinthians.

Repentance and Restoration

With 7:6, Paul returns to the narrative about his canceled plans, which he broke off after 2:13. To review, earlier problems had caused Paul to make a hasty visit to Corinth in an effort to set things right, but the visit had only made things worse and was deeply and personally painful for him. Shortly after returning to Ephesus and still hurting from his experience with the Corinthian believers, he wrote a stern and impassioned letter of rebuke to the church (2:4–5) and sent it by Titus. His hopes of reconnecting with Titus at Troas were met with disappointment (2:12), so he pressed on to Macedonia.

For Paul, Titus’ eventual arrival in Macedonia was nothing less than God’s merciful comfort. Titus told of the Corinthians’ genuine sorrow for sins against Paul and their affection and passionate concern for him. The discipline Paul had been forced to exercise was worth it because it produced “godly sorrow” and true repentance (7:8–11). The momentary sorrow he caused them had become the occasion of rejoicing as his relationship with them was now restored.

#### 8.4.2 — Identify how Paul safeguarded and restored his relationship with the Corinthians.

True Affection

Describe Paul’s joy in his relationship with the Corinthians.

Paul breaks off his account of apostolic sufferings with an emotional appeal to the Corinthians for mutual openness and affection (6:11–13). This type of direct address is rare in Paul’s letters; when it occurs, it reveals his intense passion, affection, and concern. Here, as the Corinthians’ “father” in the faith (1 Corinthians 4:15), Paul longs for intimacy with his spiritual children. Knowing that true affection is freely given, however, he coaxes but does not command. He urges them to return his affection, to “open wide” their hearts toward him as he has done toward them.

It seems strange that amid a plea for mutual affection and openness, Paul would turn so sharply to an unresolved problem he had already warned the Corinthians against: idolatry (1 Corinthians 10:1–22). Perhaps, given the prominence of idolatry in the Corinthian culture (Murphy-O’Conner 1984, 147–159), Paul’s earlier outright condemnation and threat of judgment fell on deaf ears. His words of warning are harsh, but his severity comes precisely because he has such love for them. He knows that idolatry would destroy their relationship with God.

Note that in his command not to be bound together with unbelievers (2 Corinthians 6:14), Paul is not forbidding all associations with them. That would be impossible and would not advance the kingdom of God. Rather, he appears to refer to forming relationships that would harness a believer and unbeliever, leading to some form of spiritual compromise with paganism, particularly idolatry. Exactly what kind of relationships he had in mind is uncertain; Paul was not specific. Yet he leaves us a clear principle to apply under the guidance of the Holy Spirit: Christians would be wise to avoid entering any relationship—personal, business, or otherwise—that forces them into situations of compromise and threatens the purity of their devotion to Christ (see 2 Corinthians 11:3).

Repentance and Restoration

With 7:6, Paul returns to the narrative about his canceled plans, which he broke off after 2:13. To review, earlier problems had caused Paul to make a hasty visit to Corinth in an effort to set things right, but the visit had only made things worse and was deeply and personally painful for him. Shortly after returning to Ephesus and still hurting from his experience with the Corinthian believers, he wrote a stern and impassioned letter of rebuke to the church (2:4–5) and sent it by Titus. His hopes of reconnecting with Titus at Troas were met with disappointment (2:12), so he pressed on to Macedonia.

For Paul, Titus’ eventual arrival in Macedonia was nothing less than God’s merciful comfort. Titus told of the Corinthians’ genuine sorrow for sins against Paul and their affection and passionate concern for him. The discipline Paul had been forced to exercise was worth it because it produced “godly sorrow” and true repentance (7:8–11). The momentary sorrow he caused them had become the occasion of rejoicing as his relationship with them was now restored.

## 9 - The Macedonian Collection (2 Corinthians 8–9)

### The Grace of Giving (8:1–9:5)

#### 9.1.1 — Summarize what the Macedonian giving teaches about Christian stewardship.

Summarize what the Macedonian giving teaches about Christian stewardship.

Personal Sacrifice and Devotion

To encourage the Corinthians to resume their collection for the Jerusalem believers, Paul points to the praiseworthy example of the Macedonian churches. Although the Macedonians had experienced intense persecution (1 Thessalonians 1:6–7; 2:14) that left them deeply impoverished, they responded with “rich generosity” and gave sacrificially in this collection for the poor (2 Corinthians 8:2–3). It seems that the Macedonians gave not only willingly but spontaneously and with great joy. For Paul, their selfless conduct is evidence of the operation and enablement of God’s grace (v. 1). 2

The grace that enables such a generous and selfless act flowed out of the Macedonians’ devotion to the Lord (v. 5) and a genuine compassion and love for others (vv. 7–8). As John would later indicate, these two aspects of the Christian life—love for God and love for others—are inseparable (1 John 4:20). Moreover, the selfless giving Paul commends here reflects the gracious character of God himself, who in redemptive generosity rescued us from spiritual poverty through the sacrificial gift of His Son (Romans 5:6–8; 6:23).

Because the Jerusalem offering is so important to Paul, he takes steps to ensure its success. His strategy is threefold: (1) He prepares the Corinthians to receive the ones responsible for collecting the funds by praising their virtues and qualifications (2 Corinthians 8:16–24). (2) He directs further praise at the Corinthians themselves for their zeal and willingness to participate, urging them to fulfill his boasts about them and his confidence in them (9:1–5). (3) He encourages their generosity and participation by teaching them principles of giving (9:6–15).

Apply principles from Paul’s methods of financial management.

Financial Management

Paul knew he had enemies and critics in Corinth. He also knew they would not hesitate to discredit his work by accusing him of using the offering for personal gain. Already, he has emphatically denied that he is like many who “peddle” the Word of God. Therefore, in 2 Corinthians 8, we read of the precautions Paul takes to ensure that such false charges are extremely difficult for anyone to make.

First, Paul wisely leaves the task of completing the offering at Corinth to trusted individuals. These men are not only known and well respected by the Corinthians but are also loyal to Paul. In 8:16–17, Paul names Titus as one of the trusted collectors. Titus had just arrived in Macedonia with the good news about the Corinthians’ positive response to Paul’s disciplinary letter (7:5). Titus was not only well received but had developed a special affection for the Corinthians (7:13–15; 8:16). Verses 18–24 then introduce two unnamed brothers who will accompany Titus. They are so well known that no identification is necessary.

The first brother is “praised by all the churches for his service to the gospel,” and the churches have appointed him to travel with Paul in collecting the offering (8:18–19). The other delegate, introduced as “our brother” (v. 22), also comes with sterling credentials and a proven ministry. Paul relates that this believer has been tested in many ways (literally, “things” or situations) and found to be zealous (or “diligent,” Greek spoudaios ). He also has “great confidence” in the Corinthians. Here is someone whom both Paul and the Corinthians know and respect, someone who assuredly believes in them.

Due to these credentials, Paul could not have chosen better delegates to Corinth. In Titus, he has a trusted companion and coworker (v. 23) who had worked among the Corinthians, earned their respect, and won their heart. In the two unnamed brothers, he has men whom the churches personally appointed and whose labor in the gospel was known to the Corinthians and had brought glory to Christ. Thus, his antagonists would be hard-pressed to mount a credible attack against Paul’s integrity since (1) Paul is not personally collecting the funds and (2) his associates, who can personally vouch for him, are so highly regarded within the church.

Second, not wanting to risk major misunderstanding with so much at stake, Paul clearly outlines his plans, desires, and expectations. He even tells why he is doing what he is doing: so no one will be able to discredit the handling of the collection (v. 19).

In closing 2 Corinthians 8, Paul exhorts the Corinthian believers to live up to his confidence and proud expectations of them by completing the offering. Doing so would also demonstrate their love before the churches. Whether Paul intended the phrase “so that the churches can see it” as subtle pressure to respond is difficult to determine. He may have simply wanted to underscore the unity of all the churches in this collection. In any event, the broken fences of fellowship had been mended; the Corinthians’ repentance had restored their fellowship with Paul. It was now time for the Corinthians to demonstrate their love by obeying this call to action.

Praise for the Past

Paul’s exhortation to generosity continues in 2 Corinthians 9. Here we learn something about the content of his boast concerning them. We can also see more of “Paul the motivator” as he continues to encourage them to complete the benevolence offering.

Paul begins with an emphatic statement (very nearly a boast) of his confidence in them: he does not need to write to them about this ministry to the Jerusalem saints because he already knows how eager and ready they are to respond to the need. In fact, he had told the Macedonians that those of Achaia (which includes the Corinthians) were ready and prepared to give a year before.

### Principles of Giving (9:6–15)

#### 9.2.1 — Explain why 2 Corinthians 9:6 does not guarantee a giver’s prosperity.

The Joyful Giver

Paul’s final step in motivating the Corinthians is to teach them various scriptural principles related to giving. The first could be called the “principle of sowing and reaping,” for which he employs the imagery of a harvest (2 Corinthians 9:6). 3 The lesson is very simple: the more the farmer sows, the greater the harvest. Keep in mind that Paul is not teaching a mechanical formula of giving but declaring truths about God. God gives lavishly to humanity and wants to see the same gracious generosity in His children.

Explain why 2 Corinthians 9:6 does not guarantee a giver’s prosperity.

Along with generosity, God loves “a cheerful giver” who gives freely, without a grudging sense of duty or compulsion (2 Corinthians 9:7; Romans 12:8). One incentive for giving in this manner is that “God is able to make all grace abound to you” (2 Corinthians 9:8). In context, Paul uses the word grace to refer to God’s bountiful provisions, both spiritual and material. God’s abounding grace results in believers’ having not only what they need but also a surplus to participate fully in every good work, such as the collection at hand.

List principles of benevolence that Paul taught the Corinthians.

As with the Macedonians, the Corinthians’ participation in the collection will be an expression of God’s grace. Like God, who provided for the poor of the land in ancient Israel (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 23:24–25), the Corinthians can demonstrate His mercy and compassion for the poor. Such care for the poor characterized the ministry of Jesus as well, who looked at crowds of hungry people following Him and was moved with compassion (Matthew 9:36). In fact, Jesus pronounced judgment on those who failed to show compassion toward those in physical need (Matthew 25:42–46).

Moreover, participation in acts of benevolence can have a spiritual benefit. To illustrate this, Paul quotes a portion of Psalm 112:9: “He has scattered abroad his gifts to the poor; his righteousness endures forever.” Here, righteousness (Greek dikaiosunē ) does not have the legal meaning of being in right standing before God (as in Romans 10:3 or Philippians 3:9); instead, it refers to a sense of moral rectitude or excellence (Hodge 1973, 222). Judaism had long equated giving alms to the poor with a degree of righteousness (Martin 1986, 291). Yet Paul is not contradicting his own doctrine of justification by faith, apart from the works of the Law. Rather, his point is the same as the psalmist’s: showing mercy to the poor has a lasting, even permanent, effect on a person’s moral character.

Paul then gives one final incentive. The generosity of the Corinthians’ gift will cause many to turn grateful hearts to God in thanksgiving and praise (9:11–13). The recipients will see in this gift proof that the Corinthians not only profess the gospel but also live in obedience to its teachings. Paul could not have given us a stronger incentive for preaching and living the good news. When God’s people mirror His mercy through acts of compassion and love, it creates a powerful and compelling message not easily resisted.

## 10 - Paul Confronts His Critics (2 Corinthians 10:1–11:15)

### True Apostolic Authority (10:1–11)

#### 10.1.1 — Assess Paul’s opponents’ criticism and misunderstanding of apostolic authority.

Assess Paul’s opponents’ criticism and misunderstanding of apostolic authority.

In 2 Corinthians 10, we encounter a dramatic change in tone. 1 If this letter was written over an extended period of time, it is possible that Paul received disturbing news of recent trouble caused by his adversaries. These chapters were then penned in response to that situation. In exhorting and warning the entire church, Paul specifically identifies the views of his opponents (for example, 11:4, 12, 13, 15, 20–23; 13:2). This lends weight to the view that Paul wrote these chapters primarily with the false teachers (11:13–15) or apostolic pretenders and their supporters in mind (Hodge 1973, 228).

What Is an Apostle?

The primary controversial issue seems to center on Paul’s claim of apostleship. What was the function of an apostle in the Early Church? Did Paul fit this category?

The word apostle (Greek apostolos ) means “delegate, messenger, or one who is sent.” Specifically, it refers to an ambassador of the gospel or a commissioner of Christ. Most Bible scholars acknowledge that the New Testament describes more than one category of apostle. In particular, they distinguish between (1) dominical apostles, who received their apostolic call directly from the Lord Jesus, and (2) a broader group of ecclesiastical apostles, who are sent out by the church.

William W. Menzies (2005) refers to the latter group as general apostles who were or are commissioned by the local church for special assignments. In this category, he includes Silas and Timothy (1 Thessalonians 1:1), Epaphroditus (Philippians 2:25), and Titus. Menzies distinguishes these from the unique apostles, who include the Twelve, Paul, and a broader circle of so-called associates, such as Mark, Luke, James, Jude, and perhaps the writer of Hebrews (Hebrews 2:3–4). One refinement of Menzies’ categories might be to see Timothy and Titus not as general apostles but as apostolic delegates of Paul, assigned to a specific region (Timothy to Asia Minor and Titus to Crete) to carry out apostolic leadership in Paul’s stead (Bittlinger 1973, 51; see also Taylor 1959, 626).

If the New Testament does not place Paul in the category of dominical apostles, it nevertheless accords him an equal status with the Twelve. This elite group of apostles is unique and irreplaceable in that they served a function that has been fulfilled within redemptive history. These first apostles were foundational to the church (Ephesians 2:20; 3:5) and acted as the authoritative bearers and transmitters of Jesus’ teaching (Matthew 10:1–7; Acts 1:2–8).

Their teachings and writings became the standard and the criteria by which the Early Church would determine the content of the New Testament canon. In this respect, their authority to determine the doctrinal norms of Christianity was unrivaled and beyond challenge. Paul definitely was a key person in this group of apostles. Paul has previously asserted his status as one of the apostles (though not the Twelve) in 1 Corinthians 15:7–10.

Identify truths Paul teaches about apostolic authority.

The Character of Christ

To expose his opponents and their errors, Paul contrasts them with himself and his ministry. From 2 Corinthians 11, we learn that they were jealous of Paul’s status as an apostle and coveted it for themselves. Thus, Paul describes the true character of apostolic authority . He understands that as an apostle he has a responsibility to imitate Christ. Discipleship for Paul involves living to model the character of Christ. In this way, Paul can use his own life as instruction: “Follow my example, as I follow the example of Christ” (1 Corinthians 11:1).

His opponents specifically criticized Paul’s lack of boldness and mistook it for weakness and timidity. In response, Paul points out that apostolic authority must be tempered by “the meekness and gentleness of Christ” (2 Corinthians 10:1). Some people become intoxicated with power or authority. They relish the chance to use it because it makes them feel important. Like his Lord, however, Paul understands that a person may possess authority without using it in a despotic or dictatorial fashion. Jesus taught, “You know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their high officials exercise authority over them. Not so with you. Instead, whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant” (Matthew 20:25–26). Paul does not delight in using his apostolic authority to administer discipline; in fact, he recoils from the prospect (2 Corinthians 10:2). Moreover, he knows that his authority has been given primarily for the church’s edification, for building up, not tearing down (13:10).

By contrast, Paul’s opponents put such value on corrective discipline and demonstrations of authority that they are conforming to the ways of the world (10:2). Such fleshly ways are unsuitable weapons for the spiritual warfare that Paul conducts in the work of the gospel. Furthermore, they forget that Christ gave His apostles authority, not to terrify and destroy the saints, but to build them up (vv. 8–9). Nevertheless, Paul is not timid or afraid to confront and correct error when needed (v. 6). Paul is ready to punish disobedience and oppose every idea that challenges or corrupts the true knowledge of God (vv. 5–6).

**Q:** Are there modern-day apostles?

**A:** Possibly. Paul and the Twelve constitute a unique class of apostles, yet the New Testament does not teach that the gift of an apostle (1 Corinthians 12:28; Ephesians 4:11) or its function was restricted to the first-century church.

**Q:** How can a person avoid the potentially corrupting pride of power?

**A:** Answers will vary. One key is to follow Christ’s example of humility, even in the face of ultimate authority (Philippians 2:1–11). That is, leaders must not think of themselves “more highly than [they] ought, but rather think of [themselves] with sober judgment” (Romans 12:3). They must also realize that all power comes from God and is His to add or remove.

### To Boast or Not to Boast (10:12–18)

#### 10.2.1 — Analyze 2 Corinthians 10:12–18, and describe boasting that is acceptable for believers.

Analyze 2 Corinthians 10:12–18, and describe boasting that is acceptable for believers.

Paul’s excessive use of forms of the word boast (Greek kauchomai ) in 2 Corinthians 10–12 (twenty-six times) seems to highlight his opponents’ pride and misdirected boasts (Hernando 1999, 921, 952). In fact, before contrasting their worldly or fleshly boasting with a boast of his own, Paul reveals what foolish pride looks like in these opponents. What he describes is almost humorous. With great sarcasm, he states that he cannot match their audacity. Some are so full of self that they foolishly make themselves the standard by which to measure others then brag that they meet the standard. In the Greek literary tradition, comparison and self-commendation were acceptable if done with restraint. Here, Paul shows how foolish their practice was even when measured by worldly custom (Keener 1993, 509).

At first glance, it appears that Paul’s boasting (2 Corinthians 10:13–14) compromises his criticism of boasting altogether. However, he has a different kind of boasting in mind, one that does not involve sinful pride but focuses on the “divine factor,” that is, what God is doing through him. Clearly, Paul sees his success as an apostle as resulting from God’s grace and empowerment of his apostolic call. Consequently, Paul exhorts, “Let him who boasts boast in the Lord” (v. 17).

Paul is secure and content to labor in the harvest field God has apportioned to him, namely, the Gentiles. Corinth belonged to this field of service, and Paul went there in obedience to God’s call. Unlike his opponents, Paul determines to confine his boasting “to the field God has assigned to us” (v. 13). He will not come into a church founded by another and attempt to take credit for the labor and fruit of someone else’s ministry (vv. 15–16). Besides, “it is not the one who commends himself who is approved, but the one whom the Lord commends” (v. 18).

**Q:** Is all boasting wrong?

**A:** Yes, if it is rooted in excessive pride and in elevating one’s own accomplishments, talents, and so forth apart from God. However, Christians can rightfully boast of what God has done through them if their motivation is to exalt Him.

### False Apostles Unmasked (11:1–15)

#### 10.3.1 — Describe Paul’s passion and goal for the Corinthians.

Describe Paul’s passion and goal for the Corinthians.

An Entirely Different Gospel

Paul realizes that in answering his critics, he must do more than defend himself. If the work of God is to be preserved, he must protect the Corinthians from those who would endanger their faith. Paul carries in his heart a godly jealousy (11:2) that seeks to protect them and safeguard their spiritual purity. He borrows from the Old Testament the image of a jealous husband, where Yahweh is said to be “jealous” over His people (Exodus 20:5; 34:14; Deuteronomy 4:24), whom He has betrothed to himself (Hosea 2:19–20). But in 2 Corinthians, Paul identifies the betrothed as Christ and himself as the father of the bride. His present desire is to preserve and protect the church’s purity and devotion to Christ to whom they are now betrothed (11:2).

Thus, to safeguard the bride of Christ, Paul must expose these critics, their false teaching, and the deceptive character of their ministry. Verse 4 provides a key to understanding the opponents of Paul addressed in chapters 10–13 (Martin 1986, 334). They are led by an apparent ringleader (Greek ho erchomenos , “the one who comes”) who comes and preaches “another Jesus” (Greek allon Iēsous ), different from the One preached by Paul. It is impossible to know for certain what these opponents preached about Jesus. However, given Paul’s repeated boasts of weakness and willingness to identify with Christ’s sufferings (2 Corinthians 11:23–33; 12:7–10; 13:1–6), the false teachers may have failed to give proper credit to the instrumentality of the crucifixion in securing God’s salvation. Even more likely is that they failed to see in Jesus’ death on the cross a model of God’s power perfected in weakness (13:4).

Whatever the content, such preaching of another Christ is equivalent to offering the Corinthians a “different spirit” 2 and a “different gospel” than the one they have received (11:4). That is, the false teachers misrepresent both Jesus and the work of the Spirit who brings the believer into the reality of salvation (Fee 1994, 344). Remember that for Paul there is only one Jesus, one Spirit, and one gospel. He is concerned that the Corinthians are being offered a counterfeit version of Christianity, and to his dismay, they are prepared to accept it.

Describe how Paul’s opponents would evaluate Paul.

Judging Paul

Most revealing is the worldly criteria Paul’s opponents use to criticize him. To them, Paul does not measure up to the standards of a true apostle (2 Corinthians 11:5). Earlier, they faulted him for lacking authority and boldness. Now they point out that Paul lacks rhetorical eloquence (for which both Greek and Roman statesmen and philosophers were noted). He responds that even if he is unskilled in speech, he is not lacking in knowledge. This would be obvious to the Corinthians who had spent much time under Paul’s teaching. Paul’s words carry a stinging rebuke for those who, when weighing the worth of a preacher, prefer style over substance and charisma over content.

The false teachers further criticize Paul for not accepting money for his preaching of the gospel. They link this anomaly to their accusation that Paul does not act like a true apostle. 3 To them, his refusal to take wages for his preaching seems strange given his earlier teaching that apostles had a right to earn their living from the gospel (1 Corinthians 9:6–14). Although he also had that right, he had already made it clear that he was determined not to “hinder the gospel of Christ” by doing so (9:12). Such hindrance would certainly have occurred had Paul given his opponents any grounds for accusing him of peddling “the word of God for profit” (2 Corinthians 2:17). Paul’s gospel is not for hire, and he wants no accusation that he has pinned a price tag to his ministry. Whereas his opponents may want to boast in their status as supported apostles, Paul will boast in choosing to offer the gospel free of charge (11:7–12).

Summarize Paul’s assessment of his opponents’ true identity and the character of their ministry.

Agents of Deception

Suddenly, at the mention of these would-be apostles in verse 12, Paul turns to unmasking them and revealing their true identity. They are “false apostles” and workers of deceit who masquerade as apostles of Christ. Even more condemning, they are agents of Satan, the master of deceit (John 8:44) who conceals the true nature of his work (1 Peter 5:8) by disguising himself as an angel of light. Is it any wonder, then, that his servants follow his example and try to pass themselves off as “servants of righteousness”?

The last sentence of verse 15 leaves no doubt that Paul views their work as worthy of God’s judgment. The phrase “what their actions deserve” literally reads, “according to their works” (Greek kata ta erga auton ). This is nearly identical to Paul’s words in 2 Timothy 4:14 when he condemns the opposition of Alexander the metalworker who did Paul “a great deal of harm” and whom God will repay “for what he has done” (Greek kata ta erga autou ).

1. The noticeable change in tone need not be viewed, as some claim, as evidence of a fragment of a separate letter. Dramatic changes in tone occur in other Pauline writings, including Romans 11:33; 1 Corinthians 16:1; Galatians 3:1; and Philippians 3:2. A shift in subject matter or to a different target audience are plausible explanations.

2. The NIV lowercases spirit, perhaps suggesting that the Corinthians have received a spirit apart from the spirit of Christ (not the Holy Spirit) (Martin 1986, 336). Although this would support our contention that Paul’s opponents failed to take on Christ’s weakness, self-sacrifice, and humility, it misses the big picture of what Paul is trying to portray—that his opponents are preaching an alien gospel.

In fact, the Sophists regarded a person’s refusal to take money for his or her teaching a sure sign that the teaching was worthless. On the other hand, Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle all taught that wisdom was not for sale (Martin 1986, 334).

**A:** Gospel never refers to the four literary gospels of the New Testament. Instead it refers to the message of God’s saving activity accomplished through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

Whereas his opponents may want to boast in their status as supported apostles, Paul will boast in choosing to offer the gospel free of charge (11:7–12).

Summarize Paul’s assessment of his opponents’ true identity and the character of their ministry.

Agents of Deception

Suddenly, at the mention of these would-be apostles in verse 12, Paul turns to unmasking them and revealing their true identity. They are “false apostles” and workers of deceit who masquerade as apostles of Christ. Even more condemning, they are agents of Satan, the master of deceit (John 8:44) who conceals the true nature of his work (1 Peter 5:8) by disguising himself as an angel of light. Is it any wonder, then, that his servants follow his example and try to pass themselves off as “servants of righteousness”?

The last sentence of verse 15 leaves no doubt that Paul views their work as worthy of God’s judgment. The phrase “what their actions deserve” literally reads, “according to their works” (Greek kata ta erga auton ). This is nearly identical to Paul’s words in 2 Timothy 4:14 when he condemns the opposition of Alexander the metalworker who did Paul “a great deal of harm” and whom God will repay “for what he has done” (Greek kata ta erga autou ).

1. The noticeable change in tone need not be viewed, as some claim, as evidence of a fragment of a separate letter. Dramatic changes in tone occur in other Pauline writings, including Romans 11:33; 1 Corinthians 16:1; Galatians 3:1; and Philippians 3:2. A shift in subject matter or to a different target audience are plausible explanations.

2. The NIV lowercases spirit, perhaps suggesting that the Corinthians have received a spirit apart from the spirit of Christ (not the Holy Spirit) (Martin 1986, 336). ## 11 - Strange Boastings (2 Corinthians 11:16–13:14)

### Paul’s Boasts (11–12)

#### 11.1.1 — Describe ways in which Paul’s boasts differ from those of his opponents.

Describe ways in which Paul’s boasts differ from those of his opponents.

Paul continues to draw a contrast between himself and his opponents by focusing on their pride and love of boasting. They are quick to point out their spiritual heritage or pedigree as Jews (consider Philippians 3:3–5). This foolish pride leads to a heavy-handed abuse of authority, which the Corinthians seem to gladly endure (2 Corinthians 11:19–21). With continuing sarcasm, Paul tells the Corinthians that he will come down to their level and do some boasting of his own. But it soon becomes clear that unlike the “false apostles,” Paul does not boast “the way the world does” (v. 18). If his audience expects glowing tales of church plantings and missionary exploits, they will be shocked and disappointed.

Instead, Paul’s boastings as a servant of Christ are nothing more than the countless trials and afflictions he endured in his service (11:23–33). Herein lie the uniqueness and strategic genius of Paul’s boast. His rivals played to the Corinthians’ love and fascination with externals. To them, true apostles were known for their rhetorical eloquence, authoritative manner, and charismatic presence. Miracles, signs, and wonders (12:12) were consistent with these criteria, and Paul’s opponents saw them as the authenticating marks of apostolic ministry.

Paul’s incomparable boast was that he was unrivaled in his imitation of Christ’s sufferings in obedience to God’s will. This demanded nothing less than dying to self (Romans 6:6; Galatians 2:20; Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:3–9). Yet his opponents’ prideful self-promotion stood in stark contrast to the cruciform life—the path Jesus took on the way to the cross.

By boasting of his sufferings for Christ’s sake, Paul has accommodated his opponents’ love of comparison and citing of apostolic credentials. However, he has also put forward a criterion they cannot begin to meet: a life of self-denial and suffering in obedience to his apostolic calling.

Explain the importance of visions and revelations to Paul’s opponents and why Paul boasts of those the Lord gave him.

A Boast of Great Revelations

Although he doubts anything can be gained from it, Paul feels compelled to continue boasting. His opponents likely bragged that they preached a truth given to them through visions and revelations. Thus, Paul now boasts of visions and revelations 1 given him by the Lord. Although unstated, Paul most certainly has in mind the activity of the Spirit. In 1 Corinthians 14, for example, he uses the word revelation (Greek apokalupsis ) to designate the gift of the Spirit through which God communicates with His people (vv. 6, 26, 30). In other instances, the word refers to the personal revelation of Jesus Christ that accompanied Paul’s apostolic call (Galatians 1:12; Ephesians 3:3–4).

But however important Paul considers such revelations personally, here he does not regard them as proof of his apostleship. For Paul, visions and revelations are a private matter of personal spirituality. He foolishly boasts of such visions and revelations only to expose what his opponents wrongly regard as an identifying mark of an apostle. That is, since he is forced to defend the legitimacy of his apostleship, Paul will acknowledge his revelations (2 Corinthians 12:1); yet he does not want them to think these revelations make him special or give him the right to boast of some sort of elite status.

As a result, he reluctantly boasts of a man he knows (v. 2) rather than referring to his own experiences directly, although it seems almost certain that he is indeed describing his own experience here. 2 In fact, the only circumstances Paul feels comfortable boasting about are those that reveal his weaknesses (v. 5), and one such occasion comes to his mind. Once, to prevent the magnitude of certain revelations from leading to self-exaltation, he was given a “thorn in my flesh,” which he also calls “a messenger of Satan,” to torment him (v. 7). This reference to Satan might cause some to object to an apparent unholy alliance between Satan and God.

However, while Paul at times presents Satan as an instrument of discipline in God’s hands (1 Corinthians 5:5; 1 Timothy 1:20), we must keep in mind that God limits the devil’s workings to achieve His sovereign purposes (see Job 2:1–10).

Thus, the revelations Paul received did not exalt him but humbled him. Such discipline from the Lord is redemptive and keeps Paul aware of his dependence on God’s grace and power (v. 9). Now Paul not only endures the thorn but also boasts in the weakness that requires Christ’s indwelling power. Through this, Paul can imitate Christ and display the divine wisdom of the cross: God’s power displayed through human weakness.

Identify the things that mark Paul as a true apostle of Christ.

The Marks of a True Apostle

Finishing his discussion of revelations, Paul is now ready to summarize the marks of a true apostle (12:11–13). Some discrepancy surrounds the interpretation of verse 12. Consider these two slightly different translations:

The signs of a true apostle were performed among you with all perseverance, by signs and wonders and miracles. (NASB)

The things that mark an apostle—signs, wonders and miracles—were done among you with great perseverance. (NIV)

Especially among Pentecostals and Charismatics, 3 many assume that the signs or things that mark a true apostle are actually the miraculous signs, wonders, and miracles mentioned, as the NIV seems to indicate. However, although the NASB (a significantly more literal translation of the original Greek text) uses the word signs in both the beginning and end of the verse, it seems likely that Paul meant for us to draw a distinction between the two. For one thing, the term signs has a wider range of meaning than just “miracles” (for example, Matthew 26:48, Judas’ kiss; Romans 4:11, circumcision; 2 Thessalonians 3:17, Paul’s signature). Also, as seen in the NASB, the grammar of this verse indicates that the terms at the end of the sentence describe the manner in which the signs of a true apostle are performed. In fact, Paul has been recounting the distinguishing marks or signs of a true apostle in 2 Corinthians 10–13. In addition to spiritual power to confront evil (10:3–4, 8–11; 13:2–4, 10) and divine (visionary) revelations, these include such character traits as (1) jealous care for the churches (11:6); (2) true knowledge of Jesus and His gospel (11:6); (3) sacrificial self-support so as not to burden the churches (11:7–9); (4) absence of self-serving or heavy-handed discipline (11:20–21); (5) willingness to suffer and be afflicted for the cause of Christ (11:23–29); and (6) patient endurance of the thorn in the flesh (12:7–9) (Grudem 1993, 63–66). These, then, are the “signs of a true apostle” (2 Corinthians 12:12).

Thus, the first use of the word signs in 12:12 (NASB) refers generally to the things that mark a true apostle, and the phrase “with all perseverance” indicates the inward character to patiently endure suffering as a true apostle of Christ. The second reference to signs then points to the presence of miracles as confirmations of God’s power and divine activity.

Note that the false apostles came disguised by Satan as “servants of righteousness” (11:13–15), a description perhaps based on their showy displays of righteousness and legalistic demands. Possibly, they also came with counterfeit signs empowered by Satan. Nevertheless, when Paul wants to defend his claim to genuine apostleship, he points first, not to miracles, but to the Christlikeness reflected in his blameless conduct, sacrificial service, evident character (12:13–18), and willingness to suffer for the sake of the gospel. Again, for Paul, these qualities are the marks of a true apostle. They stand in bold relief against the superficial, carnal criteria of the self-promoting impostors at Corinth.

Moreover, Paul’s apostleship is marked by a fatherly and pastoral concern for the Corinthians as his spiritual children. Like a parent, Paul is willing to sacrifice for their welfare and benefit. His love is evident, but he longs for it to be reciprocated (v. 15).

Then appears another subtle reference to Paul’s practice of not taking a living from his gospel work (vv. 14–18). Perhaps some have twisted Paul’s motive, claiming it as evidence of Paul’s lack of love (v. 15) or of some other scheming purpose. They have apparently accused him of “trickery” (v. 16), saying that he deceitfully refused to take money directly but obtained it indirectly through his collection for the believers in Jerusalem. Yet such a strategy is doubtless ineffective in a church where the integrity of Paul and his associates is well known and long witnessed (vv. 17–18).

### Paul’s Final Warnings (13:1–14)

#### 11.2.1 — Explain how Paul’s passion results in a warning and the threat of discipline.

Explain how Paul’s passion results in a warning and the threat of discipline.

Time for Discipline

Having stated his personal fears about his upcoming third visit to Corinth (12:20), Paul takes up the authority of an apostle and issues a twofold warning. First, the Corinthians can be assured that the truth will be uncovered and revealed. To support his point, Paul quotes a portion of Deuteronomy 19:15: “A matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses.” The witnesses probably refer to Paul’s previous visits (13:2) when he had given them similar warnings.

Now, he repeats the warnings “while absent”: the Corinthians have been amply forewarned; it is now time for the false apostles and their supporters to be called into account. These impostors mock Paul’s meekness (10:1) and identify their outward display of authority as proof of their apostleship, proof that Christ speaks through them. Their fascination with such outward authority betrays their ignorance of what it takes to be an apostolic servant of Christ. They also fail to understand that Christ did not give His apostles His authority to tear the church down through harsh discipline but to build it up through teaching and loving correction.

In no uncertain terms, Paul lets them know that if they are looking for evidence of apostleship, they will find it when he comes. Thus, in the second part of his overall warning, he assures the Corinthians that his impending visit will involve discipline. He will not spare anyone. After all, the Christ that speaks through Paul is not weak but a mighty power among them (13:3).

Amid this severe warning, Paul again reveals his pastoral heart. He does not want to harshly discipline the Corinthians, so he strongly urges them to “test yourselves” (v. 5). Such self-examination or spiritual inventory can help them recognize whether they are living according to their faith in Christ. If not, they can make needed corrections and avoid discipline. Paul’s concern and hope is that they “do what is right” (v. 7) and not “fail the test” (v. 5). He wants them to be made complete, or spiritually mature in the faith, whether or not they approve or commend him personally (vv. 6–8). His utmost desire is that the full blessing of redemption be their continual possession—“the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit” (v. 14).

Notes for Chapter 11

1. Paul probably does not intend to distinguish between the terms visions and revelations . For instance, his vision of Christ as recorded in Acts 9:3–9 is called a revelation in Galatians 1:12.

2. In a section where he stoops to his opponents’ level of boasting to defend the legitimacy of his apostleship, why would Paul mention the visionary experience of another man? Furthermore, the boast Paul makes on behalf of this other man appears to directly result in his “thorn in my flesh,” given “because of these surpassingly great revelations” (2 Corinthians 12:7). Why would the revelations of someone else result in Paul exalting himself and requiring a thorn of discipline? As for timing, Paul says this revelation occurred fourteen years prior (v. 2) to the writing of 2 Corinthians (ca. AD 56). This places it around AD 42–43, which falls within the long period Paul spent in Syria and Cilicia (Galatians 1:21; 2:1), about which Acts is all but silent (see Acts 9:30; 11:25).

3. For definitions of Pentecostals and Charismatics , as well as a fuller discussion of the Charismatic Movement, see Hernando 2005, 154, 170.
